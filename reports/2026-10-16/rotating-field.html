Why Antibiotics Stop Working: The Evolution of Resistance

How do bacteria become resistant, what do recent global data show, and which actions can reduce the harm?

Independent research study · Sources and evidence inside · Examples marked illustrative

Executive summary

Antibiotic resistance occurs when bacteria survive medicines that once controlled them. It follows a basic process of evolution: bacteria vary, some inherit or acquire traits that protect them, and exposure to an antibiotic can favor the survivors. Resistant organisms can then multiply and spread between people, hospitals, animals, and the environment. In 2025 the World Health Organization reported that about one in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections of common types in 2023 were resistant to antibiotic treatments in its global analysis. A separate global modelling study estimated that bacterial resistance directly caused about 1.14 million deaths in 2021 and was associated with about 4.71 million. These numbers measure different outcomes and cannot be added. This report explains the cell biology, selection process, gene transfer, surveillance data, treatment trade-offs, and public-health response. It distinguishes population evidence from individual medical advice. The central lesson is that resistance is an evolving system involving medicines, infection control, access to care, and the way humans share microbes.

1. Background: antibiotics changed medicine, but microbes evolve

Antibiotics treat certain infections caused by bacteria. They may kill bacteria or stop them from growing. They do not treat a viral illness such as an ordinary cold. Different antibiotics act on different parts of bacterial biology, including cell-wall formation, protein production, or the copying of genetic material. A treatment works only if the right drug reaches the organism at a useful concentration and the patient receives appropriate care.

The development and use of antibiotics transformed the treatment of infections and made many operations, cancer therapies, and intensive-care procedures safer. Yet resistant bacteria were observed early in the antibiotic era. Resistance is not necessarily a sign that a particular patient did something wrong. Bacteria reproduce rapidly, populations can be very large, and genetic variation already exists before many exposures. The widespread use of medicines creates conditions that can favor organisms able to survive. [1]

A pathogen is a microbe that can cause disease. An antimicrobial acts against a broad group of microbes, including bacteria, fungi, and some parasites. Antibiotic resistance is the narrower problem of bacteria resisting antibacterial medicines. WHO's 2024 priority-pathogen list covers 24 bacterial pathogens across 15 families, categorized by urgency to guide research and public health. [3]

2. Step 1: bacteria vary before treatment

Not every bacterium in a population is genetically identical. Random mutations can change cellular structures or the amount of a protective substance that a cell produces. A bacterium may also carry resistance genes acquired from another microbe. Most genetic changes are not useful in every environment. Some mutations cost the cell energy or reduce its growth. Under antibiotic exposure, however, a trait that lets a bacterium survive can become an advantage.

This matters because resistance is often mistakenly described as a conscious response: as if a bacterium decides to adapt because it is under attack. Evolution does not require a plan. Variation exists or arises, and conditions change which organisms reproduce successfully. The antibiotic supplies selection pressure by making life harder for susceptible cells while some resistant ones survive.

3. Step 2: antibiotics change which bacteria survive

Suppose a population contains many susceptible bacteria and a few resistant ones. A suitable antibiotic kills or suppresses much of the susceptible population. Resistant organisms may remain. If they reproduce, their share of the population rises. This process can make the next infection harder to treat, especially if those bacteria are transmitted to other people.

The diagram is simplified. In real infections, dose, tissue concentration, bacterial growth, immune response, treatment duration, and additional mutations all matter. Some bacteria also tolerate a drug temporarily without having stable, inherited resistance. A clinician therefore needs lab results and clinical context when treatment is difficult. The fact that resistance can evolve must never be used as a reason to refuse needed antibiotics.

4. Step 3: resistance genes can move between bacteria

Bacteria inherit genes when they divide, but they can also obtain genetic material from other bacteria. This is called horizontal gene transfer. One route involves a plasmid, a small DNA element that may move from one cell to another. A plasmid can carry genes that help a bacterium break down an antibiotic or prevent it from reaching its target. The genes may spread through a population even when their first carrier is no longer present.

Common resistance mechanisms include enzymes that disable a drug, changes to the molecular target it attacks, pumps that push a drug out of the cell, and changes to the outer barrier that reduce entry. More than one mechanism may be present. This is why a resistant strain can defeat one drug while remaining sensitive to another. Testing must identify the relevant organism-drug combination rather than labeling a person simply 'resistant'.

Resistant bacteria can move through close contact, contaminated surfaces, water, food systems, and healthcare environments. Infection control reduces transmission. Safe water and sanitation reduce the number of infections that need treatment in the first place. Vaccination against relevant infections can also reduce antibiotic demand, even when vaccines do not directly target resistance genes. [4]

5. How scientists measure resistance

A laboratory can collect a bacterial sample, identify the organism, and expose it to selected antibiotics. Susceptibility testing measures whether the organism's growth is inhibited under standard conditions. These tests are valuable, but a laboratory sample is not a random draw from the entire population. People with severe infections may be tested more often than those with mild illness; some countries have limited laboratory access. Therefore surveillance needs methods to account for uneven sampling.

WHO's GLASS system—Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System—coordinates countries' data. Its October 2025 report analyzed more than 23 million laboratory-confirmed cases from the available reporting networks. Data for 2023 came from 104 countries, while the broader time series drew from 110 countries between 2016 and 2023. It made adjusted estimates for 93 infection-pathogen-antibiotic combinations. These are valuable measurements, but the underlying sample is not every infection in the world. [1]

WHO estimated that one in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections causing common infections in 2023 were resistant to treatments. It also found resistance rising in more than 40% of monitored organism-drug combinations between 2018 and 2023, with average yearly increases of 5–15% among those rising combinations. It is important not to present the one-in-six figure as the chance that any person walking down the street has a resistant infection. The denominator is the studied laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections. [1]

6. Burden: attributed deaths and associated deaths

Researchers distinguish deaths attributable to resistance from deaths associated with resistance. Attributable deaths estimate how many deaths would be prevented under a defined comparison where resistant infections were susceptible to antibiotics. Associated deaths include deaths that occurred with a drug-resistant infection, even where the precise contribution of resistance is harder to separate. The attributed total is a subset within the broader associated burden under these modelling definitions.

A large global analysis led by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation estimated about 1.14 million deaths directly attributable to bacterial resistance in 2021, and 4.71 million associated deaths. The estimates cover many countries and rely on modelling as well as observed data. They carry uncertainty because reporting and laboratory access differ by setting. [2]

The same modelling programme projected about 39 million deaths attributable to resistance across 2025–2050 under its reference scenario. This is a forecast, not a count of deaths already observed. Forecasts depend on assumptions about infections, treatment access, population aging, resistance trends, and future interventions. Presenting a projection without these conditions can exaggerate what has been measured. [2]

7. Worked example: why a resistant minority can dominate

Consider a purely illustrative bacterial population with 10,000 cells. Suppose 9,900 are susceptible and 100 resist a particular antibiotic. Imagine treatment removes 99% of susceptible cells but only 10% of resistant cells. Afterwards, 99 susceptible cells and 90 resistant cells remain, a total of 189. The resistant share rises from 1% before exposure to 90 ÷ 189 = 47.6% after exposure. The resistant cells did not need to become resistant during treatment; differential survival was enough to raise their share.

Now assume the survivors have access to nutrients and can reproduce. Their descendants may include more resistant cells. Actual infections involve far more complexity, including immune effects and drug concentration, so the calculation is a teaching model only. Its purpose is to show the logic of selection: a treatment can reduce the total bacterial load while increasing the *fraction* of survivors with resistance. A high resistant fraction does not mean the overall infection is necessarily worse after treatment; both absolute numbers and clinical condition matter.

8. What drives the problem at a social level?

Unnecessary antibiotic use can select resistance without offering benefit, as when antibiotics are taken for a viral infection. But limited access to proper diagnosis and appropriate treatment is also harmful. A patient who cannot obtain a suitable drug may experience a prolonged infection and greater spread. Good policy must address both overuse and inadequate access.

WHO's 2025 antibiotic-use surveillance report covered data from 60 reporting countries and areas for 2022. It noted large differences in use, including both possible overuse and low access. WHO uses an AWaRe system that groups antibiotics into Access, Watch, and Reserve categories to support appropriate use. This is not a ranking that tells a patient which antibiotic to request; the right choice depends on the infection and clinical judgment. [5]

Hospitals face specific risks because they treat many vulnerable patients, use invasive procedures, and concentrate antibiotic exposure. Prevention measures such as hand hygiene, clean equipment, isolation when needed, and rapid detection can reduce spread. Outside healthcare, safer water, good sanitation, vaccination, and control of contamination in animal and food systems can reduce the overall number of infections. WHO calls this cross-sector view One Health. [4]

9. Competing views and practical trade-offs

Some policies focus on restricting antibiotic use. This can reduce unnecessary selection, but crude restrictions can leave patients without life-saving treatment. Other policies emphasize developing new drugs, diagnostics, and vaccines. Innovation is essential, but bacteria may eventually evolve resistance to new medicines as well. A third emphasis is prevention: fewer infections mean fewer courses of antibiotics and fewer opportunities for resistance to spread. These approaches are complementary, though budgets force difficult choices.

Drug development has an economic problem. Society wants new antibiotics to remain effective, which can mean reserving some medicines for serious cases. A company may then sell fewer courses than it would for a drug used routinely. This makes ordinary sales-based incentives poorly matched to public-health value. Researchers and policymakers debate public funding, advance purchasing agreements, and payment models that reward availability rather than large sales volumes. Strong oversight is needed to ensure access, safety, and fair use.

10. Why this matters and what is uncertain

Antibiotic resistance can make common infections harder to treat and increase the risk associated with surgery, childbirth, cancer therapy, and intensive care. It affects household health costs, hospital capacity, productivity, and trust in health systems. Because microbes cross borders, one country's treatment and prevention choices can affect others. Yet regional differences are large, and policies should rely on local resistance patterns rather than one worldwide average.

The strongest claims are clear: bacteria evolve; resistance can be selected and transmitted; laboratory networks document a substantial burden; and prevention, diagnostics, effective treatment, stewardship, and new tools are all important. The uncertain parts concern future burden, how much each policy will reduce resistance in particular settings, and how quickly new treatments can reach people who need them. A good public-health plan measures infections prevented, care improved, and resistance trends over time, not simply the number of antibiotics prescribed.

Key lessons

  1. Antibiotics treat bacterial infections; they do not treat ordinary viral colds.
  2. Genetic variation and horizontal gene transfer can create resistant bacteria.
  3. Antibiotic exposure can select resistant survivors; evolution has no goal or intention.
  4. Surveillance statistics apply to their defined samples and denominators.
  5. Attributable and associated deaths measure different burdens and must not be added.
  6. Forecasts for 2050 are modelled scenarios, not recorded deaths.
  7. Prevention and access to correct treatment matter alongside reducing misuse.
  8. Health, animal systems, and the environment can all affect transmission.

Knowledge check

Answer each question before you open the explanation.

How can resistance rise even if most bacteria die during treatment?

If susceptible cells die more often, resistant survivors become a larger fraction of the remaining population. Those survivors may then reproduce.

What is horizontal gene transfer?

It is the movement of genetic material between bacteria other than by ordinary parent-to-offspring cell division.

Why should the two death estimates for 2021 not be added?

Attributable deaths are estimated causal deaths under a counterfactual, while associated deaths are broader and include overlapping cases.

What is the denominator of WHO’s one-in-six finding?

Laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections in the infection types and reporting systems analyzed for 2023, not all people or all illnesses.

Why would a policy that simply bans antibiotics be harmful?

People with bacterial infections may need timely effective treatment. Stewardship means appropriate use, good diagnostics and access, together with prevention.
