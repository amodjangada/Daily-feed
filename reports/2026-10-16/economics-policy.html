Why Housing Becomes Unaffordable in Growing Cities

Why do homes get more expensive even when cities become richer, and which policies improve access?

Independent research study · Sources and evidence inside · Examples marked illustrative

Executive summary

Housing is both a place to live and an asset that people buy, rent, and finance. Its price depends on how much usable housing exists where people need it, how demand changes, and what buyers and renters can pay. In high-demand cities, new jobs can attract workers faster than builders add homes. Land limits, planning rules, slow permits, construction costs, and finance can restrict supply. Yet a simple call to 'build more' misses the needs of low-income households, the location of jobs, and the cost of services. The OECD's July 2026 housing review finds that affordability pressures reflect both a supply-demand mismatch and too little affordable or social rental provision. About two in five low-income tenants across the relevant OECD data spend more than 40% of disposable income on rent. This report explains housing supply, land value, credit, rents, and the policy choices available to governments. It uses a transparent numerical rental-market example and weighs deregulation, public housing, subsidies, and tenant protection. The central question is how to produce enough suitable homes in useful locations while protecting people who cannot wait for construction.

1. Background: housing is local, slow to build, and costly to move

A house differs from a phone or a bag of rice in three ways. It is fixed to a location, it lasts for decades, and a new unit often takes years to plan and build. A city may have empty homes on its edge while people compete for costly homes near transport and jobs. Total housing stock is therefore a poor measure of availability if it ignores where homes are and whether households can reach work from them.

Housing demand depends on population, household size, income, credit conditions, local jobs, and expectations about future prices. Housing supply depends on available land, permitted density, infrastructure, skilled labour, building inputs, finance, and developer risk. A housing market is a set of linked markets: buying property, renting it, financing it, and developing new units. Each moves differently, and the same policy can help one group while hurting another.

The OECD reported in July 2026 that real house prices have risen across nearly all its member countries over three decades. Its analysis identifies high construction costs, skilled-worker shortages, borrowing costs, land-use limits, and weak public investment among the pressures on affordability. It also reports that around two in five low-income renters spend over 40% of disposable income on rent. The indicator measures rent burden in a defined population; it does not mean 40% of all households are in that position. [1]

2. Step 1: demand can increase faster than the number of usable homes

When a city creates well-paid jobs, more people can bid for homes. Existing residents may also seek larger units. If the number of useful homes near those jobs changes little, landlords can raise rents when a lease ends, and buyers can bid up prices. This is a basic supply-demand mechanism. The scale depends on how much housing is available and how easily people can move elsewhere.

The response of construction to higher prices is called supply elasticity. Elastic supply means higher prices bring much more building; inelastic supply means construction changes little even as prices rise. The distinction helps explain why identical income growth can produce different housing inflation in two cities. A growing city with well-serviced land and predictable permissions may add homes. A city with restrictive density rules, a coast or hills on one side, and long approvals can experience steeper price pressure. [2]

Higher housing prices can themselves change a city. Some workers accept longer commutes. Others share homes, delay forming new households, or move to less attractive areas. Firms may struggle to recruit workers who cannot afford nearby rent. These outcomes are economic costs even if recorded city GDP continues to rise.

3. Step 2: the price of land can absorb gains from the city

A home price includes the value of the structure and the right to use a particular plot. The building can be expensive to construct, but a valuable location can cost far more than the physical materials. Better transport, schools, safety, and jobs increase the appeal of a neighborhood. Those gains may be capitalized into land prices: people pay more to be close to the improved services.

Density rules determine how many homes can share a given plot. If a site can hold only one small building, a large part of its value must be recovered from a few units. If more safe housing is permitted near existing transit and utilities, that value can be divided among more households. But permitting high density without adequate water, drainage, schools, and safe streets can harm residents. The practical issue is coordinated land use and services, not building height in isolation.

An increase in construction capacity also has a limit. Developers need buyers or tenants who can pay enough to cover costs. If loans become expensive or materials scarce, permitted land may remain undeveloped. OECD research stresses that housing supply responds slowly because both planning and actual construction take time. [2]

4. Step 3: credit can lift purchasing power without adding homes

Mortgage finance makes it possible to buy a home over many years. Lower interest rates often raise what borrowers can afford in monthly payments. If available homes do not increase, buyers may use the larger borrowing capacity to bid up prices. A subsidy for first-time buyers can have a similar effect in a tight market. The household may receive support, but part of the benefit can flow to sellers through higher prices.

Credit also creates risk. If prices rise rapidly and banks lend against optimistic valuations, a downturn can leave owners with debts larger than the value of their property. This is why affordability must be measured through purchase costs, rents, income, and financial vulnerability rather than through mortgage rates alone.

Price-to-income ratios compare a home's sale price with annual income. Rent burden compares rent with disposable income. Both are useful but incomplete. A lower-priced distant home may require a long and costly commute. A tenant paying a modest share of income may still be unable to afford adequate food, energy, or medical care if their income is very low.

5. Worked example: why construction takes time to relieve pressure

Consider an imaginary district with 100,000 rental homes occupied at the start of the year. Suppose growing employment produces demand for 105,000 homes at current rents. Only 1,000 new units finish that year because construction takes time. At the old rent, demand exceeds supply by roughly 4,000 homes. Prices and living patterns must adjust: some households share units, commute farther, wait, or pay more. The example does not predict exactly how much rents rise because that depends on households' willingness and ability to pay and the choices of landlords.

Now suppose faster permits and infrastructure allow 4,000 additional completed units the following year, while demand remains at 105,000. The gap at the old rent can become much smaller. But if demand grows by another 8,000 homes, the gap may persist. The lesson is that the relevant variable is the change in usable supply relative to demand over time, not simply the announced number of building permits.

A permit is not a completed, habitable home. Counting permits alone can overstate relief. A rigorous city dashboard would publish units permitted, started, completed, occupied, demolished, and vacant by location and price bracket. These are distinct measures of the housing process.

6. What the OECD evidence shows

The OECD's 2026 affordability brief reports that in around two-thirds of its member countries, social rental housing is less than 5% of total housing stock. Only the Netherlands, Austria, and Denmark exceed 20% on that measure. The report argues for a mix of putting underused housing into service and adding affordable rental supply, because new construction alone takes time. It also notes that some apparent vacant housing is in the wrong location, poor condition, or set aside for seasonal use. [1]

OECD evidence from 2025 and 2026 links weak supply response in high-demand areas to land-use limits and lengthy permits. This association does not imply that every planning rule is harmful. Restrictions may protect safety, historic buildings, floodplains, and public space. The policy question is which rules deliver enough benefit to justify their cost in lost housing opportunities. [2][3]

An important analytical distinction is between a city-wide average and individual affordability. More market-rate homes can reduce overall scarcity and may create vacancies in older stock, but very poor households can still struggle to pay. In a growing city, low-income residents may need direct assistance even if new construction moderates average rents. The OECD makes this distinction by supporting both housing supply and an affordable social rental sector. [1]

7. Competing policies and trade-offs

A supply-focused view favors faster permits, more density near transit, and infrastructure that opens useful land. Its strongest argument is that lasting affordability is difficult when the number of homes cannot respond to demand. Critics correctly point out that developers may concentrate on expensive units, and supply takes time while current renters face eviction or financial stress.

A public-housing approach directly adds homes for households that the market may underserve. It can offer durable protection, but requires land, capital, sound allocation, and long-term maintenance. Rent regulation can protect sitting tenants from sudden increases. Poorly designed rules can reduce mobility, discourage maintenance, or deter new rental construction. The size of these effects depends on the rule and local market, and cannot be inferred from the words 'rent control' alone.

A purchase subsidy or tax deduction may increase access for a selected buyer, especially where supply can grow. In a constrained market it can also be absorbed into higher land or home prices. Governments need to measure who receives the financial benefit after market responses, rather than assuming the named beneficiary receives it all.

8. Two policy examples and wider effects

The OECD describes the use of vacant and underused buildings as a near-term option for expanding affordable rentals. This works only where properties are safe, usable, and close enough to jobs or services. A count of empty units is not evidence that all can become affordable housing. [1]

In dense metropolitan areas, planning reform and transport must be coordinated. Allowing more homes beside a railway may increase access to jobs and reduce travel time. But if transit, drainage, or safety is already under strain, the city needs to invest in those systems alongside housing. Land-value increases created by public investments also raise questions about who should receive the gain and how the city funds the infrastructure.

Housing policy affects productivity and inequality. When workers cannot move toward better jobs, labour and skills may remain in less productive locations. High housing costs also transfer income from renters to owners. Owners can gain wealth as prices rise while first-time buyers and younger households face higher barriers. The same price movement therefore creates different political interests.

9. What remains uncertain

No single policy package works equally well in every city. Good evidence needs detailed local data on land, approvals, household demand, building completions, rents, services, and relocation costs. An international finding can identify a mechanism but cannot provide an exact rent forecast for a specific neighborhood. Cities also face climate, water, and flood constraints that make some construction genuinely unwise.

The strongest general conclusion is modest but important: making housing more accessible requires more usable homes in useful places, a construction process that can respond to change, and direct support for households whose incomes remain too low. These goals reinforce each other when planned together. Whether a particular intervention succeeds must be judged from completions, access, affordability, and residents' actual welfare rather than the number of policy announcements.

Key lessons

  1. Homes are tied to locations; counting units without considering jobs and travel is inadequate.
  2. Housing demand can respond quickly to jobs and credit while new construction takes years.
  3. Supply elasticity determines how much new building follows rising demand.
  4. Infrastructure and planning rules change the quantity of homes a valuable plot can hold.
  5. Buyer subsidies may increase prices where supply cannot expand.
  6. Social and affordable rental housing serves groups the market may not reach.
  7. Every tool involves costs and side effects; evaluate completions, affordability, and access.
  8. The effective test of policy is residents’ welfare, not permits or announcements.

Knowledge check

Answer each question before you open the explanation.

Why can a city with rising wages still have worsening affordability?

Demand for well-located homes may rise faster than usable housing supply. Higher wages and credit can raise bids when construction is slow.

What is supply elasticity in housing?

It is the extent to which building responds to changes in prices or demand. Low elasticity means higher prices bring relatively little new supply.

Why might a buyer subsidy raise sale prices?

In a tight market, buyers with greater financial capacity can bid more for the same limited stock, passing some benefit to sellers.

Why is a vacant-home total insufficient?

Some homes are in unsuitable places, unsafe, seasonal, or too costly to repair. Affordability depends on location, condition and actual availability.

Which measures show whether a reform is working?

Track units permitted, completed and occupied; rents and purchase costs relative to income; access to jobs; housing quality; and changes for low-income residents.
