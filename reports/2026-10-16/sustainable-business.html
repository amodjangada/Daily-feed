Green Hydrogen: Where It Makes Sense — and Where It Does Not

Can low-emissions hydrogen replace fossil fuels at scale, and when is it worth the extra energy and cost?

Independent research study · Sources and evidence inside · Examples marked illustrative

Executive summary

Hydrogen is often described as a clean fuel, but its climate impact depends on how it is produced and used. Most hydrogen today comes from fossil fuels, largely to serve refining and chemical production. The International Energy Agency's June 2026 review reports that global demand exceeded 100 million tonnes in 2025, while low-emissions hydrogen output reached only about one million tonnes. Green hydrogen uses electricity to split water. It may help reduce emissions from ammonia, iron production, and some long-distance fuels, where direct use of electricity can be difficult. But electrolysis, compression, transport, and conversion all consume energy. Using hydrogen for applications that can use electricity directly often creates more demand for scarce clean power. The business case also depends on firm buyers, financing, industrial standards, and infrastructure. This report follows the physics and costs step by step, separates announced projects from committed capacity, and weighs strong and weak uses. A worked electrolyser example shows why an apparently cheap electricity price is not the full price of hydrogen.

1. Background: hydrogen is already a large industry

Hydrogen is the lightest chemical element. In its common gas form, a molecule contains two hydrogen atoms, written H₂. Hydrogen can release energy when it reacts with oxygen, producing water, but it is rarely available as a free gas at useful scale. Most industrial hydrogen is manufactured from another material. Energy is used to separate hydrogen from compounds such as water or natural gas.

This distinction matters. Hydrogen is an energy carrier: a way to move or store energy made somewhere else. It is not a primary energy source like sunlight or a fossil fuel deposit. Calling it clean without specifying the production method can hide very different emissions profiles.

Today the principal uses are oil refining, ammonia for fertiliser, and chemical production including methanol. The IEA Global Hydrogen Review 2026 found that global hydrogen demand passed 100 million tonnes in 2025. Use remained concentrated in these established industries. Production with low emissions reached almost one million tonnes, around 1% of the scale of total demand. The figures refer to 2025 and should not be confused with forecasts for 2030. [1][2]

2. Step 1: how the production methods differ

Conventional steam methane reforming reacts natural gas with steam to form hydrogen and carbon-containing gases. It is widely used because existing gas infrastructure and chemical plants can provide steady output. But without emissions controls, the process releases carbon dioxide. Upstream leaks of methane, itself a strong greenhouse gas, add climate effects.

Electrolysis uses electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen. A low-emissions result depends on electricity supply, how the equipment operates, water treatment, and the factory making the equipment. If an electrolyser runs on a coal-heavy grid, its output should not automatically be called low-carbon. Using new low-carbon generation matched to operations can improve the emissions profile, though exact accounting rules remain a policy question.

A third route makes hydrogen from fossil fuels while capturing and storing some carbon dioxide. Its results depend on the share captured, methane emissions in the supply chain, energy consumed by capture systems, and how securely the carbon is stored. Labels such as green and blue are shorthand; robust comparison requires a life-cycle emissions measure across the full process. [2][3]

3. Step 2: electrolysis converts electricity into a different energy form

An electrolyser contains electrodes and an electrolyte, a material that lets ions move between the electrodes. Electricity drives reactions that form hydrogen at one side and oxygen at the other. The gases must be separated, handled safely, and in many cases compressed. A water-treatment system may be needed because certain electrolysers require high-purity water.

Conservation of energy still applies. The chemical energy stored in the hydrogen is smaller than the electrical energy sent to the complete system, because some energy leaves as heat and process losses. Further energy is needed if the gas is compressed, liquefied, transported, turned into ammonia, or converted back into electricity in a fuel cell or turbine.

This explains the practical hierarchy for many energy uses. When a battery or electric machine can use electricity directly, converting electricity into hydrogen and back often adds avoidable losses. A battery-electric car generally uses electricity more directly than a route that first manufactures hydrogen, transports it, and converts it back to power in a fuel cell. But chemistry, high-temperature industrial reactions, long storage periods, or high-energy fuels for ships and aircraft can change the comparison. The relevant question is which service is being supplied, not whether hydrogen is a fashionable material.

4. Step 3: cost follows electricity, capital, and operating hours

The levelized cost of hydrogen combines the project's investment, financing, operating expense, electricity, and output over its useful life. Electrolysers are expensive equipment. If they run for only a small share of the year, fewer kilograms of hydrogen carry the same fixed annual costs. If they run on costly electricity around the clock, the energy bill rises. The best schedule balances these competing effects.

Electricity price is important because producing one kilogram of hydrogen requires far more electricity than the chemical energy contained in the kilogram, after losses. The commonly used lower heating value of hydrogen is approximately 33.3 kWh per kilogram. Electrolyser efficiency varies by technology, operating conditions, and what equipment is included in the measurement. It is unsafe to compare efficiency claims without checking system boundaries.

Worked example: 1,000 kilograms of hydrogen

Assume a hypothetical plant consumes 52 kWh of electricity for each kilogram of hydrogen. To make 1,000 kg it needs 52,000 kWh, or 52 MWh. At an illustrative electricity price of US$40 per MWh, the electricity bill is US$2,080, equal to US$2.08 per kg. That is only the electricity input cost. It excludes the electrolyser, water supply, maintenance, financing, compression, storage, transport, and any fees.

The ratio between hydrogen's lower heating value and electricity consumed would be 33.3 ÷ 52, or about 64% under these assumptions. If electricity costs US$80 per MWh, the energy component doubles to US$4.16 per kg. If the electrolyser consumes 60 rather than 52 kWh per kg, the energy cost at US$40 per MWh rises to US$2.40 per kg. These calculations show how costs change; they are not a plant quotation or an estimate of current regional market prices.

Water consumption also needs a boundary. Chemical reaction mass requires about nine litres of water per kilogram of hydrogen, but real facilities may use more after treatment and cooling. In a water-scarce location, access to water and safe handling of waste streams need direct local study. The water requirement should be compared with competing uses, not judged by a global average alone.

5. What current data show

The IEA's 2026 review reports that electrolyser capacity doubled during 2025 to exceed 4 GW, with China accounting for much of the new installation. That indicates faster equipment deployment, but installed capacity is not the same as annual hydrogen production, and announced capacity is not the same as a finished plant. [2]

For projects expected by 2030, the IEA distinguishes those with final investment decisions from those only announced. In 2026 it estimated 4.3 million tonnes of committed annual low-emissions production for 2030, with a higher potential if additional plausible projects secure decisions. The total announced project pipeline had shrunk to 27 million tonnes after delays and cancellations. These numbers are different certainty levels, not contradictory forecasts. [2][4]

Investors often require an offtake agreement: a contract in which a buyer agrees to purchase output. Without dependable demand at a price that covers production costs, manufacturers and lenders may hesitate to fund a new plant. The IEA reports that newly signed low-emissions hydrogen offtake agreements were about 1.7 million tonnes per year in 2025, but only around one-fifth of the volume had firm commitments. Supply targets therefore do not solve the demand problem by themselves. [1]

6. Where hydrogen is comparatively useful

Hydrogen is already an input to making ammonia. Replacing conventional hydrogen at a fertiliser plant can reduce emissions without inventing a completely new market for hydrogen use. But costs still matter: higher fertiliser prices can affect farmers, food producers, and public subsidies. A policy may need to support verified low-emissions inputs during the transition.

In ironmaking, hydrogen-based direct reduction can replace part of the carbon used to remove oxygen from iron ore, if the process has suitable materials, technology, and low-carbon power. By contrast, ordinary space heating can usually use electric heat pumps much more efficiently. Hydrogen's value depends on the chemical process and available alternatives.

A future application that is technically possible is not necessarily economically attractive today.

7. Competing views and business risks

Advocates argue that hydrogen can decarbonize industries that cannot easily run on direct electricity and can link renewable energy to chemical and fuel markets. This claim is credible for some uses, especially replacing conventional hydrogen in existing industry. Skeptics warn that broad hydrogen targets can divert clean electricity from more efficient uses and depend on permanent subsidies. This criticism is strongest when a proposed application has a straightforward electric alternative.

Both positions are best tested application by application. Compare the full chain from energy input to service delivered, then include emissions, cost, safety, land, infrastructure, and supply reliability. A project's press release often contains capacity figures. An investment analysis needs contracted buyers, operating hours, prices, and evidence that the product meets emissions standards.

There is also a policy trade-off between supporting early production and creating reliable demand. Subsidizing supply alone may lead to idle capacity. Mandating demand without enough affordable supply may increase industrial costs. A coordinated policy can target existing users, develop safety and accounting rules, and invest in shared infrastructure where several businesses need it.

8. Why it matters

Hydrogen affects industry, energy security, fertiliser production, trade, and the cost of reaching climate goals. The IEA's 2026 report also highlights geopolitical risks in hydrogen-derived product supply chains. Existing global manufacturing and shipping networks can be disrupted even when the end consumer never sees hydrogen directly. [1]

The long-term role of low-emissions hydrogen is important but uncertain. Lower electrolyser costs, cleaner power, durable industrial contracts, and credible carbon accounting could improve competitiveness. Higher financing costs, weak demand, or poor grid access could delay projects. The evidence supports targeted development in hard-to-electrify and existing chemical uses before assuming hydrogen is the best solution for every sector.

Key lessons

  1. Hydrogen is an energy carrier, and its emissions depend on production and delivery.
  2. Existing industrial uses dominate hydrogen demand.
  3. Low-emissions output was about 1% of 2025 global demand scale.
  4. Electrolysis consumes electricity, and transport or conversion can add losses.
  5. Direct electricity is often more efficient when it can deliver the same service.
  6. Electricity price, operating hours, capital and financing shape hydrogen cost.
  7. Announced projects and investment-committed projects are different.
  8. Firm buyers, standards and infrastructure are central to the business case.

Knowledge check

Answer each question before you open the explanation.

Why can hydrogen made with electricity have high emissions?

The power may come from high-emission generation. A sound assessment tracks the electricity supply and the full life cycle.

What does the 52 kWh/kg worked example imply for 1,000 kg?

52,000 kWh, or 52 MWh. At US$40 per MWh, electricity alone costs US$2,080.

Why is a final investment decision different from a project announcement?

The decision shows a stronger financial commitment. Announced projects can be delayed, downsized, or cancelled.

Why can direct electrification be better for some tasks?

It avoids the energy losses from producing, transporting, and converting hydrogen into useful power.

What can turn a plausible hydrogen factory into a weak business?

High power prices, low operating hours, expensive financing, absent buyer contracts, transport costs, and uncertain regulation.
