How Does the Internet Cross an Ocean—and What Happens When a Cable Breaks?

The physics, geography and governance behind the world's undersea communications network.

Executive summary

Most international digital traffic travels through glass fibres on or below the seabed. The fibres carry changing patterns of light. Coastal stations and underwater amplifiers keep those signals usable over long distances. The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) reported in February 2025 that submarine telecommunications cables carried more than 99% of international data traffic. The Internet Society's November 2025 policy brief gave a 97–98% estimate for intercontinental Internet traffic. The measures differ, but both show the same dependence on cables. Fishing gear, anchors and natural events damage this network. Operators may redirect traffic through other routes, yet a backup needs independent infrastructure and spare capacity. Physical repair needs specialist ships, tests, fibre splicing and permits. This study explains the optical mechanism, the geography of risk, how repair works, and the policies that can make international connections more dependable. Its main finding is that the reliability of a digital service depends on the complete route, including its coastal landing and inland network. [1] [3]

1. Why does the Internet use the seabed?

A message sent from India to Europe usually crosses several networks. The user's device connects to a local provider. Larger carriers and routers move the data toward a destination. Where the route crosses an ocean, a submarine optical cable is normally the main high-capacity link. The cable carries many users' data streams at the same time. It is not a dedicated wire for each video call or payment.

Satellites are useful for remote communities, ships, emergency links and places without a cable. But the large amount of cross-ocean traffic makes fibre attractive because of its high capacity and cost per unit of data. Satellite links have limits in spectrum, terminal capacity and shared service load. Both systems can support each other. It would be a mistake to assume that a satellite can always replace the full capacity of a failed transoceanic cable.

Two questions follow. First, how can thin glass move so much information thousands of kilometres? Second, why can one cable break have almost no visible effect while another interrupts an entire region? The answer requires physics, network routing, geography, economics and international law. [1] [3]

2. Essential terms

Optical fibre
A glass path that guides light carrying digital information.
Fibre pair
Two fibres often used for opposite transmission directions.
Landing station
A coastal site that joins a marine cable to land networks and supplies power to underwater equipment.
Repeater
An underwater device that strengthens optical signals.
Capacity
The amount of information a link can carry per second. Designed and used capacity are different.
Latency
The time between sending and receiving information.
Redundancy
Extra equipment or a different path available after a failure.
Resilience
The ability to continue useful service and recover after damage.
BGP
Border Gateway Protocol, which networks use to exchange information about reachable destinations.
A cable may be intact while an error in a router disrupts service. A cable may be broken while users stay connected through another path. These are different failures. A useful investigation tests the physical link and the network that uses it. [8]

3. From telegraph wires to fibre optics

The first transatlantic telegraph cable worked briefly in 1858. Its failure showed how difficult it was to insulate a wire, install it across a deep ocean and keep its signal readable. A more durable transatlantic connection began in 1866. Later systems carried telephone calls, with electrical repeaters helping signals travel longer distances.

The next major change was optical fibre. In 1988, the TAT-8 system became the first transatlantic fibre-optic cable. The IEEE records its initial rate as 280 megabits per second, connecting the United States, United Kingdom and France. That figure looks small today, but the technology gave engineers a path to much greater capacity. Improvements in lasers, optical amplifiers, modulation and signal processing allowed more information to pass through glass. [7]

The commercial system changed too. A cable may belong to a consortium of telecom firms or other investors. Capacity can be sold to several networks. Cloud companies may invest directly in routes. Therefore a line on a public cable map does not show exactly which service or company uses it.

Despite technical change, the old constraints remain. Every system needs a landing point, a surveyed marine route, equipment, maintenance and legal access. Faster light-based communication did not remove the influence of geography.

4. How big is the network in 2026?

TeleGeography's printed 2026 Submarine Cable Map depicts 694 systems, including in-service and planned projects, and 1,893 landing stations. Its continuously updated 2026 count gives 607 in-service systems and 102 planned systems. The figures differ because one is a dated snapshot and the other changes as projects are announced, completed or retired. It is incorrect to add the figures or treat them as measurements made at the same time. [2] [4]

The ITU's February 2025 announcement described about 500 submarine telecommunications cables spanning more than 1.7 million kilometres. That is an earlier, rounded estimate using its own definitions. A named cable system can contain several branches, segments and fibre pairs. A map route is often a simplified corridor rather than the exact surveyed position. Private and specialised infrastructure may be missing from public datasets. [1]

5. How light carries information

A transmitter turns electrical digital information into an optical signal using a laser. Modern equipment changes properties of the light in a controlled pattern. At the receiving end, equipment measures the optical signal and reconstructs the data. Forward error correction adds extra structured information so that some errors can be found and corrected.

Glass guides light along the fibre, but it does not carry a perfect signal forever. Some optical energy is lost. Dispersion can spread or distort pulses. Strong optical signals also interact with the glass in ways that affect quality. Engineers must balance distance, power, transmission rate and an acceptable error level.

Wavelength-division multiplexing allows many optical channels to share one fibre. Each channel uses a distinct wavelength, and equipment combines and separates them. Multiple fibre pairs increase available paths. The practical capacity depends on fibre quality, the optical channels, amplifier design and the equipment at each end.

An optical repeater strengthens light during a long crossing. Many systems use erbium-doped fibre amplifiers, powered by electricity carried through a conductive part of the cable. IEEE's technical overview gives typical long-haul repeater spacing of roughly 50–100 kilometres. A repeater increases signal strength, but it cannot erase accumulated noise. The complete link must still be designed with safety margins. [9] [10]

Near shore, cables may have steel armouring and be buried to reduce damage from anchors and fishing gear. In deep water, lighter cable can be appropriate because those hazards are less common. Protection depends on depth, seabed conditions and expected human activity.

The diagram explains a critical point. A cable can be healthy while a power failure at its landing station or an inland fibre cut stops service. Conversely, the submarine segment can fail while a separate route keeps a service online. An end-to-end test must include both the marine and land portions.

7. What happens when a route stops working?

Internet data travels in packets. Routers select the next network link toward a destination. Within one operator's network, routing and traffic engineering manage internal paths. Between separate networks, Border Gateway Protocol, or BGP, exchanges information about reachable destinations. When a path fails, operators may withdraw it and use another available path. [8]

This change is not guaranteed to be immediate. Network equipment must detect the fault, update routes and find a usable alternative. A backup path may have less capacity or be much longer. Some services can continue with greater delay, packet loss or reduced speed. Other services may fail completely.

BGP also does not expose the exact physical location of every fibre. Two network paths that appear separate can depend on the same landing station or coastal corridor. A country with three named cable systems is not necessarily protected against a single coastal disaster. A truly independent route needs separate physical assets and sufficient spare capacity.

Local caches and domestic Internet exchange points can help keep some traffic within a country. They cannot replace access to a remote service that has no local copy. The Internet Society's 2025 analysis discusses how diverse terrestrial links and local infrastructure helped reduce the impact of some submarine outages. [3]

8. What causes cable faults?

Most recorded damage involves human activity near the seabed. Heavy fishing equipment can catch a cable. An anchor may drag across its route. Busy coastal waters contain many vessels, so they face different risks from the deep ocean. Natural hazards include underwater landslides, earthquakes, strong sediment flows and abrasion. A large event can affect several cables in the same corridor.

A 2026 statistical paper places about 80% of recorded faults in fishing and anchoring categories combined, including cases where investigators cannot distinguish the two. Other industry summaries give figures around 70% or 86%, depending on dataset, year and classification. These are not interchangeable probabilities for every country. The supported conclusion is that fishing and anchoring are major global causes. [5] [11]

Cable damage sometimes appears in geopolitical news. But the physical fact of a break does not establish sabotage. Investigators need evidence about ships, equipment, damage and intent. It is also incorrect to assume that every unexplained cut was accidental. Reliable reporting separates what is observed from what remains uncertain.

Prevention can include better route surveys, accurate marine charts, burial in suitable seabed, safe anchoring practices and coordination with fishing fleets. None of these measures removes all risk. Resilience also needs recovery plans.

9. How specialists repair a broken cable

Step 1: Detect and locate. Monitoring equipment notices an optical or electrical fault. Engineers at landing stations test the system and estimate how far along the route the damage lies. They compare results with marine survey records. The first position may be approximate.Step 2: Mobilise a ship. A specialist cable vessel needs spare cable, tools, trained crew and access to the repair area. Weather, vessel availability and permits can delay work. A repair may involve several maritime jurisdictions.Step 3: Recover the cable. The ship may use a grapnel, a large seabed hook, or a remotely operated vehicle. A damaged cable may need to be cut before its ends can be lifted. The crew recovers and tests one end, secures it, and then recovers the other.Step 4: Splice and test. Engineers remove the damaged section and join individual optical fibres to a spare section. They rebuild protective joints and test the result. After the final connection, the repaired cable is lowered to the seabed and reburied where suitable. KIS-ORCA's technical guidance explains why this work can take many days. [6]

A network may regain service through another cable before the damaged one is repaired. Alternatively, a repaired cable may still require further equipment or routing work. Marine repair time and user-service recovery time are different measures.

10. Worked example: the delay of light

Illustrative calculation; not a measured cable route. Assume an optical path is 8,000 kilometres long and light travels through its glass at approximately 200,000 kilometres per second. One-way propagation time equals distance divided by speed: 8,000 ÷ 200,000 = 0.040 seconds, or 40 milliseconds. A return trip of the same length would take about 80 milliseconds for propagation alone.

Actual application delay is higher. Routes are not perfectly straight, network devices process packets, queues form, and return traffic can take a different path. Some applications also need several exchanges before completing an action. A detour after a cable cut may therefore make an interactive service slower even when enough bandwidth remains.

This is a teaching estimate. It does not measure the speed of a specific submarine system or the delay of an India–Europe route.

11. Worked example: the backup-capacity gap

Illustrative network planning case. A network carries 120 gigabits per second of busy-hour international traffic through its main route. An independent backup path has 80 gigabits per second of unused capacity. If the primary route fails, the backup can carry at most 80 of the 120 gigabits per second under these assumptions. The uncovered demand is 40 gigabits per second, one-third of the original load.

Operators might prioritise payments and emergency services, use local caches or buy temporary capacity. Such measures can reduce disruption but do not create free bandwidth. If both paths share the same landing station, one coastal incident could remove both, making the calculation too optimistic.

Good planning tests more than a single broken fibre. It asks what happens if a whole landing station fails, if several cables in a corridor fail, or if an alternate route is congested. It also checks whether operators have the contracts and technical controls needed to use backup capacity.

12. Why some countries face greater risk

A country with many physically separate routes can usually absorb more failures than a country with one cable and no practical land connection. But cable count alone is not enough. Several cables can land in one building, use the same inland backbone or cross the same exposed seabed. One natural event may then damage multiple systems.

Small islands face a difficult economic choice. A first cable can greatly improve digital access, while a second independent cable may cost more than the local market can repay through service charges. A public subsidy or regional partnership may be justified if the wider value of dependable communication exceeds commercial revenue. Large countries face different problems: they may have many cables but still depend on a few important international corridors.

The Internet Society's 2025 brief discusses outages in West Africa and examples of Bangladesh using terrestrial links and local content caches. It recommends geographic diversity, stronger local infrastructure and cooperation. [3]

Resilience should be measured through spare capacity, landing diversity, inland connectivity, network-provider diversity, local hosting and observed recovery time. Public maps and traceroute tests offer useful clues, but neither reveals every private fibre or the exact route of every packet.

13. Law, investment and security

A new cable requires surveys, manufacturing, ships, landing facilities, equipment and years of planning. Investors compare expected demand with the cost and risk of construction. High-demand routes attract finance more easily than a second cable to a small island. Governments may support socially valuable backup routes, but subsidies should have clear rules on access, performance and accountability.

The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea recognises rights to lay cables in specified maritime areas. Article 112 addresses the high seas beyond the continental shelf. Article 113 requires states to adopt laws against certain wilful or culpably negligent cable damage on the high seas. National coastal permits, environmental rules and port procedures still matter. International law does not create one universal permit for every repair. [12]

Security has several parts: protecting landing sites, controlling access to network equipment, monitoring physical faults and keeping routing systems secure. The Internet Society notes that BGP requires additional operational security practices. A cable cut is not enough to prove a hostile act. [8]

The ITU and International Cable Protection Committee have promoted cooperation on repair access and geographic diversity. The ITU's 2026 advisory-body publication gives guidance on these issues. Faster emergency approval should still respect environmental protection and legitimate coastal interests. [13]

14. What about the marine environment?

Telecommunications cables can disturb the seabed during laying, burial and repair. The effects depend on local habitat, sediment, depth and method. A deep-ocean cable placed on a stable seabed is different from a cable buried through sensitive coastal habitat. Installation vessels and trenching can affect marine organisms and sediment.

NOAA's regulatory overview says the environmental effects of submarine fibre-optic cables are generally believed to be modest, while warning that poor installation and trenching can cause damage. That is a qualified assessment, not a claim of zero impact. The overview also distinguishes communications cables from some power cables that may have additional risks. [14]

There is a real trade-off. Burial can reduce later damage from anchors and fishing, but the trench itself causes disturbance. Leaving a cable on the seabed can avoid some trenching but may increase future snag risk. Route surveys and local environmental assessment help choose the better method.

Environmental review and resilience planning should be done together. A route that avoids one sensitive habitat may face a different geological hazard or longer repair time. The best choice depends on measured local conditions.

15. Three choices with no universal answer

Build another cable or improve the existing network? A new route can reduce common-cause risk. But local caches, independent inland fibre, extra spare capacity and better routing may solve an immediate weakness at lower cost. The right priority depends on the actual failure point.Use public funding or rely on private investment? Private investors can build where demand is strong. In small markets, the public benefit of a backup connection may be greater than the revenue a cable operator can earn. Public finance can be reasonable, but it needs measurable service and open-access conditions.Focus on preventing damage or on recovery? Better surveys, cable protection and maritime awareness reduce faults. They cannot stop every natural event or accident. Spare routes, repair ships, permits and tested response plans remain necessary.

These are practical trade-offs, not disputes that one slogan can settle. The ITU's 2026 resilience guidance and the Internet Society's 2025 policy work support combining prevention, independent paths and recovery planning. [3] [13]

16. Ten lessons

  1. Submarine optical cables carry most international digital traffic.
  2. Light carries data; shore stations and repeaters support long crossings.
  3. Published cable counts change with date and definition.
  4. More cables do not always mean independent routes.
  5. Landing stations and land fibre are part of the risk.
  6. Fishing and anchoring are major recorded causes of damage.
  7. A break alone does not establish sabotage.
  8. Rerouting requires spare capacity and usable paths.
  9. Marine repairs need specialist ships, tests and often permits.
  10. Resilience requires prevention, backup paths and recovery plans.

Five knowledge questions and answers

1. Why can one fibre carry several data streams?
Several optical wavelengths can share the same fibre. Equipment combines and separates them. Signal quality and equipment still limit capacity.
2. Does having three cables guarantee three independent paths?
No. The cables may share a landing station, corridor or inland network.
3. Why can a cut cause slower service rather than total failure?
Traffic may move to a longer or more congested backup route.
4. Why can marine repair take days?
Specialist ships must locate, recover, splice, test and redeploy the cable, often after obtaining permits.
5. What proves a cut was deliberate?
Investigative evidence connecting an act and actor to intentional damage. A broken cable alone is insufficient.
