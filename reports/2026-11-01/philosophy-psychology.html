Why Do People Keep Investing in a Failing Plan?

The psychology of sunk costs, evidence for escalation and a clear way to choose what comes next.

Research checked 1 November 2026 · Original source dates below · Both calculations are illustrative

Executive summary

People sometimes continue a project because they have already spent money, time or effort on it. Economists call these past, unrecoverable investments sunk costs. In a decision about what to do next, the relevant comparison is between future benefits and future costs. Yet experiments show that prior investment can influence decisions even when it should not change that comparison. Psychology offers several possible mechanisms: a wish to justify an earlier choice, fear of appearing wasteful, attention to progress toward a goal, and the way people record spending in mental accounts. The evidence is mixed in important ways. Some experiments find strong persistence effects; others find small effects under carefully controlled conditions. Research also separates the decision to use something already bought from the decision to spend more on an unfinished project. This guide traces the original studies, explains each mechanism and reviews competing interpretations. Two illustrative calculations show when stopping is rational and when continuing can still be justified. The practical goal is a decision process that respects new evidence without treating past effort as either a command to continue or proof that stopping is always correct.

1. The central question

A team has spent months building a product. Early tests are poor, but members still want to finish because stopping would make the earlier work feel wasted. Another team faces the same future prospects but has invested almost nothing. Should the two teams make different choices? If their future options and outcomes are identical, earlier spending alone should not change the answer. Yet research finds that it often does. The central question is: why can an unrecoverable past investment change a decision about future action?

The question applies to public projects, businesses and personal plans. A bad result does not automatically mean that stopping is wise. A team may learn from failures, gain new capabilities or face duties to others. The task is to identify whether continuation follows from future value or mainly protects an earlier decision. This distinction prevents a useful psychological concept from becoming a label for every unpopular project. [1] [2]

2. Define the terms

A sunk cost is a resource already spent that cannot be recovered through a new choice. A marginal cost is the additional cost of the next step. A marginal benefit is the extra benefit expected from that step. Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative forgone. Expected value combines possible outcomes with their probabilities. These concepts help compare choices that remain available today.

The sunk-cost effect occurs when irrecoverable spending changes a choice in a way future costs and benefits do not justify. Escalation of commitment is continued or increased support for an earlier plan despite unfavorable feedback. Escalation can have causes beyond sunk costs. Mental accounting means assigning money or effort to psychological budgets. Self-justification is the effort to defend an earlier choice or one's image as a competent decision-maker. These are possible mechanisms, not diagnoses of a person's motives.

3. The history of the evidence

Barry Staw studied escalating commitment in 1976. In a simulated business decision involving 240 students, he varied responsibility for an earlier choice and the results of that choice. Participants committed the most additional resources when they felt personally responsible for negative outcomes. The finding supported a role for self-justification, although the study used a specific simulated task. [2]

Arkes and Blumer's 1985 paper established several forms of the sunk-cost effect. Later work tested the amount invested, the role of budgeting, the treatment of time and the conditions under which persistence disappears. By 2007, a laboratory study found a surprisingly small effect under its conditions. A meta-analysis published online in 2014 and in a 2015 volume integrated 98 effect sizes. The research history is therefore a sequence of refinements, not a single finding that everyone always continues. [1] [6] [8]

4. Step 1: list the options that still exist

A decision must start with present choices. Can the project stop, pause, change direction, sell an asset or continue? For each option, describe what will happen next. Money paid last year cannot be recovered by another month of work. It belongs in the historical account but does not become a new benefit of continuing. The same logic applies to time already spent learning a skill or preparing a plan.

History can still matter because it may leave assets. Equipment can be sold. Research may be reused. A trained team may perform better tomorrow. Those are current or future benefits, so they belong in the calculation. The distinction is between a past cost that is gone and a capability that still changes outcomes. A good analysis respects history without letting the amount already spent determine the next decision.

5. Step 2: compare future benefits and costs

Estimate the extra cost of each option, the likely future return and the chance of failure. Compare continuing with the best available alternative. If continuing offers a higher expected return after additional costs, it can be rational even when the project has disappointed its sponsors. If the return is worse, continuing only to justify past spending can increase losses.

Expected value is not a complete moral or business rule. A project may offer a positive average return but expose a small company to an unacceptable chance of insolvency. A public project may create benefits that are difficult to price. Contracts and safety duties may limit the available choices. These factors should be stated openly. They are real future consequences and can justify an action without relying on sunk costs.

6. Step 3: examine personal responsibility

A manager who approved a project may feel that ending it would admit an earlier error. Continuing may appear to preserve competence or reputation. Staw's experiment linked personal responsibility and negative feedback to greater further investment. The result is consistent with self-justification, although no one experiment can prove that this mechanism explains every continuation decision. [2]

Responsibility also has a useful side: people should remain accountable for decisions. The problem appears when accountability becomes pressure to defend a choice regardless of evidence. One safeguard is an independent continuation review. Ask a reviewer to evaluate the remaining options before showing the historical spending. Then compare the conclusions. If the answer changes sharply after revealing the past cost, examine whether that change reflects useful information or emotional commitment.

7. Step 4: understand the wish to avoid waste

People dislike wasting money and effort. This concern can encourage care and planning. But a loss that already occurred cannot be undone by creating another loss. The statement that a project must continue because so much has been spent does not show that the next investment will produce value. It only describes the past.

The word waste can also identify a genuine future loss. An unfinished system may contain reusable components, and closing it might destroy a valuable option. The review should estimate the value of those components, the cost of preserving them and the best alternative. This makes the distinction clear: preserving a valuable asset can be rational; defending an unrecoverable payment is not a sufficient reason to spend again.

8. Step 5: separate spending from completion

Past spending and project progress often rise together. A project that has used most of its budget may also be close to completion. Finishing can then be a good choice because the remaining cost is small. Research must separate the amount already spent from how much useful work remains. Otherwise an apparent sunk-cost effect may partly reflect a rational response to progress.

Howard Garland's 1990 experiment varied how much of a research budget had already been used. The study found strong patterns of further commitment in two measures. Later research emphasized the need to distinguish sunk costs, progress and remaining work. A project review should therefore request an independent estimate of work left, rather than rely on the original percentage-complete claim. [3] [8]

9. Step 6: use mental budgets carefully

People divide resources into budgets for travel, research, teams and products. This mental accounting can help control spending. It can also encourage continuation because a budget has already been assigned. Chip Heath's 1995 study examined how budgets and mental accounts can support escalation or de-escalation. A budget is therefore a tool whose effect depends on how it is used. [4]

A useful rule sets a maximum additional commitment before the project begins. It also sets review dates and evidence needed for the next stage. A weak rule lets managers increase the budget each time they approach its limit. A strong rule requires a fresh case for every extension, including what the next payment will teach or produce. This changes the focus from defending the old budget to buying future value.

10. Step 7: distinguish money from time

It is common to treat time already spent as equivalent to money already paid. The evidence is more complex. In three experiments published in 2001, Dilip Soman found that earlier time investments did not create the same sunk-cost pattern as monetary investments in the tested choices. When time was represented in monetary terms, the effect reappeared. The format of the decision changed behavior. [5]

This does not mean that time never affects persistence. Different tasks can involve effort, waiting, identity and accumulated skill. A person who spent years on a career may have gained knowledge that changes future opportunities. They may also feel reluctant to change because of the years already used. A careful decision separates these two effects. Real skills are future assets; irrecoverable time is not itself a future return.

11. Why the evidence is mixed

Arkes and Blumer, Staw and Garland reported effects in particular experimental settings. Friedman and colleagues later used a computer search game, varied eleven treatment conditions and found a surprisingly small sunk-cost effect. Macaskill and Hackenberg's 2013 laboratory experiments found that persistence depended on the relative work needed to continue or escape. These results challenge a claim that the effect must appear at the same strength in every task. [6] [9]

Research methods differ. A hypothetical project scenario may evoke responsibility or reputation. A repeated computer task may emphasize immediate rewards. Participants may have different information about success and progress. A fair interpretation considers the task, sample and measured outcome. The evidence supports the existence of sunk-cost behavior while leaving open how often it controls major real-world decisions.

12. What a meta-analysis adds

Roth, Robbert and Straus published a meta-analysis online on 19 September 2014, with the article appearing in a 2015 volume. It combined 98 effect sizes from studies of monetary sunk costs. The authors distinguished utilization decisions, such as using something already purchased, from progress decisions, such as spending more to finish a project. They found evidence of the effect, with size and moderators depending on the decision type. [8]

The review also found that some utilization effects weaken with time and that participant age can matter. Such findings describe patterns across selected studies, not a forecast for a particular individual. A decision about attending a prepaid event is different from approving another round of investment. One involves using an existing right; the other creates new costs. Combining them without distinction can lead to weak general claims.

13. What brain imaging can show

Haller and Schwabe's 2014 functional magnetic resonance imaging study used a financial decision task. The authors reported associations between sunk-cost behavior and activity in prefrontal brain regions involved in valuation and control. They suggested that previous investments can change how current value is processed. This provides a possible biological perspective on the behavior. [7]

An imaging association does not identify a single cause or prove that a brain region produces the decision. The study used a specific task and sample. Social pressure, incentives and incomplete information can also influence behavior. The practical lesson is to examine several processes at once. A company cannot diagnose sunk-cost errors from a brain scan, and the research does not support such a use.

14. Diagram: two paths after a poor result

The same disappointing result can produce two different forms of reasoning. One starts with the amount already spent and asks how to justify it. The other starts with the options that remain and compares their future consequences. Both paths may end with continuation, but only the second directly tests whether the new investment is worthwhile.

Use the diagram during a project review. Ask the team to explain why it should continue without referring to the past payment. If the case still rests on credible future benefits, the project may deserve support. If the argument disappears when past spending is removed, more evidence is needed. The diagram is a decision aid, not an experimental result.

15. Table: three different decisions

A utilization choice asks whether to use something already purchased. A progress choice asks whether to add resources to unfinished work. An exit choice asks whether to sell, transfer or close an activity. These decisions have different future costs, rights and alternatives. Research that mixes them may obscure the causes of persistence.

The table identifies the relevant comparison for each choice. Historical spending still matters for financial records, contracts and learning. It should not be counted as a new benefit. If an old asset can be sold or reused, its current value belongs in the future comparison. That is why a precise definition of the decision is more useful than simply telling people to ignore history.

16. Illustrative example 1: stopping is rational

Illustrative data, not a real company. A firm has already spent ₹12 lakh developing a tool. This amount cannot be recovered. Completion requires another ₹5 lakh. Independent research estimates a 40% chance of ₹8 lakh in future receipts and a 60% chance of ₹2 lakh, both before the additional completion cost. Expected future receipts are 0.40 × ₹8 lakh + 0.60 × ₹2 lakh = ₹4.4 lakh.

The incremental expected value of continuing is ₹4.4 lakh − ₹5 lakh = minus ₹0.6 lakh. Assume stopping has no further cost or salvage value. On expected monetary value alone, stopping is better by ₹0.6 lakh. The earlier ₹12 lakh does not change this result. Other obligations or benefits could change the decision, but they must be identified separately. Calling the old spending a reason to finish does not create a positive return.

17. Illustrative example 2: continuing is rational

Illustrative data, not a real project. A software team has spent ₹20 lakh and needs ₹3 lakh to finish. A review estimates a 70% chance of ₹10 lakh in future benefits and a 30% chance of ₹1 lakh, expressed in comparable present-value terms. Expected benefits are 0.70 × ₹10 lakh + 0.30 × ₹1 lakh = ₹7.3 lakh.

The incremental expected value is ₹7.3 lakh − ₹3 lakh = ₹4.3 lakh. If the best alternative offers no higher value and the risk is acceptable, continuing is justified. The earlier ₹20 lakh is irrelevant to this calculation. This case shows why the principle is about future evidence, not an instruction to stop every troubled project. A rational decision can recommend continuing after a large past loss.

18. Competing explanations and interpretations

Self-justification predicts stronger persistence when people feel responsible for the original choice. Mental accounting predicts that the way costs are recorded can change behavior. Progress-based explanations predict that people continue when they believe completion is near. An information-based account notes that earlier spending may signal private knowledge or create valuable capabilities. These explanations can coexist, and different tasks may activate different processes.

The evidence also differs in effect size. Strong patterns in some scenario studies coexist with small effects in controlled repeated tasks. The meta-analysis found a general effect with important differences across decision types. A defensible conclusion is that sunk-cost behavior exists but depends on context. It would be wrong to call every continuation irrational, just as it would be wrong to assume that earlier spending is always a valid reason for new spending. [6] [8]

19. How to design a better review process

Before work starts, record what success would look like and what evidence would trigger a change. Use staged budgets with clear decision dates. At each review, estimate remaining work from current facts, not from the original plan. Compare continuing with an alternative. Invite an independent reviewer to challenge the forecast and document the range of possible outcomes.

Separate learning from blame. Teams are more likely to report bad news when stopping a weak project is treated as a legitimate result of testing. Keep the original assumptions visible so revisions can be checked. If the project continues, state what new evidence will be collected and when the decision will be reviewed again. This process does not remove uncertainty, but it makes the reasons for commitment easier to inspect.

20. The ethical limits of a financial rule

Some commitments create duties to customers, workers or communities. Closing a project can require refunds, safe transition plans and support for affected people. These are genuine future costs. They belong in the decision even if the original investment is unrecoverable. A public project may also create social benefits that a narrow financial forecast misses.

Persistence can express loyalty or responsibility. Yet continuing a harmful project to avoid admitting error may violate the same duties that motivated it. An ethical review asks who bears future costs, who gains, which promises remain binding and whether affected people can respond. The principle of ignoring sunk costs does not erase these questions. It makes room to discuss them without confusing moral duties with the wish to recover an old payment.

21. Wider effects on institutions and personal life

In business, weak projects can absorb funds that might support stronger products. In government, political concern about cancellation can make large projects difficult to stop. In personal life, years invested in a plan can make change feel like failure. The incentives and obligations differ, so the same numerical rule cannot settle every case.

Institutions can improve accountability by publishing forecasts, recording changes and using independent reviews. A project stopped after weak evidence can still produce valuable learning. A project continued after strong new evidence can also be sound. The useful standard is whether decision-makers explain the expected future consequences and accept review when the facts change. That standard rewards accurate updates rather than completion for its own sake.

22. A final decision checklist

List the options. Separate unrecoverable spending from remaining assets. Estimate extra costs, expected benefits, risk and opportunity costs. Include legal duties and other effects on people. Ask whether the conclusion would be the same if a different team had made the original investment. Record what evidence could change the recommendation.

If the project continues, define the next milestone and review date. If it stops, document what was learned and protect people affected by the closure. Do not use the phrase sunk-cost fallacy as a shortcut for rejecting a plan. Use it to test whether the future case stands without the past payment. The strongest choice follows current options, credible forecasts and stated values.

Key lessons

  1. Separate past losses from new costs.
  2. Compare continuation with a real alternative.
  3. Do not confuse money spent with work completed.
  4. Estimate remaining work independently.
  5. Check the effect of personal responsibility.
  6. Use staged budgets and review dates.
  7. Treat time and money as different possible cues.
  8. Expect effects to vary across tasks.
  9. Continuing can be rational on future value.
  10. Include duties, risk and affected people.

Five knowledge-check questions and answers

1. What is a sunk cost?

A past investment that cannot be recovered through the present choice.

2. How is escalation different from the sunk-cost effect?

Escalation is continued support for a plan. Sunk costs are one possible cause, alongside progress, incentives and information.

3. What did Staw test in 1976?

Responsibility and outcomes in a simulated investment. The strongest further commitments followed personal responsibility for negative results.

4. When is continuing rational?

When its expected future value, after new costs and risks, is better than the available alternatives.

5. What belongs in a review?

Remaining costs, future benefits, risk, alternatives, duties, evidence and a clear next decision date.
