Executive summary

A business usually buys electricity when its machines need it. Yet some work can move by minutes or hours without changing the final product. Demand flexibility is the ability to change the time or level of electricity use when prices, grid conditions, or renewable supply change. It can reduce peak demand, ease network stress, and create revenue through electricity-market programmes. It does not automatically save energy or cut emissions. A cheap hour can still rely on coal, and a poorly designed load shift can interrupt production or cause extra losses. The International Energy Agency's 2026 work finds that industrial demand response is already important but much potential remains unused. Britain's electricity system operator has also published measured results from paying customers to shift demand. Original research shows why financial savings and carbon savings must be tested separately. This guide explains the engineering, commercial contracts, measurement methods, and competing options. It provides two illustrative calculations for a factory, with clear assumptions. The central decision is whether a reliable, measurable shift creates net value after operational risk and real emissions are included.

Central question and decision rule

Central question: When can a business change the timing of electricity use, keep its service or production output, earn a financial return, and reduce the environmental impact of its operations?

Answer with five checks. Identify a load that can move without harm. Measure its actual power and timing. Find a tariff or market that rewards the change. Include equipment, labour, product quality, and contract costs. Finally, calculate emissions using the generation that actually changes when the load moves. A positive electricity-bill result is useful, but it does not by itself establish a climate benefit.

This guide extends earlier reports on industrial heat pumps and data-centre cooling. Those reports asked which equipment uses less energy or water. Here the key variable is time. Two factories can consume the same annual electricity and place different demands on a power system because they use it at different hours.

1. From fixed schedules to flexible demand

Electricity networks must keep supply and demand in balance every moment. Traditionally, system operators adjusted generators to follow customers' consumption. Large factories sometimes accepted interruptible tariffs: they received lower rates in return for reducing demand during system stress. Utilities also used time-of-use prices to discourage consumption at busy hours.

Digital meters, controls, and automated production systems now make shorter and more precise changes possible. Wind and solar output also varies by weather and hour. This creates value in moving some consumption toward periods of abundant supply, provided the move is technically safe. The International Renewable Energy Agency's February 2019 innovation review described demand response, storage, market design, and digital control as connected parts of renewable integration. [1]

The International Energy Agency's 23 December 2025 policy brief and 26 June 2026 report treat demand flexibility as a system resource, rather than only an emergency measure. The reports discuss peak management, network use, consumer participation, and the need for better market rules. They are system analyses, not guarantees of savings for an individual business. [2] [3]

2. Terms that make the numbers clear

Power is the rate of electricity use, measured in kilowatts (kW) or megawatts (MW). Energy is power used over time, measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh) or megawatt-hours (MWh). Reducing 100 kW for one hour changes 100 kWh during that hour. Peak demand is the highest power requirement over a defined interval. Demand response means changing use after a price, control, or system signal.

Load shifting moves electricity use from one period to another. Load shedding reduces use without necessarily making it up later. Demand flexibility covers both. Baseline is an estimate of electricity that a participant would have used without a demand-response event. Aggregator combines flexible loads from many customers and may sell their response to a grid or market operator.

Time-of-use tariff charges different rates at defined times. Dynamic price can change more often with market conditions. Capacity payment rewards availability to reduce load; activation payment rewards a measured response when called. Marginal emissions estimate how much generation emissions change because demand changes at a particular time and place. They differ from an annual average grid emissions factor.

3. The operating mechanism in seven steps

Step 1 — Measure the load. Use interval meters to find power demand by machine and hour. Do not infer flexibility from a monthly bill. Step 2 — Identify the process constraint. A cold store may have a safe temperature band; a batch process may have a production deadline. Record limits for temperature, pressure, quality, safety, and delivery.

Step 3 — Define a shift window. Decide how far the load can move and how long the change can last. A process that can pause for ten minutes is different from one that can move to the next day. Step 4 — Receive a signal. A tariff, market operator, or aggregator requests a reduction or a shift to a lower-cost hour.

Step 5 — Execute safely. Automated controls change the schedule while retaining manual override. Step 6 — Measure performance. Compare actual consumption with a credible baseline and record any later rebound. Step 7 — Settle and learn. Apply contract rules, payments, penalties, and operational costs. Update the safe operating window after each event. This cycle turns a theoretical flexible load into a service that can be audited.

5. Which business loads can move?

Flexible loads often have a buffer between electricity use and the service they provide. Refrigeration can sometimes cool within a safe band before a peak period. Water pumping may fill a tank earlier. Compressed-air systems can use storage if pressure and leakage are controlled. Electric heating may use thermal storage. Some manufacturing batches can start earlier or later if workers, materials, and delivery deadlines permit.

Other loads are difficult to move. A continuous chemical reaction may have strict temperature and safety conditions. A hospital cannot delay critical equipment. A factory that already runs at full capacity may have no spare production hours. A machine that takes hours to restart may lose more value than any electricity saving. Flexibility is therefore a property of the whole process, not only the motor or compressor.

The IEA's Electricity 2026, published 6 February 2026, estimated about 100 GW of demand response in use globally in 2024, with around 75 GW from industry. These are system-level estimates of utilised capacity. They do not mean every industrial process can reduce power for the same duration or frequency. [4]

6. Four commercial ways to earn value

Time-of-use savings: A company moves a process from an expensive tariff period to a cheaper one. The value depends on the difference in energy prices and the amount of energy actually moved. Peak-demand reduction: Some tariffs charge for maximum measured power. A carefully scheduled load can lower that charge, but only if it reduces the relevant billing peak.

Grid-service payments: An operator or aggregator may pay a customer to reduce consumption at specified times. Contracts can include capacity commitments, minimum performance, penalties, and settlement delays. Renewable matching: A company may shift consumption toward periods of abundant low-carbon generation. That can reduce curtailment or system emissions in suitable conditions, but it needs a credible time- and location-specific method.

These values are not always additive. A company cannot assume it will receive a tariff saving, a grid payment, and a carbon credit for the same action. Market rules may prohibit double payment or impose a baseline method that changes measured delivery. A sound business case lists each permitted revenue stream and the exact contract condition that makes it available.

7. Worked example one: the value of shifting a factory load

Illustrative figures; these are not an actual Indian tariff or market offer. A factory can move a 200 kW process for two hours on each of 120 days. Energy moved is 200 × 2 × 120 = 48,000 kWh. Assume the old hours cost ₹12/kWh and the new hours cost ₹5/kWh. The gross bill difference is 48,000 × ₹7 = ₹3,36,000 per year. Suppose added labour, control maintenance, and process costs total ₹80,000 a year. The illustrative annual net benefit is ₹2,56,000, before tax and any investment cost.

The calculation assumes no loss of output and no extra electricity use. If the shift causes overtime, spoilage, heat loss, or extra start-up energy, the net benefit falls. If the plant has a demand charge, the bill effect also depends on whether the shifted load sets the monthly peak. No carbon saving can be inferred from this price calculation alone.

8. Why a baseline is essential

Suppose a factory is paid for using less electricity during an event. The operator cannot directly observe what it would have consumed without the request. It needs a baseline. One method uses recent comparable days and adjusts for weather or production. Another uses a statistical forecast. Both can be wrong if the factory changes shifts, product mix, or maintenance.

A participant might increase consumption just before an event to make the measured reduction look larger. A poor baseline can reward behaviour that creates no real system benefit. This is why markets use eligibility rules, interval meters, event logs, and independent settlement methods. A study using real feeder data from Goa, published online on 27 October 2022, examined methods for estimating customer baselines and their effect on demand-response evaluation. Its findings are methodological; they are not a national estimate of available Indian flexibility. [5]

Measurement should also include the rebound. If a chiller uses less electricity for one hour but then works harder for three hours, the event may reduce peak power without reducing total energy. That can still have grid value. The result must be labelled accurately as peak relief, energy saving, or both.

9. What measured programmes show

Britain's National Energy System Operator reported results for its Demand Flexibility Service for winter 2024–25 in a release dated 4 July 2025. It said 1.98 million consumers had registered, the service was used 44 times, and participants reduced or shifted 3,917.7 MWh. It reported accepted tenders of about £1.23 million and estimated wider balancing-cost savings of about £483,000. The operator also estimated more than 620 tonnes of avoided carbon emissions. [6]

These figures demonstrate that a coordinated programme can deliver measurable response. They are not a typical return for one factory. Registered customers are not the same as active participants, accepted bids are not the same as delivered energy, and system savings differ from payments made to participants. The baseline and dispatch method matter.

The IEA's June 2026 Scaling Up Demand Flexibility report uses case studies for South Africa, Thailand, and Ireland to examine how flexibility may support reliability and renewable integration. Some are future scenarios, so they should not be described as measured outcomes. The report stresses that smart equipment, clear incentives, and inclusive rules are needed to turn technical potential into dependable service. [3]

10. Why cheaper electricity may have higher emissions

A grid's average emissions factor divides total generation emissions by total electricity. It is useful for some annual accounting tasks. But a decision to move one load changes the output of the generators that respond at that hour. The relevant short-run question is which generator increases or decreases production. That is a marginal emissions question.

Fleschutz and co-authors studied price-based load shifting across 20 European electricity markets using 2017–19 data. Their 2021 Applied Energy study simulated standard shifts and found that price-based choices increased operating emissions in eight countries and by 2.1% on average across the study cases. The result came from specific fuel prices and dispatch conditions, including low-cost coal in some markets. It is a model result, not proof that every low-price hour is dirtier. [7]

Oak Ridge National Laboratory researchers published a 2023 study of load shifting guided by locational marginal emissions. Their simulated network cases showed potential emissions reductions when timing and location were considered. Those simulations support better control methods but are not field measurements for every grid. [8]

11. Worked example two: a carbon test for the same shift

Illustrative marginal emissions, not measured grid factors. Reuse the 48,000 kWh annual shift from the first example. Suppose the original hours have marginal emissions of 0.90 kg CO₂/kWh and the new hours have 0.45 kg CO₂/kWh. The estimated avoided emissions are 48,000 × (0.90 − 0.45) = 21,600 kg CO₂, or 21.6 tonnes. This assumes the same total electricity use and no change in other system operations.

Now reverse the carbon pattern: original hours at 0.35 kg CO₂/kWh and new hours at 0.80 kg CO₂/kWh. The same amount of energy moved would increase emissions by 21.6 tonnes. The price saving from the first calculation could still exist. The two objectives can therefore disagree.

A real assessment must use time- and location-specific estimates, account for extra energy from storage or start-up, and explain uncertainty. It should also avoid claiming an emissions reduction twice when several parties use the same activity in their reports. The lesson is a measurement rule, not a claim about the carbon intensity of any particular Indian hour.

12. The role of storage, controls, and process design

Storage can make a process flexible without changing its final output. A chilled-water tank can store cooling capacity. A hot-water vessel can store heat. A compressed-air receiver can cover a short peak if pressure remains safe. A battery can shift electricity separately from the production process. Each option has losses, capital costs, space needs, and maintenance duties.

Controls also matter. A manual instruction to switch off a compressor may be unreliable when production changes. A controller can read meter data, prices, temperature, and equipment state. It can choose an action within preset limits and record the result. Safety interlocks must override price signals. Operators need clear procedures for failure, network loss, and manual return to normal production.

Do not buy equipment before measuring the flexible load. A business might gain value simply by staggering existing machines. In other cases, an extra tank or improved insulation creates a wider safe window. A proper design compares the least-cost combination of process changes, control systems, storage, and tariff choice over the equipment's life.

13. Operational risks that can erase the return

Production has value beyond electricity. A food plant must protect temperature and hygiene. A metal furnace may face costly restart cycles. A warehouse must meet dispatch times. Even a small failure can outweigh a year's demand-response revenue. Each proposed load needs a documented safe operating range and a named person who can cancel an event.

Contract risk is separate. A market may require a minimum response, short notice, or proof of availability. Penalties can apply if the business fails to deliver. An aggregator may charge fees or keep part of the payment. Tariff structures can change. The finance team should test lower event frequency, smaller price differences, delayed settlement, and a period of zero revenue.

Cybersecurity and privacy also matter when controls connect to external services. The business should restrict access, log commands, test fail-safe modes, and separate safety controls from remote optimisation. A grid programme that cannot be audited or safely overridden is not ready for production deployment.

14. Strong competing views and alternatives

View one: demand flexibility is cheaper than building more supply. It can be, especially when a short peak drives large network or generation costs. The IEA reports show substantial untapped potential. Yet flexibility cannot produce energy during a long shortage. A process that can pause briefly cannot replace firm supply for many days. Networks, storage, and generation may still be needed.

View two: businesses should focus on efficiency first. Reducing wasted electricity often saves energy in every hour and can be easier to measure. This is a strong argument. But efficiency and flexibility can work together: efficient equipment can have smarter controls, while flexible scheduling can reduce expensive peaks without lowering annual consumption. The best order depends on the plant's costs and process.

View three: a cheap-hour shift is automatically green. Original research rejects that general rule. The generation mix, dispatch order, and location determine the emissions effect. In some markets the cheaper period can be more carbon-intensive. A business that values emissions must measure both price and marginal carbon signals, or clearly state that it is optimising cost only.

15. What a good flexibility contract should specify

Define the exact meter and equipment covered. State the response capacity in kW, the maximum event length, notice time, and allowed event frequency. Specify how the baseline is calculated, how rebound is treated, and who owns the interval data. Define what counts as a failed event and whether force majeure or a safety override changes the penalty.

Separate payments for availability from payments for delivered response. Show fees, minimum commitments, tax treatment, and settlement timing. Confirm whether the same load may join another market or tariff programme. Some markets restrict simultaneous participation to prevent double counting. The business should know who carries imbalance risk if a forecast is wrong.

Set a review date. A contract that is attractive under today's tariff can become poor if production changes or prices converge. For a pilot, limit the size of the commitment until the site has measured several real events. The pilot should record operational incidents and product quality, not only energy and payment data.

16. A practical twelve-week pilot structure

Weeks 1–2: Collect interval meter data and map large electrical loads. Record production schedules, equipment limits, and existing tariffs. Weeks 3–4: Select one process with a credible buffer. Confirm that moving it will not create safety, labour, or quality problems. Agree on the baseline method before any trial.

Weeks 5–6: Run controlled shifts without external payments. Measure the actual kW reduction, duration, rebound, and any extra kWh. Ask operators to report interruptions. Weeks 7–8: Compare available tariffs and market programmes. Include control costs, fees, and expected penalties. Check whether carbon data are suitable for the stated environmental claim.

Weeks 9–10: Test a small live event with a manual override and documented responsibility. Weeks 11–12: Review the results with operations, finance, and sustainability staff. Decide whether to scale, redesign, or stop. The timetable is a suggested workflow, not a universal implementation standard. Large or hazardous processes need a longer engineering and safety review.

17. Wider effects on the grid and society

When many businesses move demand away from a stressed hour, the system may need less costly peaking capacity and can use existing network assets more effectively. Flexible demand may also absorb some renewable generation that would otherwise be curtailed. These benefits depend on the location and timing of the response. A factory reducing load on an unconstrained feeder may deliver less network value than one reducing demand at a local bottleneck.

There are distributional questions. Large businesses may have meters, controls, and staff that make market participation easy. Small firms may face high setup costs relative to expected earnings. Aggregators can reduce those barriers, but their fees and contract terms matter. A fair programme should make payments and risks clear and should not shift grid costs onto customers who cannot participate.

Grid operators also need confidence that flexible demand will arrive when called. If every participant responds to the same low-price signal, a new peak can form. Market design must coordinate schedules and account for local network limits. The long-term goal is a reliable power system with more choices, rather than a promise that demand response can solve every shortage.

18. How to judge success without overclaiming

Report delivered capacity in kW and the length of each event. Report energy shifted and energy saved separately. Report financial value after control, labour, production, fees, and penalties. Report emissions using a stated method, time resolution, and geographic boundary. Include the baseline and any rebound.

Use a comparison period that accounts for weather, production volume, and maintenance. A quiet production day is not evidence of a successful demand-response event. If the operator pays for a reduction, document the difference between the estimated baseline and actual load. If the firm claims emissions savings, state which generators were assumed to respond and how uncertain that estimate is.

For management, the most useful question is whether the process remains safe and profitable under weaker prices, fewer events, and higher costs. For public policy, the question is whether payments buy real system value without rewarding artificial baselines. Both require independent data and clear reporting.

19. Ten lessons to retain

  1. Power in kW and energy in kWh are different measures.
  2. Flexible demand changes timing or level of use; it need not save energy.
  3. Process safety and product quality define the usable flexibility.
  4. Measure the load before selecting equipment or signing a contract.
  5. Tariff savings, demand-charge savings, and grid payments have different rules.
  6. A credible baseline is necessary to verify a paid reduction.
  7. Rebound can erase an apparent energy saving while peak relief remains useful.
  8. Low-price hours are not always low-emission hours.
  9. Contract penalties, labour, and control costs belong in the business case.
  10. Test real performance and uncertainty before scaling the programme.

Five knowledge-check questions and answers

1. What is the difference between shifting and saving electricity?

Shifting moves consumption to another time. Saving reduces total kWh. A shift can help the grid even if annual kWh stays the same.

2. Why is a baseline needed for demand-response payments?

The operator must estimate what the customer would have used without the event. Payment depends on the measured difference, so an inaccurate baseline can create false rewards.

3. Why might the cheapest hour produce more emissions?

Electricity prices and marginal emissions depend on which generators respond. In some markets a cheap coal plant may set the marginal supply, so a price-based shift can increase emissions.

4. What is the first safety test for a flexible factory load?

Confirm the process can move or pause without breaching equipment, product, worker, or delivery limits. A grid signal must never override a safety interlock.

5. What must a business include in the net financial return?

Tariff and market payments, extra energy, labour, controls, maintenance, fees, expected penalties, lost output, and investment costs. Gross tariff savings alone are incomplete.
