Can Cheap Solar Power Make an Electricity System Cheap?

Evidence-led study · 17 October 2026

Executive summary

Solar power can be cheap during sunny hours, yet an electricity system must meet demand at every hour and location. Grids, storage, demand response and backup generation all affect the delivered cost. The IEA's Renewables 2024 assessment describes increasing curtailment when networks and flexibility do not keep pace with new wind and solar plants. Curtailment means that available generation cannot be used. Cheap panels therefore do not guarantee low bills or reliable electricity. This study separates plant costs from whole-system costs and uses a simplified project model to explain production, curtailment and annual revenue. It also examines the opposing priorities of fast buildout and system readiness. The core business lesson is to model when and where clean electricity has value, as well as how much a plant can generate annually.

Foundations: power and energy

Power measures a rate and is commonly expressed in megawatts (MW). Energy is the amount produced or used over time and is measured in megawatt-hours (MWh). A 1 MW plant delivering full output for one hour generates 1 MWh. Solar panels cannot sustain full nameplate output through the night or in every weather condition. Capacity factor compares actual generation with the maximum possible at full rated power. The difference between capacity and energy explains why installed megawatts cannot alone establish reliable supply.

Mechanism: timing, wires and storage

Grid operators must balance electricity supply with demand continuously. Many solar panels generate most at the same time, often lowering daytime wholesale prices. If transmission is weak or supply exceeds what the system can take, operators may curtail available output. Batteries move some electricity from one hour to another but cost money and lose energy during charging and discharge. Demand response shifts flexible electricity use, such as industrial cooling, to periods with abundant supply. The best solution varies by location and load.

Evidence: growth and constraints

The IEA's Renewables 2024 report projected rapidly growing renewable electricity production and warned that system integration needs investment. It reported examples of wind and solar curtailment in Chile, Ireland and the United Kingdom reaching about 5–15%; these are selected cases rather than a global average. The IEA's Global Energy Review 2025 reported that low-emissions sources met nearly all of the global increase in electricity demand in 2024, yet energy-related carbon emissions still rose. Both progress and constraints are real.

Worked example — illustrative

A 10 MW solar plant with a 20% annual capacity factor produces about 10 × 8,760 × 0.20 = 17,520 MWh before curtailment. An 8% reduction leaves approximately 16,118 MWh delivered. At ₹3 per kWh, annual gross sales are about ₹4.84 crore. Subtract ₹0.65 crore in assumed operating costs to get ₹4.19 crore before debt, tax, depreciation and other expenses. This is an illustration, not an investment forecast. More curtailment, lower midday prices or higher financing costs can change the outcome sharply.

Competing views and business response

Rapid solar expansion can cut fuel use and emissions, while critics warn that grid bottlenecks and the lack of flexibility can waste output. A delay in generation prolongs dependence on fossil fuels; a delay in networks leaves new clean production underused. Project developers should model hourly generation, hourly selling prices, transmission limits, contract terms and finance. Decision-makers should compare delivered clean energy and reliability, not only installed capacity. Storage should be compared with alternative ways to shift load or improve grids.

Key lessons and knowledge check

Key lessons: MW differs from MWh; output depends on capacity factor; electricity has a time and location value; curtailment reduces revenue; financing and flexibility matter. Questions: What is an MWh? Why can midday prices fall? What causes curtailment? What is the example's yearly output before curtailment? What costs remain after gross sales? Answers: power multiplied by time; simultaneous supply; network and demand limits; 17,520 MWh; operations, finance, tax and other charges.
