Why Does Economic Growth Fail to Measure a Good Life?

Evidence-led study · 17 October 2026

Executive summary

Gross domestic product measures the value of final production, not every condition of human well-being. A country can produce more while rent, pollution or insecurity worsen. The OECD's How's Life? 2024 report therefore studies income, health, work, housing, environment, safety, social ties and trust, as well as resources needed by future generations. This is not an argument to discard GDP. The issue is to choose measures that fit the policy question and show differences between groups. A single figure makes comparison easy but hides important value judgments; a dashboard makes many conditions visible but is harder to summarise. This study explains how to compare economic flows, lasting resources and lived outcomes without pretending that complex social goals can always be reduced to one number.

Background and definitions

GDP became central to national accounts during the twentieth century. It measures production through an agreed statistical system. It does not fully measure unpaid care, personal freedom, environmental quality or inequality in the conditions people face. A flow describes activity over a time period. A stock is a resource available at a point in time. GDP is a flow, while forests, skills and infrastructure are stocks. A society may increase this year's production while depleting resources important for future welfare.

Mechanism: constructing better measures

First identify the policy goal: healthier lives, better housing or broader opportunity. Then select measures that directly describe the goal. OECD's well-being framework includes present outcomes and four resources for the future: economic, natural, human and social capital. A useful public report includes the average, changes over time and differences between groups. Income growth concentrated in one group can coexist with stagnation elsewhere. Health may improve while pollution worsens. Multiple dimensions prevent one positive trend from concealing other losses.

Evidence and its limits

OECD How's Life? 2024 reported that around 48% of people across OECD countries trusted national government in 2022–23. General interpersonal trust averaged 6.1 on a 0–10 scale in 2023. These measures come from surveys, and survey wording and context affect comparison. They do not prove that growth causes trust to change. Objective data also have limits: a housing price index may not capture space, condition or commute time. The strongest assessment uses several relevant series and states how they were collected.

Worked example — illustrative

Suppose a city records 6% output growth. Median rent rises 12%, a typical commute lengthens from 40 to 52 minutes, and local air quality worsens. The GDP figure accurately reports more production but does not answer whether most residents are better off. The three changes use different units. Combining them requires explicit assumptions about value and distribution. A practical evaluation asks who gained income, who paid more rent, and how health or time costs changed.

Competing views and implications

A broad dashboard is transparent but can overwhelm decision-makers. A single weighted index is easy to compare but hides which values received priority. Neither approach eliminates choices about fairness. Governments should publish underlying measures and disclose weights if they combine them. Hospital policy needs measures of preventable illness and care quality, rather than spending alone. Education policy needs learning and participation, rather than only the number of schools built. The right indicator depends on the problem being solved.

Key lessons and knowledge check

Key lessons: GDP is a production measure; stock depletion can damage the future; averages can hide unequal outcomes; surveys need careful interpretation; indices embed value judgments. Questions: How do flows differ from stocks? Why show group differences? Can higher GDP coexist with lower well-being? What does an index weight do? What should a hospital measure? Answers: time-period activity versus accumulated resources; to reveal distribution; yes; it defines relative importance; health outcomes and care quality.
