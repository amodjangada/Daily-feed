Who Pays for India's Instant Payment Network?

Evidence-led study · 17 October 2026

Executive summary

Unified Payments Interface makes payments easy for users, but banks, apps and payment infrastructure still incur costs. A zero merchant discount rate does not mean that transactions are free to operate. The cost may be carried by banks, government support or revenues elsewhere. NPCI reported 24.51 billion UPI transactions in August 2026 worth about ₹29.82 lakh crore. At this scale, a small charge can generate large amounts, yet it can also discourage some merchants. Payment policy therefore concerns funding, access, competition and reliability. The important question is who ultimately bears the economic cost, not just which organisation is formally charged. This study explains the network, considers the arguments for fees and subsidies, and shows how to test an illustrative funding model.

Background: shared payment infrastructure

UPI is operated by the National Payments Corporation of India and lets participating banks and apps exchange payment instructions under common rules. Interoperability means a payer using one app can send money to a recipient using another participating service. This common network reduces the need for separate closed payment systems. But network effects also mean reliability and broad acceptance matter: each additional user or merchant increases the usefulness of the service for others.

How a transaction works

A payer starts a payment through an app. The banks and network verify the instruction, route it and record the result under applicable rules. The payer and recipient receive status messages. Behind this simple flow are costs for secure access, hosting, fraud checks, dispute handling, customer support and resilience. A published merchant discount rate is one possible payment mechanism. A fee charged to a merchant can also affect consumer prices. The final burden depends on market competition and the ability to change prices.

Evidence and policy uncertainty

NPCI's August 2026 statistics list 24,508.96 million transactions and ₹29,82,355.95 crore in value. This is operational scale, not a published estimate of industry profit or network operating cost. Debate over possible MDR adjustments should not be mistaken for a confirmed universal new charge without an official decision. Policymakers need credible per-transaction costs, information about failures and fraud, and evidence about how merchants respond to charges. Transaction volume alone cannot choose a fee.

Worked example — illustrative

Assume there are 20 billion merchant transactions in a month and the service costs ₹0.12 per transaction on average. The bill is ₹240 crore. A proposed fee of ₹0.20 on one quarter of the transactions yields ₹100 crore, before the cost of fee collection. There is still a ₹140 crore gap to fund. These numbers are hypothetical. A percentage charge would have different effects on large and small payments. A targeted subsidy could support small merchants, but it must have a funding source.

Competing perspectives

One view favours minimal charges to preserve broad acceptance, reduce cash handling and support small firms. Another argues that reliable service requires a predictable way to recover expenses and fund better fraud protection. Both points matter. Permanent subsidies move costs elsewhere, while universal fees can hurt low-margin businesses. Market concentration among consumer-facing apps adds competition concerns. Sound policy tests both the direct price response and the indirect gains from a trusted network.

Key lessons and knowledge check

Key lessons: zero price is not zero cost; interoperable networks create shared value; unit charges matter at scale; cost incidence may differ from formal payment; merchant response must be measured. Questions: Who operates UPI? What does interoperability mean? Why can a zero fee still be costly? How large is the example's funding gap? What should an MDR pilot monitor? Answers: NPCI; compatible providers; infrastructure costs remain; ₹140 crore; adoption, reliability, costs and competition.
