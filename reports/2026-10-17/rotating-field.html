Why Does Antibiotic Resistance Keep Growing?

Evidence-led study · 17 October 2026

Executive summary

Antibiotic resistance occurs when bacteria carry traits that reduce the effect of an antibiotic. The bacteria, rather than the patient, are resistant. Treatment can select bacteria with those traits: susceptible organisms decline while survivors may reproduce and spread. Resistance also spreads through contact, health systems, water, animals and transferred genes. This means the issue cannot be solved by prescribing guidance alone. Infection prevention, clean water, laboratory tests, access to appropriate treatment and new medicines all have roles. Global health studies estimate a large burden, but exact totals depend on surveillance and models; they are not direct counts of every case. This study explains the biological mechanism, distinguishes deaths associated with resistance from deaths attributable to it, and considers the tension between reducing unnecessary drug use and ensuring access to lifesaving treatment.

Background and biological terms

Antibiotics changed treatment of bacterial infections during the twentieth century. Bacteria are small living organisms that can reproduce rapidly. Random mutations and acquired genes can change their response to medicines. Resistant traits may make a drug less effective by breaking it down, changing its target or limiting its entry. A plasmid is a small DNA element that can carry genes between bacteria. Resistance can therefore spread both when a bacterial strain multiplies and when useful genes pass to other organisms.

How selection works

A population contains variation before treatment. An antibiotic reduces susceptible cells, while a resistant group may survive. If the survivors continue reproducing, resistance can become more common. This is natural selection: no bacterium needs to plan or choose. Hospitals, sanitation, travel and contact then influence spread. A resistant infection can rise because of transmission even where local prescribing is careful. The system has at least two controls: reduce unnecessary selection and reduce infection spread.

Reading the evidence correctly

Global burden estimates combine laboratory data, hospital records and statistical models. Deaths associated with resistance include situations where a resistant infection was present. Deaths attributable to resistance estimate the difference against a counterfactual in which the infection was susceptible. Those figures are not identical. Countries also have unequal laboratory coverage. Limited surveillance does not prove low local resistance. WHO's GLASS programme supports more consistent monitoring, while published global studies model gaps and uncertainties.

Worked example — illustrative

Suppose 1,000 bacteria consist of 990 susceptible and 10 resistant organisms. Under one hypothetical antibiotic exposure, 99% of susceptible bacteria die and 10% of resistant bacteria die. About 10 susceptible and 9 resistant bacteria remain. Resistant organisms have risen from 1% of the starting population to roughly 47% of survivors. This is a teaching example, not a treatment prediction. The drug did not intentionally create the resistance; it changed which traits survived. Proper treatment remains necessary for many serious infections.

Competing priorities and wider effects

Drug stewardship aims to avoid unnecessary use and choose effective treatment. But aggressive restrictions can cause harm when people lack timely diagnosis or necessary antibiotics. New medicines are important, yet firms may find investment difficult because careful use limits sales. Vaccination, clean water and infection prevention reduce demand for antibiotics in the first place. Health systems need surveillance, diagnostics, appropriate access and research together. Viral illness alone is not a reason to use antibacterial medicines.

Key lessons and knowledge check

Key lessons: resistance is a property of bacteria; selection changes trait prevalence; genes can move between bacteria; surveillance gaps distort comparisons; access and stewardship must coexist. Questions: Who becomes resistant? What is a plasmid? Why can resistance rise during treatment? What distinguishes attributable and associated death estimates? Why is infection prevention part of the solution? Answers: bacteria; DNA that may carry traits; selective survival; causal counterfactual versus presence; fewer infections require fewer treatments.
