How Do We Read a Script That Nobody Alive Understands?

Why the Rosetta Stone and Linear B yielded their meanings, while the Indus signs remain unread.

Executive summary

How can scholars read a text when no living person knows its language? A successful decipherment is a testable account of how signs represent words, sounds or other information. It must explain many inscriptions, including material that the researcher did not use to invent the reading. The Rosetta Stone helped scholars connect Egyptian hieroglyphs to a known Greek decree. Linear B was solved without a bilingual text, through repeated sign patterns, grammatical changes and tests against ancient Greek. The Indus signs remain undeciphered because their inscriptions are short, the language is unknown and no accepted bilingual key has been found. Even whether all these signs record speech remains contested. This guide follows the evidence from object to sign, word and historical meaning. It explains why a plausible translation proves little, how scholars test competing readings, and what computers can and cannot do. Two illustrative calculations show how useful patterns can exist without known meaning. The main rule is simple: a reading earns trust when it predicts new evidence.

1. What does it mean to decipher writing?

A language is a system of words and grammar. A script is a system of signs that can record language. The same language can use different scripts, and a script can record different languages. Some symbols identify goods, people or offices without recording complete sentences. These distinctions matter when we meet unfamiliar marks on an ancient object.

To decipher a language-writing system, researchers need to show what its signs do. Some signs represent sounds, some syllables, some whole words, and some provide clues about a word's class. A fish-shaped sign may stand for the word fish, a sound linked to that word, or something else entirely. Its picture alone does not tell us.

There are levels of progress. Scholars might first determine writing direction and recurring signs. They might then propose values for some signs or identify numbers. Full decipherment requires consistent readings across many texts, grammar and a language that fits the period. New inscriptions must provide a fair test. This is why a single attractive translation does not settle a case.

2. Terms that make the evidence clear

Corpus
The set of recorded inscriptions, with their photographs and context.
Grapheme
A distinct written sign. Damage and changes in style make counting difficult.
Phonetic sign
A sign used for a speech sound or set of sounds.
Logogram
A sign representing a word or meaningful unit.
Syllabary
A script in which many signs represent syllables, such as pa or ti.
Cartouche
An oval around a royal name in Egyptian writing.
Bilingual inscription
A text in two languages or scripts that scholars can compare.
Inflection
A change to a word's form that can mark grammar.
Conditional entropy
A measure of uncertainty about a sign when earlier signs are known. It measures structure, not meaning.

These are working categories, not fixed boxes. Egyptian hieroglyphs combine several sign functions. A seal can combine an animal image with a line of signs. The image and signs need separate analysis before their relationship can be understood.

3. A short history of lost and recovered scripts

Egyptian hieroglyphs were used for many centuries, but the ability to read them declined as writing practices and institutions changed. For a long time, many European scholars treated them mainly as symbolic pictures. That approach missed their use to record speech. In 1799, French soldiers found the Rosetta Stone near Rashid in Egypt. It carried a decree from 196 BCE for King Ptolemy V in hieroglyphic, Demotic and Greek. Greek could be read. That gave scholars a strong point of comparison.

Thomas Young made progress matching signs in royal names. Jean-François Champollion drew on Coptic, a later form of Egyptian, and other monuments. He announced his breakthrough in 1822. His insight that hieroglyphs also represented sounds opened a wider programme of reading and testing. The stone was a key, but it was not a ready-made dictionary.

Linear B had no comparable Greek translation beside its signs. Clay tablets from Bronze Age Greece and Crete showed repeated groups and quantities. Alice Kober studied systematic changes in signs. Michael Ventris proposed in 1952 that the script recorded Greek. John Chadwick helped test and develop the result. The Indus signs, found on seals and other objects from South Asia, remain a much harder case.

4. Why the Rosetta Stone worked

The Rosetta Stone is broken. Its three sections are versions of the same decree, rather than exact word-for-word lines. The readable Greek version nevertheless gave scholars names, events and repeated ideas to seek in the Egyptian sections. Royal names enclosed in cartouches were particularly useful. A proposed reading for Ptolemy could be checked against other names and other monuments.

Young identified signs that represented sounds in foreign names. Champollion went further. He used Coptic vocabulary and evidence from names of Egyptian rulers to show that sound signs were not limited to foreign names. This explained far more of the system than a theory based only on pictures. Some signs also acted as word signs or classifiers. The script was mixed, which is why a simple one-sign-to-one-letter table would not work.

The important point is the method. Each new value had to explain more than the example that suggested it. A reading supported by Greek comparison, Coptic and additional inscriptions is much stronger than a reading based on visual resemblance. The British Museum's object record, its 2022 exhibition guide and the Smithsonian's September 2022 history document this process.

5. How Linear B was read without a Rosetta Stone

Linear B was mainly used for administrative records. Tablets list goods, people, animals and quantities. This made repeated patterns useful. Alice Kober observed that some groups of signs had related endings. Such changes could indicate grammatical inflection. She analysed these relations without first assigning sounds to the signs. That reduced the range of possible explanations.

Ventris developed sign grids and tested possible place names. In 1952 he proposed that the underlying language was an early form of Greek, contrary to some earlier expectations. When he assigned sound values, more groups began to resemble Greek words and grammar. Chadwick, a Greek-language specialist at Cambridge, helped test the results and explain unusual older forms.

A powerful test came from a Pylos tablet studied in 1953. It listed vessels and included drawings. The proposed reading produced ti-ri-po-de, related to the Greek word for tripods, beside relevant vessel information. This tablet was not part of the evidence Ventris used to announce his initial reading in 1952. Cambridge's account and Chadwick's recollections explain why the match mattered: the reading predicted information on an independent object. It was more than a clever guess about a familiar inscription.

6. The steps of a reliable decipherment

Researchers begin with the object. They photograph it, record the excavation site and archaeological layer, and mark damage. They must distinguish a broken stroke from a real sign. They also need consistent rules for deciding when two similar shapes are variants of one sign. Without that work, even the sign count can be wrong.

Next they compare inscriptions. Which signs begin or end a sequence? Which groups recur beside numbers or drawings? Do changes in sign groups match changes in the objects or quantities? Does the evidence support a writing direction? These questions reveal structure before anyone claims to know a word.

Researchers can then propose sound values, grammar or a language. Each proposal needs a test on different material. It should explain common and rare signs, not only a few selected examples. It must fit the date and place of the objects. A rival hypothesis should be compared under the same rules. If many translations fit equally well, the evidence has not selected a winner. A rigorous study reports uncertainty and failures as well as successful matches.

7. Diagram: the evidence path

Full corpora are essential. If a researcher selects only inscriptions that fit, almost any story can seem correct. Other scholars must be able to use the same sign values and rules on texts that were not chosen to support the theory.

8. What survives from the Indus civilisation?

The mature Indus civilisation flourished around 2600–1900 BCE in parts of present-day Pakistan and northwestern India. Many seals, tablets and pottery pieces carry short sign sequences. A seal held by the Metropolitan Museum of Art shows an animal and signs above it. The museum records that the script has not been fully deciphered. The animal provides context, but it does not tell us the words represented by the signs.

Asko Parpola's 2011 scholarly review described a corpus of roughly 3,000 inscriptions then considered, averaging about five signs, with no bilingual text. Other researchers cite larger counts because catalogues differ in which objects they include. Counts of distinct signs also differ because scholars disagree about variants. The reliable conclusion is that there are thousands of objects, yet very few long continuous texts.

The language is unknown. A Dravidian-language hypothesis has serious scholarly supporters, but alternative proposals exist and none is established. A guessed word that seems to match an animal or object is not enough. Without longer text, grammar and repeated sound values are hard to test. A long inscription or bilingual object with secure archaeological context would make a major difference.

9. Do the Indus signs even record speech?

This is a genuine scholarly disagreement. In 2004, Steve Farmer, Richard Sproat and Michael Witzel argued that the short inscriptions and other features are better explained by a nonlinguistic system of symbols than by full language-writing. Their argument challenges an assumption built into many decipherment attempts: that every sign sequence must encode spoken words.

Rajesh Rao and colleagues took a different approach. In a Science paper published online on 23 April 2009, they reported that the conditional entropy of Indus sign sequences resembled natural languages more closely than several comparison systems. A 19 March 2010 PLOS ONE study by Nisha Yadav and colleagues found structured sign order, including differences between signs that start and end inscriptions. These results are consistent with a rule-governed system and may support language encoding.

Neither side has produced a universally accepted decipherment. A structured symbol system can have rules without writing complete sentences. A writing system used mainly on seals can also leave unusually short texts. Statistical results depend on the comparison data and the treatment of sign variants. The best response is to keep both the structure and the unresolved meaning in view, then ask what new evidence would distinguish the hypotheses.

10. What computers and AI can really do

Computers can count signs, compare sequences, estimate which signs tend to follow others and find likely errors in a damaged inscription. An n-gram model predicts a sign from the signs before it. The 2010 PLOS ONE research used such models and evaluated them against held-out inscriptions. These methods can find useful patterns that are difficult to see by hand.

But prediction is not translation. Software might learn that a certain mark usually follows another mark in a catalogue. That does not show that the marks spell a sentence. A language model can also produce fluent English from uncertain signs, but fluency is not evidence that the proposed meaning is true.

Strong computational work uses an open corpus, clear rules for damaged signs, separate training and test material, and comparisons with alternative models. It reports uncertainty. With short inscriptions, a small change in how sign variants are grouped can alter results. AI can organise data and suggest testable hypotheses; independent linguistic and archaeological evidence must still establish a reading.

11. Worked example one: structure without meaning

Imagine an illustrative collection of 100 five-sign seals. One sign occurs at the end of 60 inscriptions but at the start of only 5. The observed end rate is 60 ÷ 100 = 60%. The start rate is 5 ÷ 100 = 5%. The end-to-start frequency ratio is 60 ÷ 5 = 12. The sign seems to have a strong position preference in this invented collection.

What does that tell us? It might be a suffix, title marker, fixed closing symbol or part of an administrative formula. The counts alone cannot select one meaning. We would need to check the sign in middle positions, compare other signs and repeat the test across sites and object types.

This separates syntax, the order of signs, from semantics, their meaning. A pattern is evidence about structure. It is not a dictionary entry. These numbers are invented for teaching and are not measured Indus data.

12. Worked example two: testing a proposed reading

Suppose an illustrative decipherment predicts the type of record on 18 of 20 new tablets. Its accuracy is 18 ÷ 20 = 90%. That looks good. But imagine 16 of those 20 tablets are inventory records. A simple rule that always predicts 'inventory' would score 16 ÷ 20 = 80%. The proposed system improves on that baseline by 10 percentage points.

This is useful evidence, but not proof of a language. Were the tablets really new? Were the categories set before the test? Could another team reproduce the result? Do the words match objects and grammar? A sample of 20 tablets also gives an uncertain estimate.

Linear B's vessel tablet offered several kinds of agreement at once: proposed sound values, words, drawings and numerical context. That combination is stronger than one score. The numbers here are invented and do not describe the actual Linear B tests.

This comparison ranks the available evidence, not the complexity of the civilisations. A society can have complex cities, trade and administration without leaving long surviving texts. Missing texts may reflect materials, preservation or the purposes for which signs were used.

14. Why false decipherments can seem convincing

A frequent error is to begin with a desired conclusion, then assign signs to words that support it. A symbol that resembles an animal or a modern letter can seem meaningful, but resemblance is weak evidence. Short inscriptions leave room for many possible readings. A researcher can make a translation look convincing by choosing only the examples that fit.

Another problem is changing sign values whenever a text fails. A theory with unlimited exceptions cannot be tested. Language comparisons also require care. Modern languages differ from their ancient forms, and similar sounds can occur by chance. The proposed language must fit the time, region and archaeological record.

Responsible claims publish a sign list, transcription rules, proposed values, failed cases and predictions. They let independent specialists test the rules on new objects. A proposal can be interesting before it passes these tests, but it should be called a hypothesis, not a solved script.

15. Archaeology constrains what words can mean

A text is part of an object. A stamp seal may mark ownership, status, a commodity or an official role. Its wear, excavation layer and repeated impressions can help identify its use. Signs found across several sites may reveal shared practices or trade. These facts constrain possible readings, but do not translate the signs by themselves.

The Rosetta Stone is a political and religious decree. It was made under Ptolemaic rule and later reused before its discovery in 1799. Its transfer to Britain and the present debate over cultural property are part of the object's history, though distinct from the technical process of reading hieroglyphs.

For the Indus world, seals, weights, city plans and trade evidence help researchers ask better questions. A claim that a five-sign seal contains a long royal narrative needs a strong explanation. A claim that every seal is a merchant label must explain signs on other kinds of objects. Context cannot replace language analysis, but it can expose implausible interpretations.

16. What could change the Indus problem?

A long, well-dated inscription would provide more chances to identify repeated words and grammar. A bilingual object that pairs Indus signs with a known language would be especially valuable, although scholars would still need to prove that the versions correspond. A securely excavated seal with unusually clear administrative context could also help.

Better public catalogues are another route. Researchers need high-quality images, consistent records of damage and transparent decisions about sign variants. Digitising objects does not itself decipher them, but it makes independent tests easier. Even a reliable partial reading of numbers, names or goods would be important if it worked across many objects.

For now, the responsible conclusion is that the Indus signs remain undeciphered. This is a statement about available evidence, not about the ability of the people who made the objects. Their urban and material achievements can be studied even while the inscriptions remain unread.

17. Ten lessons

  1. Script and language are different things.
  2. A picture-shaped sign does not necessarily name that picture.
  3. A bilingual text is a strong anchor but not a complete dictionary.
  4. Egyptian hieroglyphs combine sound and meaning signs.
  5. Linear B shows that a bilingual text is not always required.
  6. New inscriptions provide stronger tests than familiar ones.
  7. The Indus corpus contains many objects but few long texts.
  8. Statistics reveal sign patterns without proving meanings.
  9. Scholarly disagreement should be tested with shared evidence.
  10. Uncertainty is the correct conclusion when tests are insufficient.

18. Five questions and answers

1. Why did the Rosetta Stone help?

It gave scholars versions of one decree in hieroglyphic, Demotic and readable Greek. Names and repeated ideas could be compared and tested elsewhere.

2. What confirmed the Linear B reading?

The proposed Greek values worked across tablets, including independent records with vessel words and drawings.

3. Why are five-sign inscriptions difficult?

They give little grammatical context. Many different words or functions can fit the same short sequence.

4. Does language-like sign order prove language?

No. It shows structure compared with chosen reference systems. Structured nonlinguistic symbols can also have regular order.

5. What would count as real progress on the Indus signs?

A reproducible reading that predicts new inscriptions, ideally supported by longer text, a bilingual anchor or strong archaeological evidence.
