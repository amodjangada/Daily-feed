Why Do Groups Follow Rules Most Members Privately Dislike?

Why public compliance can hide private disagreement, and how social-norm research tests the gap.

Research checked 2 November 2026 · Original publication dates below · Both numeric examples are illustrative

Executive summary

A group can keep a rule even when many members privately disagree with it. This can happen because people mistake silence or public compliance for genuine approval. Psychologists call one form of this error pluralistic ignorance. It differs from the false-consensus effect, in which people overestimate how common their own views are. Original studies of campus drinking, household energy use, workplace choices and climate attitudes show that perceived norms can differ sharply from private beliefs or measured behavior. Some experiments find that correcting these beliefs changes later decisions. Other studies show that norm messages can fail or have unexpected effects, especially when group identity matters. This guide explains the difference between what people do, what they approve and what they think others approve. It traces the history of the research, describes the feedback process that can maintain an unpopular norm, and reviews evidence and limits. Two illustrative calculations show how to measure a perception gap and why public behavior can give a misleading estimate of private support. The practical lesson is to measure norms carefully before trying to change them.

1. The central question

A group meets every week. Most members find one rule wasteful, but nobody objects. Each person sees the others follow it and concludes that the rule is popular. The public pattern then continues. No one needs to deceive the group for this to happen. Each person can be responding to a mistaken estimate of what others believe.

The central question is: How can a social rule survive when many people privately want it to change? The answer requires separating private attitudes, public conduct and beliefs about other people's attitudes. These are three different measurements. A rule can also persist for rational reasons: formal penalties, unequal power, coordination costs or genuine support among a decisive minority. A careful explanation must test those possibilities rather than assume that every unpopular practice is a case of collective misunderstanding. This guide focuses on the cases in which wrong estimates of social approval help maintain a practice, and on what original experiments show about changing those estimates.

2. Define norms, beliefs and misperceptions

A descriptive norm is what people usually do. An injunctive norm is what people believe others approve or expect them to do. A private attitude is a person's own evaluation. A perceived norm is that person's estimate of the group's behavior or approval. A social sanction is a cost for breaking a rule, such as criticism or exclusion. A reference group is the set of people whose behavior or opinion matters to the person making a choice.

Pluralistic ignorance occurs when members systematically misunderstand what other members privately think, often because public behavior conceals disagreement. False consensus is a related but distinct bias: people may overestimate how many others share their own views. Ross, Greene and House demonstrated false-consensus patterns in four studies in 1977 [1]. Neither term means that all groups are secretly in agreement. A population may contain several genuine and competing norms. The research question is whether beliefs about those norms match the relevant evidence.

3. How the research developed

Social psychologists have long studied conformity, social influence and the gap between public and private judgments. The 1977 false-consensus studies showed that a person's own choice can bias estimates of how common that choice is [1]. In 1993, Prentice and Miller reported four studies of students' views about campus alcohol practices. Many students believed that their peers were more comfortable with the practices than they themselves were [2]. This is a classic pattern of pluralistic ignorance.

Later work moved from measuring mistaken beliefs to testing interventions. Schroeder and Prentice compared different discussion formats for entering students and followed up on drinking behavior [3]. Field studies then examined how messages about household energy use could improve or worsen behavior [4]. Research on work decisions in Saudi Arabia and global climate attitudes expanded the range of settings [5] [6]. These studies do not imply a single universal mechanism. They show why norms must be measured in context.

4. Step 1: observe public behavior

People often learn what a group expects by watching what others do. Public behavior is easy to see. Private attitudes are harder to see. If everyone follows a rule, an observer may infer that everyone approves of it. That inference can be wrong because people may comply to avoid penalties, preserve relationships or finish a task without conflict.

Descriptive norms can be informative. If a building's residents regularly sort waste correctly, a new resident may learn a useful local practice. But a visible pattern can also reflect limited choice. A person may attend a meeting because attendance is required, not because they find it useful. To study a norm, researchers should record the action and ask what options people actually had. A public action is evidence of behavior, not direct evidence of preference. This distinction matters for surveys, workplaces, schools and online communities, where the most visible people may be a small and unusual subset of the group.

5. Step 2: infer what others approve

After observing behavior, a person estimates what the group considers acceptable. That estimate can be shaped by loud members, repeated stories, leaders and social media. People may pay more attention to the few who speak strongly than to the many who stay silent. The estimate also depends on the reference group. 'Most people in the country' and 'most people in my team' can produce very different answers.

Prentice and Miller found that students' estimates of their peers' comfort with campus drinking differed from their own comfort [2]. The result is important because it concerns approval, not only how often students drank. If a student believes others approve of heavy drinking, that perceived approval can create pressure even when the student's private view is more cautious. Researchers should ask separately what respondents do, what they approve and what they think peers approve. Combining these questions into one measure can hide the mechanism and lead to the wrong intervention.

6. Step 3: choose silence or compliance

A person who thinks their view is unusual may decide that speaking up is costly. They may expect embarrassment, a loss of status or direct punishment. Silence can be a sensible short-term choice, even if the person dislikes the rule. The next person then sees one fewer objection and becomes more certain that the group approves. This is a feedback process: perceived approval changes behavior, and behavior then shapes perceived approval.

The feedback does not require that everyone be afraid. Some people may be indifferent. Others may care about harmony or have more urgent work. Still others may truly support the rule. These differences matter because the same visible silence can have several causes. A researcher must measure the private attitudes and the perceived attitudes, ideally before any group discussion changes them. A manager who treats silence as consent may unintentionally strengthen a false belief about what the team wants. A leader who treats every silence as hidden disagreement makes the opposite error.

7. Step 4: distinguish influence from agreement

Social influence can operate through information and through social pressure. A person may copy others because they think the others know something useful. They may also copy them because they want acceptance. These mechanisms can produce the same outward behavior but respond differently to new evidence. Accurate information can change the first mechanism. Private feedback, protection from sanctions or a different decision process may be needed for the second.

Consider the difference between asking 'How many colleagues use this process?' and 'How many colleagues believe this process should be required?' The first is a descriptive-norm question. The second measures perceived approval. If people use the process because it is mandatory, a message that many people use it adds little. If people privately dislike it but fear speaking, a safe and representative survey can reveal the disagreement. However, a survey alone cannot remove a real penalty for dissent. Norm correction and institutional reform are separate tools.

8. Step 5: test whether correcting beliefs changes action

The strongest evidence for a causal role of norm beliefs comes from experiments that change information while holding other conditions as similar as possible. Schroeder and Prentice compared a peer-focused discussion about mistaken campus norms with a discussion focused on individual decisions. Four to six months later, the peer-focused group reported less drinking in that study [3]. The result supports a role for norm perception, but the outcome was self-reported and the setting was one campus program.

Bursztyn, González and Yanagizawa-Drott studied young married men in Saudi Arabia. Many privately supported women working outside the home but underestimated other men's support. Correcting beliefs increased costly sign-up for a job-matching service; later follow-up found changes in wives' job-search actions [5]. The study connects a measured belief gap with behavior. Its social and legal setting is specific. It cannot be used to claim that the same message will change labor decisions in every country.

9. Step 6: distinguish a message from a change in norms

Giving people a statistic does not necessarily change a norm. The statistic may be irrelevant to their reference group, untrusted, or too abstract to affect a decision. A person may already know the information. They may also believe that a small but powerful minority will punish them even when most people privately agree. In that case, majority support does not remove the expected cost of dissent.

A useful intervention measures three stages. First, did people understand the message? Second, did their estimate of others' views change? Third, did their actual behavior change? These are different outcomes. A study that measures only immediate agreement with a message cannot prove a lasting change in conduct. Follow-up periods and independent behavioral records improve the test. Researchers should also watch for unwanted effects, including a message that makes an undesirable behavior seem more common or normal than participants previously believed.

10. Diagram: how a mistaken norm can persist

The loop is useful because it identifies places to test an intervention. An anonymous survey can reveal private attitudes. A well-designed group discussion can test perceived approval. A change in procedure can reduce the cost of dissent. But a diagram does not show which cause is dominant in a real group. Researchers need observations and, where possible, controlled tests. The aim is to distinguish a wrong belief from a real constraint before recommending a change.

A strong study uses comparable wording and a clearly defined reference group. It reports who answered, when the survey took place and how respondents were selected. Anonymous responses may reduce pressure, but anonymity cannot remove all social-desirability bias. A respondent can still give the answer they think is respectable. In addition, people's views can change between the measurement of private attitudes and the measurement of perceived norms. These are reasons to interpret a perception gap as evidence that needs care, not as an exact count of hidden dissent.

12. Evidence from a global climate survey

Andre, Boneva, Chopra and Falk surveyed nearly 130,000 people in 125 countries through the Gallup World Poll in 2021–2022. Their paper appeared on 9 February 2024 [6]. They reported population-weighted estimates: 69% expressed willingness to contribute 1% of household income each month to fight global warming, 86% supported a pro-climate social norm, and 89% wanted stronger government action. The researchers also found that people systematically underestimated others' willingness to contribute.

This is evidence of a large perception gap in a defined survey setting. It does not show that 69% actually paid 1% of their income. Stated willingness and completed payment are different outcomes. Nor does it mean that every country had the same level of support. The paper reports substantial cross-country variation. Its contribution is to measure both stated private views and beliefs about other people's views across many places. That makes the misperception visible, while leaving open how much behavior would change if the gap were corrected.

13. Why accurate descriptive norms can still backfire

Schultz and colleagues tested messages about neighborhood household electricity use in a 2007 field experiment [4]. A descriptive message about average consumption encouraged some high-using households to reduce use. But households that were already below the average could move in the wrong direction, a boomerang effect. Adding a signal of approval or disapproval helped prevent that unwanted response in their study.

The lesson is about message design, not a universal rule that descriptive norms are harmful. If a campaign says 'many people waste energy,' it may accidentally make waste seem normal. If it says that careful use is approved, it communicates a different kind of norm. The result also shows why a group average can be a poor target for everyone. People above and below the average may respond in opposite ways. A serious evaluation should report subgroup outcomes rather than only a single mean change. It should also measure actual use when possible, not only what people say they intend to do.

14. Why group identity changes the response

Norm messages depend on whose behavior they describe. Rand and Yoeli reported experiments in 2024 showing an unusual response in a polarized setting [7]. In their US masking studies, telling Biden supporters that many Trump supporters did not plan to wear masks increased the Biden supporters' stated masking intentions. The authors examined how the information affected perceived judgments from the participants' own group. In a separate, nonpolarized task, they found a more conventional pattern.

This does not show that norm messages should generally highlight the conduct of an opposing group. The masking studies concerned intentions in a specific political and public-health context, and the design used selected norm information from an earlier pilot. The result demonstrates that a message can change how people think their own group will judge them. Reference groups and identity therefore matter. A program that copies a successful message into another setting may produce a smaller effect, no effect or an unexpected response.

15. Illustrative calculation 1: measure the perception gap

All numbers in this example are invented. An anonymous survey of 200 employees asks whether a weekly reporting rule should remain compulsory. Of those surveyed, 140 say no. Thus 140 ÷ 200 = 70% privately oppose the rule. A separate question asks each person what percentage of colleagues want the rule removed. The average estimate is 35%. The perception gap is 70 − 35 = 35 percentage points.

The result suggests that employees underestimate opposition. It does not prove that 70% of all employees oppose the rule unless the sample represents the workforce. It also does not tell us why the rule survives. Some staff may fear criticism; others may accept a rule they dislike because it helps another team. The next step is to test the source of the gap and the costs of changing the process. Reporting both the actual estimate and the perceived estimate is more useful than saying 'most people are unhappy' without evidence.

16. Illustrative calculation 2: public behavior hides preference

All numbers in this example are invented. In a group of 100 people, 80 attend a required event. A manager assumes that attendance means approval and reports 80% support. A confidential survey finds that only 30 attendees approve, while 50 attended mainly because of a formal requirement. If the 20 non-attendees also disapprove, total approval is 30%, even though attendance is 80%.

The 50-percentage-point difference comes from measuring different things. Attendance answers 'who went?' Approval answers 'who wanted this?' The example does not imply that a confidential survey is automatically accurate. People may misunderstand the question or answer strategically. It shows why a visible majority is not sufficient evidence of an approving majority. When leaders want to know whether a rule should continue, they should measure approval directly and also ask about the rule's benefits, costs and distributional effects.

17. What the evidence cannot prove

Most norm studies concern a specific population, behavior and time. A campus drinking intervention cannot by itself predict the result of a workplace policy campaign. A national survey of stated willingness cannot establish completed actions. A short experiment on intentions may not predict behavior months later. Even a randomized intervention can have spillovers if participants talk to one another. These limits do not make the studies useless. They define the claims that the data can support.

Survey wording is another risk. Asking whether people approve of a broad goal can produce different results from asking whether they will pay a specific cost. Respondents may also fear that their answers will be seen, especially in small groups. Some beliefs are genuinely diverse, and the average can hide sharp divisions. A credible study reports uncertainty, subgroup variation and the full wording of key questions. It avoids assuming that a group is secretly united because a few respondents were surprised by a survey result.

18. Competing explanations for an unpopular rule

Pluralistic ignorance is one possible explanation. A second is coordination: people may dislike a rule but prefer it to several incompatible alternatives. A third is power: a small group may enforce the rule despite majority opposition. A fourth is preference intensity: a minority may care strongly while a majority cares only a little. A fifth is that the rule has benefits that are difficult to see from an individual viewpoint. These explanations can coexist.

One approach to reform is to publish reliable information about private attitudes. Another is to change the formal incentives or the decision process. The right intervention depends on what the evidence shows. If people already know the majority dislikes a rule but expect real punishment, more survey data will not solve the problem. If the main barrier is a false belief about approval, a credible correction may help. An ethical intervention should not use selective statistics to manufacture a preferred norm. It should give accurate context and allow genuine disagreement to remain visible.

19. Ethics: information, pressure and consent

Norm correction can help people make independent choices, but it can also become a tool of manipulation. A leader may present a selected sample as if it represents the whole group. A campaign may use a majority statistic to pressure a minority to conform. Even an accurate majority view does not establish that the majority is morally right. Rights, safety and evidence still matter. A norm is a description of social expectations, not a complete ethical argument.

Good practice starts with voluntary, private data collection and clear statements about the sample. Report how many people responded and what was asked. Protect minority views and avoid revealing individual responses. Explain the difference between the survey result and any proposed policy. People should be able to disagree without penalty. In high-stakes settings, independent oversight can help separate research from advocacy. A change in perceived norms should be judged by its effect on welfare and autonomy, not only by whether it produces more compliance.

20. Wider effects on organizations and public life

Perception gaps matter in organizations because silence is common in meetings. They matter in education because students observe peer behavior before they know peer attitudes. They matter in public policy because citizens may underestimate support for a shared goal or misread the strength of opposition. Online platforms can amplify a small number of active voices, making them appear more representative than they are. But digital visibility is not a population survey.

Institutions can improve decisions by separating opinion measurement from public performance. Anonymous surveys, careful sampling, structured discussion and published decision rules can make hidden disagreement easier to detect. These tools have limits. A survey cannot replace a formal vote when a vote is required, and a vote cannot reveal every reason behind a choice. A healthy process allows people to update beliefs after seeing accurate information, then decide whether the rule should change. The aim is better collective knowledge and fairer choices, not automatic conformity to a new majority.

21. How to test a proposed norm intervention

Begin with a defined reference group and a clear behavioral outcome. Measure private attitudes, perceived descriptive norms and perceived injunctive norms separately. Use a representative sample and protect respondents. If a perception gap appears, test whether a truthful correction changes the belief. Where appropriate and ethical, compare the correction with a control group and measure behavior after a meaningful delay. Include a check for unintended effects among people who already behave well.

Then ask whether the result transfers to another team, place or period. Record the message exactly, including any source and date. Report uncertainty and subgroup differences. If the message changes beliefs but not behavior, examine real sanctions and practical barriers. If behavior changes without belief change, another mechanism may be operating. Finally, do not confuse the goal of a study with the welfare of the people involved. A successful intervention is one that improves informed choice and justified outcomes, not merely one that moves a metric in the intended direction.

Key lessons

  1. Public behavior and private approval are different measurements.
  2. Pluralistic ignorance concerns mistaken beliefs about others' private views.
  3. False consensus is a separate bias toward overestimating agreement with oneself.
  4. Specify the reference group before measuring a norm.
  5. Use comparable questions to measure actual and perceived attitudes.
  6. A randomized correction can test causality, but results may remain local.
  7. Descriptive norm messages can produce unintended effects.
  8. Group identity, sanctions and power can outweigh a simple majority statistic.
  9. An accurate majority view is not proof that a rule is ethical.
  10. Measure lasting behavior and welfare, not only immediate survey responses.

Five questions and answers

What is pluralistic ignorance?

A systematic mismatch in which members misjudge what others privately think, often because public behavior or silence hides disagreement.

How is false consensus different?

False consensus is a tendency to overestimate how common one's own view is. Pluralistic ignorance often involves underestimating how many others share a privately held objection.

Why does silence fail to prove approval?

People may remain silent to avoid costs, because they are indifferent, or because the decision process gives them no safe way to object.

What did the global climate survey measure?

Stated willingness, approval of pro-climate norms, demand for government action and beliefs about others. It did not directly measure everyone making a payment.

Why can an accurate norm message cause harm?

A descriptive message can normalize an undesirable behavior, and a majority statistic can pressure minorities. Effects depend on reference group, framing and context.
