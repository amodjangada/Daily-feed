When Should an AI System Say 'I Don't Know'?

How calibrated confidence, selective prediction and clear fallback rules can reduce unsupported AI answers.

Research checked 2 November 2026 · Source publication dates below · Both numeric examples are illustrative

Executive summary

An AI system can produce a clear answer even when its evidence is weak. A reliable system must decide when to answer, when to ask for more information, and when to stop. Researchers call this selective prediction or abstention. The decision depends on two separate tests: whether a confidence score predicts real accuracy, and whether the cost of a wrong answer justifies taking that risk. Studies of neural-network calibration, language-model self-evaluation, and semantic uncertainty show that useful warning signals exist. They also show that these signals can fail when tasks, users, or data change. This guide explains calibration, coverage, selective risk, uncertainty over meaning, and statistical risk controls. It compares original research with practical limits, including the problem of confident but consistently wrong answers. Two illustrative calculations show how to select an answer threshold and how the cost of an error changes that threshold. The main conclusion is operational: a refusal rule needs a measured error rate, a defined fallback, and regular checks on new data. A polite statement of uncertainty is not itself a safety guarantee.

1. The central question

Suppose an assistant reads a contract and is asked whether a clause permits early termination. It finds wording that looks relevant, but the contract refers to an appendix that is missing. Should it give a likely answer, state a probability, request the appendix, or decline to decide? The best choice depends on the task, the evidence and the cost of error. A confident sentence alone cannot settle the issue.

This guide asks a precise question: How can an AI service decide when to withhold an answer while still remaining useful? The question differs from whether the model can generate a plausible reply. An answer policy is a decision system. It needs a target level of reliability, a way to measure uncertainty and a useful action when the target is not met. The same policy can be sensible for a movie recommendation and unsafe for a medical decision. We must specify the use before we set a threshold.

2. Define the important terms

Confidence is a score intended to express how likely an answer is to be correct. Calibration means that answers assigned a given probability are correct at about that rate over many comparable cases. Abstention means withholding a direct answer. Coverage is the share of requests answered. Selective risk is the error rate among the requests the system does answer. A fallback is the next action after abstention, such as asking for a source or sending the case to a person.

Two other distinctions matter. Aleatoric uncertainty concerns variation that remains even with good information, such as an inherently uncertain future event. Epistemic uncertainty concerns missing knowledge or a model's limited ability. In language systems these categories can overlap. Also, the probability of the next word is not automatically the probability that a full claim is true. The words and the claim are different objects. The studies on verbalized confidence and semantic uncertainty address this gap [4] [6].

3. From rejection rules to language models

Prediction with a reject option existed before modern chatbots. A classifier could identify a pattern or decline when the score was too weak. The objective was to reduce errors on accepted cases, while retaining useful coverage. Geifman and El-Yaniv adapted this idea to deep neural networks and reported selective classification results on image datasets in 2017 [2]. In the same year, Guo and colleagues showed that high-performing neural networks could still give poorly calibrated probabilities. They found that a simple post-processing method, temperature scaling, often improved calibration on their tested datasets [1].

Language generation added new difficulties. There may be many correct ways to state the same answer, and a long reply may mix supported and unsupported claims. Work published in 2021 studied calibration in question-answering language models [3]. Research in 2022 tested whether models could evaluate their own answers and express uncertainty in words [4] [5]. Later studies measured uncertainty over meanings rather than strings [6] [7]. These results are related, but their experimental guarantees do not transfer automatically to every deployed chatbot.

4. Step 1: define what counts as correct

A refusal rule cannot be tested until the task has a clear outcome. For a multiple-choice question, correctness may be one matching option. For a sourced report, correctness may require every key claim to match the cited record. For a medical summary, the system may also need to avoid missing a critical warning. These tasks have different error types. A single accuracy number hides that difference.

Start by writing an evaluation rule before collecting model results. Identify the permitted sources, the date range, and what happens when the source is silent. Decide whether a partly correct answer is acceptable. Use independent human review for ambiguous cases, and record reviewer disagreement. If two qualified reviewers disagree, the uncertainty may belong to the task definition, not only to the model. A good test set includes unanswerable questions and cases with missing documents. Otherwise a system can appear reliable because every benchmark item was designed to have an answer. This is especially important when the system works with changing policies, records or laws.

5. Step 2: collect an uncertainty signal

Possible signals include the model's probability for a candidate answer, its self-rated chance of being correct, disagreement among repeated samples, and whether retrieved evidence supports the claim. No signal is a universal truth meter. A high token probability may reflect common wording. A low token probability may reflect an unusual but correct phrasing. A self-rating may also change when the prompt changes.

Kadavath and colleagues reported that models could assess the truth of proposed answers in several controlled formats, and that some self-evaluation measures improved with scale. They also found weaker generalization for a trained measure of whether the model knows an answer on new tasks [4]. Lin, Hilton and Evans trained a model to express probabilities in words and reported useful calibration on their tested tasks [5]. These are evidence that uncertainty can be learned. They do not show that asking an untested model to write 'I am 90% sure' makes that number reliable.

6. Step 3: calibrate scores against outcomes

Collect a held-out set of cases that resembles real use. For each case, store the confidence score and an independently checked result. Group predictions by score range. If a group with confidence near 80% is correct on only 60% of cases, the score is overconfident. If it is correct on 95%, the score is underconfident. Both errors can distort the decision to answer.

Guo and colleagues showed that temperature scaling can adjust a classifier's probability distribution without changing its predicted class [1]. Jiang and colleagues found that several earlier generative language models were poorly calibrated on question-answering tasks and evaluated ways to improve the match between score and correctness [3]. Calibration is measured over a population of cases. It does not promise that a specific answer with an 80% score is correct. Small score groups also give noisy estimates. Report group sizes and uncertainty intervals, and keep calibration data separate from the final evaluation set.

7. Step 4: choose the answer threshold

A selective system answers only when a score passes a chosen rule. A higher threshold often reduces coverage and can reduce errors among accepted cases. The relation must be measured. If the score is weak, raising the threshold can simply select a different group of mistakes. A graph of selective risk against coverage is more useful than a single accuracy result. It shows what happens as the service answers fewer requests.

Geifman and El-Yaniv developed a procedure for selecting predictions while controlling a target risk with high probability under their stated assumptions [2]. This is stronger than merely sorting outputs by a confidence score. Yet a risk guarantee applies to its defined data and loss. It does not automatically cover a new model, a new language, or a changing customer population. A service must decide what level of selective risk it can accept and how many requests it can afford to send to fallback review. Those are policy choices as well as technical ones.

8. Step 5: treat meaning as the unit of uncertainty

Text generation makes uncertainty difficult because many sentences can mean the same thing. If one sample says a meeting is on Tuesday and another says it takes place the second day of the workweek, the wording differs but the claim may agree. A word-based disagreement score can exaggerate uncertainty. Conversely, two fluent answers may look similar while giving different dates or amounts.

Kuhn, Gal and Farquhar proposed semantic uncertainty: group generated answers by meaning, then measure how probability is spread across the groups [6]. Farquhar and colleagues later used semantic entropy to detect some confabulations, where answers vary across generations in meaning [7]. This method can flag instability that a single confident answer conceals. It also has costs. It may require several generations and a reliable way to compare meanings. Most important, consistent agreement does not prove truth. A model can repeat the same false belief with very low apparent uncertainty.

9. Step 6: separate missing evidence from uncertainty

A system should ask a different question before it decides to abstain: is the answer uncertain because the evidence is missing, because the evidence conflicts, or because the model cannot interpret it? These states need different fallbacks. Missing records call for retrieval or a user request. Conflicting records call for source comparison and a date check. Difficult reasoning may call for a specialist or a narrower question.

For source-based work, attach each material claim to a relevant passage. Then test whether the passage supports the exact claim. A source can be authentic but outdated, or it can be relevant without resolving the question. The service should distinguish 'no evidence found' from 'evidence that the answer is no.' It should also report when a retrieval system failed to access a document. This workflow limits unsupported answers, but it cannot eliminate errors. A wrong document, an incomplete index or a faulty entailment check can still create false confidence. Human review remains important for high-cost decisions.

10. Step 7: make abstention useful

Abstention should be an action with a clear next step. A system might say that the available contract lacks an appendix, list the clause that needs review, and ask for the missing document. It might give a short answer only to the part that the sources support. It might route a request to a person with a reason code. These responses preserve useful information without pretending that every question has a complete answer.

Design the fallback before the threshold. If review takes a week, excessive abstention can block important work. If the service silently rejects requests, users may bypass it and seek an untested answer elsewhere. Track the time and cost of each fallback, and whether users understand the uncertainty. A good refusal is specific: it identifies what cannot be established, what evidence is available, and what action could change the result. It should avoid a generic disclaimer that appears on every response, because constant warnings lose their value.

11. Diagram: the answer-or-abstain path

The decision should be recorded in a form that can be audited. Store the model version, score method, threshold, evidence identifiers, final action and eventual correctness judgment where available. Avoid storing unnecessary personal data. If the system uses a person to review difficult cases, measure the person's error rate too. A human fallback is not automatically perfect. This record helps identify whether an apparent improvement comes from better model answers, more refusals, or a change in the case mix.

The rows describe distinct tools, not a ranking. A calibrated score does not supply evidence. A valid citation does not establish that a whole answer is correct. A statistical guarantee controls a specified measure under a specified sampling process. For a high-risk application, the best design often combines several checks and makes their failure modes visible. The extra work has a cost in latency, computing and human review, so the design must match the stakes.

13. What the experiments establish

The strongest lesson from the original literature is conditional. Calibration studies show that a model's confidence and its correctness can diverge [1] [3]. Selective classification shows that refusing some cases can improve the error profile of accepted cases when the score and procedure are effective [2]. Language-model self-evaluation studies show that models sometimes carry useful information about whether their answers are correct [4] [5]. Semantic-entropy studies show that disagreement in meaning can help identify some generated confabulations [6] [7].

These are different results on different tasks. The 2017 selective classifier worked with image classification, not an unrestricted conversational agent. The 2022 studies used particular model families and prompts. The 2024 semantic-entropy study focused on a defined class of errors. None proves that a single uncertainty number will remain calibrated across all languages, topics and future models. A responsible deployment cites the method, the evaluation population and the failure cases rather than presenting an aggregate result as universal.

14. Illustrative calculation 1: coverage and risk

All numbers in this example are invented for teaching. A service receives 1,000 questions with verified answers. At a permissive threshold, it answers 900 and gets 810 correct. Coverage is 900 ÷ 1,000 = 90%. The accepted error rate is (900 − 810) ÷ 900 = 10%. At a stricter threshold, it answers 600 and gets 582 correct. Coverage falls to 60%, while accepted error falls to (600 − 582) ÷ 600 = 3%.

Which setting is better? The second gives fewer wrong answers, but leaves 400 requests unresolved. If the unanswered cases go to a careful reviewer, the stricter rule may be sensible. If users receive no help, the cost of delay may be large. Also check who gets refused. If the system abstains much more often on one language or user group, an average risk of 3% can hide unequal access. The calculation is descriptive. It is not a formal guarantee for the next 1,000 cases.

15. Illustrative calculation 2: the cost of being wrong

All numbers and costs in this example are illustrative. Suppose a correct answer gives a benefit of 2 units, a wrong answer creates a loss of 18 units, and a safe review creates a net value of zero units. If a model has a calibrated probability p of being correct, the expected value of answering is 2p − 18(1 − p) = 20p − 18. Answering beats review only when 20p − 18 is greater than zero, or p exceeds 0.90.

If the cost of a wrong answer is only 3 units, the calculation becomes 2p − 3(1 − p) = 5p − 3. The threshold falls to 0.60. The model did not become smarter. The decision changed because the costs changed. This is why a universal 'answer above 80%' rule is not defensible. The values also depend on how quickly review happens, whether a wrong answer can be corrected, and whether users act on the answer before a correction arrives.

16. Limits: shifts, rare failures and false certainty

Calibration measured on last month's questions may not describe tomorrow's questions. A new policy, a new model version, a different language or a change in user behavior can alter the score-error relation. This is called distribution shift. Recalibration may help, but only if the new data contain enough checked cases. Rare but severe mistakes are especially hard to estimate. An overall error rate can look low while a small class of harmful failures remains hidden.

Another limit is systematic error. Repeated samples can agree because the model learned the same false claim. A retrieved passage can be misleading if it is old or outside the relevant jurisdiction. A model can also give a high confidence score because the question resembles training data, even when the answer has changed. Audit error types separately: missing evidence, wrong extraction, wrong reasoning, outdated facts and invented citations. Record uncertainty about the measurement itself. A small test set cannot support a precise claim about a very low failure rate.

17. What statistical risk control adds

Conformal methods use a calibration set to choose output rules with statistical guarantees under stated conditions. Angelopoulos and colleagues extended this approach to control expected values of certain monotone loss functions, including examples in computer vision and language processing [8]. The result is valuable because it connects a threshold to a formal target rather than a convenient number selected after looking at test results.

But the words 'guaranteed risk' need careful reading. Such guarantees concern a defined loss, a sampling assumption and an average over relevant randomness. They are not a certificate that each accepted sentence is true. A guarantee may also be invalid if the calibration and deployment cases are not exchangeable, meaning they do not follow the required common sampling structure. Some extensions study shift, but they require additional assumptions. For free-form language, defining a reliable loss can itself be difficult. The right use is to state exactly what is controlled and what remains outside the theorem.

18. Competing views: answer more or refuse more?

One view favors broad coverage. A system that frequently refuses can be slow, costly and frustrating. Users may prefer a qualified answer with supporting evidence, especially for low-stakes exploration. A second view favors conservative thresholds because a fluent mistake can be difficult to detect and can spread through later decisions. Both positions address real costs. Neither can set the policy without a defined task and a measured fallback.

A third design uses graduated responses. The system answers simple, well-supported questions directly; gives partial answers when only some claims are supported; asks for missing facts when the gap is clear; and escalates high-cost uncertainty. This approach can outperform a single yes-or-no refusal rule in usefulness, but it is harder to test. Every response type needs its own success measure. A partial answer can still mislead if the user assumes it resolves the whole request. The product should make the scope of the answer easy to see.

19. Wider effects on work and public trust

Selective answering changes how work is divided. An assistant may handle routine requests and send unusual cases to specialists. This can reduce routine load, but the specialist queue may become harder because it contains the most difficult cases. Reviewers need enough time and access to source material. If managers judge only the number of automated answers, teams may raise coverage by accepting more risk. A better dashboard shows coverage, selective error, severity of error, review time and user outcomes together.

Public trust also depends on how the system communicates uncertainty. A vague disclaimer can protect neither the user nor the operator. Specific evidence gaps help people act. At the same time, an assistant should not use uncertainty language to avoid simple questions that it can answer well. Fairness matters: if one population has less training data, it may receive more refusals. That can reduce direct harm but also reduce access. A responsible policy measures both types of effect and reports them separately.

20. A practical evaluation protocol

First, define the task, the permitted evidence and the loss from each error type. Second, build a representative labeled set that includes missing and conflicting evidence. Third, measure the model's raw correctness and confidence. Fourth, calibrate a score on a separate split. Fifth, plot selective risk against coverage and choose a threshold using the real cost of review. Sixth, test the locked rule on untouched cases, including changed dates, languages and difficult inputs. Seventh, audit source support and review cases that the model refused.

After launch, sample answered and refused cases for independent review. Track drift, error severity and fallback delays. Re-evaluate after every major model, retrieval or prompt change. Keep a rollback rule for unexpected failures. Do not use a formal guarantee without checking its assumptions. Finally, report the numerator and denominator behind every metric. 'Three errors' means little without knowing whether there were thirty cases or thirty thousand. This procedure is demanding, but it makes the claim of reliability testable.

Key lessons

  1. Define correctness and the cost of error before setting a refusal threshold.
  2. Calibration is a property of groups of predictions, not proof for one answer.
  3. Coverage and accepted error must be reported together.
  4. Word-level confidence can differ from confidence in a claim's meaning.
  5. Repeated agreement can conceal a stable false belief.
  6. Missing evidence, conflicting evidence and hard reasoning need different fallbacks.
  7. Statistical guarantees apply only to their stated loss and assumptions.
  8. Test for data shift, rare failures and unequal refusal rates.
  9. An abstention should identify the next useful action.

Five questions and answers

What is selective risk?

The error rate among requests the system chose to answer. It must be read together with coverage, the share of all requests answered.

Does a model's 90% confidence mean a particular answer is correct?

No. A calibrated 90% score means that comparable answers with that score are correct about 90% of the time over a suitable population.

Why can repeated answers give false comfort?

The model may repeat the same incorrect claim. Consistency measures variation, not independent truth.

What changes the best abstention threshold?

The measured score-error relation, the cost of a wrong answer, the value of a correct answer and the cost of review or delay.

What does conformal risk control guarantee?

Under its assumptions, it can control a specified expected loss. It does not certify each free-form answer or remain valid after arbitrary data shifts.
