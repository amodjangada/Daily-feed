Can Producer Responsibility Make Packaging Less Wasteful?

Executive summary

Packaging protects products, but much of it becomes waste soon after purchase. Local authorities and households often bear the cost of collection, sorting, litter, and disposal. Extended producer responsibility, or EPR, shifts defined waste-management duties and costs toward the companies that place products and packaging on the market. It can finance better collection and recycling and encourage brands to use packaging that is easier to recover. Yet paying a fee or buying a recycling certificate does not prove that a company reduced waste or caused extra recycling. Design, collection quality, end markets, and enforcement determine results. The OECD estimated that only 9% of global plastic waste was ultimately recycled in 2019. New Eurostat data published on 22 October 2026 show that the EU recycled 42.4% of plastic packaging waste generated in 2024, while waste per person still increased. India uses category-based EPR obligations for plastic packaging, and new EU packaging rules began to apply in August 2026. This guide explains the economics, rules, evidence, business choices, and limits of producer responsibility, with practical tests for real environmental outcomes.

Central question: who should pay for waste?

Central question: Can making producers responsible for packaging after sale reduce waste and improve recycling, or does it mainly move costs and create compliance paperwork?

Packaging has a useful purpose. It keeps food safe, prevents damage, makes transport possible, and gives customers information. The environmental problem begins when a product's price does not reflect the full cost of managing its packaging after use. A producer may choose a material that is cheap to buy but expensive to collect, separate, or recycle. Local governments, waste workers, residents, and ecosystems can bear the difference.

EPR attempts to connect product design with waste management. Producers can be required to finance or organise collection, sorting, recycling, or other approved treatment. The policy can also set reuse and recycled-content targets. The central test is whether these duties cause less virgin material use, better recovery, lower pollution, and fairer costs. Counting certificates or registrations alone is insufficient. [1] [2]

1. How producer responsibility developed

Traditional waste management focused on what households and cities did after products were discarded. Producers usually paid for raw materials, production, and distribution, while municipalities paid for much of the collection and disposal. As packaging volumes increased, policymakers sought ways to place some end-of-life responsibility on the companies that choose packaging design and put it into commerce.

Extended producer responsibility grew in policy practice during the late twentieth century. The OECD published guidance in 2001 and revised it in 2016. The revised guidance examined collection, financing, recycling markets, and the need to adjust systems to different countries. A 2024 OECD paper then summarised core principles for current policy design. [2] [3]

India notified EPR guidelines for plastic packaging on 16 February 2022, under its Plastic Waste Management framework. Subsequent amendments refined the system. The European Union adopted Regulation (EU) 2025/40, which entered into force in February 2025 and generally began to apply on 12 August 2026. The EU rules cover waste prevention, recyclability, reuse, recycled content, and other requirements. These dates matter: an older recycling result cannot demonstrate the effects of rules that had not yet applied. [4] [5]

2. Terms that separate promises from results

Extended producer responsibility (EPR) is a policy that assigns producers responsibilities for products, including their post-consumer stage. Producer responsibility organisation (PRO) is an entity that may organise collection or compliance on behalf of producers. Eco-modulated fee is a fee that changes with product or packaging features, such as weight, material, or recyclability.

Collection means gathering discarded packaging. Sorting separates materials. Recycling processes waste into material that can be used again. These are different stages. Material collected is not automatically recycled. Recycled content is recovered material used to make a new product. Reuse means using an item again for its intended function, often after cleaning or inspection.

Recovery is a broader term that can include energy recovery, depending on the rules. End-of-life disposal is a treatment route for material that is not recycled; it should not be reported as material recycling. Additionality asks whether an action caused an environmental improvement beyond what would otherwise happen. Mass balance tracks quantities entering, leaving, and remaining in a system. Clear boundaries are essential for checking claims. [1]

3. The mechanism in six steps

Step 1: Define responsibility. Law identifies which producer, importer, or brand owner must comply, which packaging is covered, and how quantities are measured. Without clear boundaries, firms can shift obligations to one another.

Step 2: Record packaging placed on the market. Firms measure material type and weight. A reliable baseline is necessary because targets and fees often depend on these quantities. Supplier invoices and packaging specifications are useful, but they need checks.

Step 3: Finance recovery. A producer pays fees, arranges collection, or purchases eligible compliance services. The system funds collectors, sorting facilities, recyclers, and administration. The money must reach real activities rather than stop at certificate trading.

Step 4: Process and verify waste. Collectors and processors record received material, contamination, rejects, actual recycling, and other treatment. Independent audits and traceable records reduce the risk of duplicate claims.

Step 5: Change design and purchasing. Fees, recycled-content rules, and demand for recovered material can reward lighter or more recyclable packaging. A flat fee that ignores design may create little incentive to improve products.

Step 6: Test environmental results. Measure packaging generated, virgin material use, collection, actual recycling, litter, emissions, and costs. Compare with a credible baseline, not only the volume of certificates. [2] [3]

4. Diagram: the producer-to-recycler loop

The process contains two linked systems. Physical materials move through collection and processing. Money, records, and legal obligations move between producers, organisations, and processors. Good policy makes the two systems consistent. A firm should be able to trace a claim from a defined obligation to eligible processing, with no duplicate counting.

5. What global plastic data actually show

The OECD's Global Plastics Outlook, published in 2022, estimated that global plastic production reached 460 million tonnes in 2019 and plastic waste reached 353 million tonnes. After accounting for losses during recycling, only 9% of plastic waste was ultimately recycled. The study also reported large shares incinerated or landfilled, with other waste mismanaged. These are modelled global figures for 2019, not measurements for 2026 or for packaging alone. [6]

Plastic packaging is important because it often has a short life. But different materials have different collection and processing routes. A clean single-polymer bottle can have a stronger recycling market than a mixed-material sachet contaminated with food. Labels and theoretical recyclability do not guarantee a collection system exists where the customer lives.

Recycling also loses material during sorting and processing. It uses energy and can have quality limits. Therefore, a high collection rate does not equal a high final recycling yield. Waste prevention and reuse can sometimes avoid the need to produce new packaging at all. The best option depends on product safety, transport, washing, return rates, and the full life cycle.

6. The EU's latest data and new rules

Eurostat published updated packaging statistics on 22 October 2026. In 2024, the EU generated 81.5 million tonnes of packaging waste, or 181.0 kg per person. Plastic packaging waste was 35.9 kg per person, of which 15.3 kg was recycled. The reported plastic packaging recycling rate was 42.4%, compared with 41.6% in 2023 under the updated series. These figures cover waste from households, businesses, and government. [7]

The rate improved, but plastic packaging waste per person also increased. This shows why recycling rate and waste prevention must be assessed separately. Eurostat notes a calculation-method change in 2020, which also limits simple comparisons across long periods.

The EU's Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation generally began to apply on 12 August 2026. The European Commission says it adds requirements for recyclable design, recycled content, prevention of unnecessary packaging, reuse, and controls on some harmful substances. Many obligations have later deadlines, including 2030 requirements. The 2024 waste data predate these rules and cannot measure their effect. [5]

7. India's category-based EPR approach

India's plastic packaging framework distinguishes rigid packaging, flexible plastic packaging, multilayer packaging containing other materials, and specified compostable plastic packaging. The government's December 2022 parliamentary reply set out category-based minimum recycling levels as a share of the EPR target. For the 2026–27 financial year, the published schedule lists 70% for Category I rigid packaging and 50% for Categories II and III. Rules and amendments must be checked for the precise entity, material, and compliance year. [4]

A government reply dated 23 March 2026 reported that, as of 5 March 2026, 2,986 plastic waste recyclers were registered under the relevant framework and 196.97 lakh metric tonnes of plastic waste recycling and corresponding EPR certificates were reported. These are administrative implementation figures. They do not independently establish how much recycling was additional, whether every batch met the same quality standard, or how much plastic waste was prevented. [8]

For a company, the compliance task starts with identifying its legal role and measuring packaging accurately by category. A brand cannot safely assume that purchasing certificates settles every obligation, including reuse, recycled-content, or product-specific requirements.

8. Worked example one: a category target

Illustrative compliance arithmetic, not legal advice. Suppose an obligated producer has a verified Category I rigid-plastic EPR target of 100 tonnes for the 2026–27 compliance year. The government's published minimum recycling level for that category and year is 70% of the EPR target. The arithmetic minimum is 100 × 70% = 70 tonnes of eligible recycling. This does not mean that the remaining 30 tonnes can be discarded without restrictions. Other applicable recovery, reuse, recycled-content, and reporting rules still apply. [4]

If the producer holds documents for 70 tonnes of certificates, it must still confirm that the certificates are valid for the relevant category and year, were not used twice, and meet the portal's rules. The calculation shows the structure of a target. It does not prove that 70 tonnes of additional plastic were recycled because of this producer.

A useful internal control compares packaging sold, the applicable EPR target, verified processing, eligible certificates, and outstanding obligations. Each quantity should have a clear unit and time period.

9. Who pays, and who gains?

EPR shifts part of waste-management costs toward producers. The legal payer is not necessarily the final economic payer. Firms can absorb costs in margins, negotiate with suppliers, change packaging, or raise consumer prices. The result depends on competition, customer demand, and available alternatives. Local governments may save money if producer funding replaces public spending, but they may still provide services or face unfunded costs.

Collectors and recyclers can gain steadier demand and investment incentives. Producers may gain a more reliable supply of recycled material and better evidence for customer requirements. But fees and administrative work can be harder for small firms, especially when reporting systems differ across regions.

Fee design matters. If every kilogram of a material pays the same rate, firms mainly have an incentive to reduce weight. If fees reflect sorting cost, recyclability, contamination, and other verified impacts, firms may have a reason to change design. The OECD notes that simple collective fee schedules often create weak design incentives. More complex fees can improve signals but require sound data and may increase compliance costs. [9]

10. Worked example two: the cost of lighter packaging

All values illustrative; no actual EPR fee schedule is implied. A business sells 100,000 units. Each unit uses 25 grams of plastic packaging, so annual packaging is 2.5 tonnes. A redesign reduces the weight to 18 grams, giving 1.8 tonnes. The reduction is 0.7 tonnes, or 28% of the original packaging weight.

Assume a hypothetical producer fee of ₹12,000 per tonne, unchanged between the two formats. The annual fee falls from ₹30,000 to ₹21,600, a saving of ₹8,400. If the redesign costs ₹15,000 more per year in materials or operations, the fee saving alone does not justify it. If it reduces material purchases by more than that extra cost, the full business result can differ.

This calculation also says nothing about recyclability or product damage. A lighter package that causes more spoiled food could have a worse total environmental outcome. A complete decision checks material use, product protection, collection compatibility, life-cycle impacts, and actual fees before approving the design.

11. What original research can establish

A peer-reviewed study by J. T. Pruess and R. D. Garrett, published in Resources, Conservation and Recycling in 2025, assessed the potential effectiveness of EPR in Belgium, France, and Germany. It compared policy scope, design, and national context. The authors found strengths in all three systems and differences in technology, municipal roles, costs, and transparency. Their method was an ex-ante assessment: it evaluated expected potential rather than directly measuring the causal impact of a new rule after implementation. [10]

That distinction is important. A recycling rate may improve because of EPR, but it can also change because of commodity prices, collection investment, product mix, import rules, or statistical methods. A credible causal study needs a comparison that accounts for these other forces.

The OECD's 2016 and 2024 guidance draws on policy experience and research across countries. It supports EPR as a useful approach, while stressing institutional capacity and scheme design. Guidance and comparative studies are evidence for policy choices; they are not a guarantee of the same outcome in every city or industry. [2] [3]

12. Serious competing views

Support for strong EPR: Producers choose packaging and can influence materials, labels, and suppliers. Assigning costs to them can finance collection, reduce pressure on local budgets, and reward better design. Mandatory targets can also create demand for recycled material when voluntary purchasing is weak.

Concern about compliance without change: Companies may buy certificates while continuing to place difficult-to-recycle packaging on the market. If a certificate represents recycling that would have happened anyway, the payment may redistribute revenue without causing extra environmental benefit. Weak monitoring can allow double counting or low-quality processing.

Concern about cost and fairness: Fees may raise consumer prices. Small producers may face high administrative costs. Informal waste workers may lose access to valuable material if collection becomes centralised without fair integration. These are implementation risks, not reasons to assume that all EPR schemes fail.

Concern about recycling-first policy: Recycling is valuable, but reducing unnecessary packaging and building workable reuse systems may prevent more waste. Reuse is not automatically superior if washing, transport, breakage, or low return rates create large costs. A sound scheme tests alternatives using local evidence. [1] [9]

13. Waste workers and local institutions

In many Indian cities, informal workers recover material before formal waste systems process it. They provide collection and sorting services but may have limited legal protection, stable income, or safe working conditions. An EPR system that funds new facilities without recognising existing workers can shift income away from them. A better design considers contracts, fair payment, safety equipment, traceability, and access to training.

Local governments also matter. They manage public spaces, collection systems, and some disposal services. A producer organisation may not know the collection costs or waste patterns of every ward. Cooperation can reduce duplicate routes and improve service, but contracts must define responsibilities clearly.

The United Nations Environment Programme describes EPR as a way to move end-of-life costs toward producers and stimulate better design. It also stresses that implementation must fit local conditions. A programme should therefore evaluate worker outcomes, public service coverage, and material recovery together. Tonnes of certificates alone cannot show whether the people doing the collection are better off. [1]

14. How to audit environmental claims

Start with a physical inventory of packaging placed on the market by type, mass, and reporting period. Record the supplier, product format, and material specification. Then map the collection route and the registered processor. Request evidence of material received, contamination, rejected material, recycling output, and final destination.

Check that the same processed waste was not used to support multiple incompatible claims. Reconcile certificate quantities with physical records and legal categories. Record whether the process was material recycling, reuse, or another permitted treatment. If the material was exported, identify the receiving facility and applicable controls.

Next, measure outcomes that a company can influence: packaging per product sold, absolute virgin material purchased, return rate, recovered material quality, and total waste generated. Compare periods using consistent methods and account for sales growth. A fall in waste per unit can coexist with an increase in total waste.

Finally, separate compliance, reported recycling, and additional environmental improvement. Each requires different evidence. A certificate can be a valid compliance record without proving that the buyer caused a reduction in total pollution.

15. Wider business and policy effects

Well-designed EPR can create a more predictable market for collection and recycled materials. This may support investment in sorting, processing, and product redesign. It can also improve data on packaging flows. Businesses that sell across borders may face more consistent product standards within a common market, while still needing to meet different national reporting requirements.

However, regulation can change competition. Large brands may spread compliance costs across many products, while small firms face higher costs per unit. Producers can pass some fees to consumers. Public agencies need capacity to detect fraud, resolve disputes, and verify processors. The environmental outcome also depends on energy use, collection distances, material quality, and the markets for recycled output.

The EU's 2026 rules add prevention and reuse measures alongside recycling. India's approach uses category targets and a certificate system. Neither design can be judged from the law alone. The important question is whether measured waste, virgin material demand, leakage, and harm decline after accounting for economic growth and changes in reporting. [5] [8]

16. Nine lessons to retain

  1. Packaging protects products, so environmental design must preserve its function.
  2. EPR assigns end-of-life duties and costs to defined producers.
  3. Collection, sorting, recycling, and recycled content are different measures.
  4. Valid compliance records do not automatically prove additional recycling.
  5. Fee design determines whether firms have a strong reason to improve packaging.
  6. Waste prevention and reuse deserve separate tests alongside recycling.
  7. Absolute waste can increase even when recycling rates improve.
  8. Worker conditions, municipal capacity, and enforcement affect real outcomes.
  9. Judge policy with physical material flows, dated evidence, and a credible baseline.

Five knowledge-check questions and answers

1. What problem does EPR try to correct?

It makes producers responsible for defined end-of-life costs and duties that would otherwise fall mainly on municipalities, households, and the environment.

2. Why is collection not the same as recycling?

Collected material can be contaminated, rejected, stored, or sent to another treatment route. Recycling requires processing into usable material under the relevant accounting rules.

3. Can an EPR certificate prove additional environmental benefit?

No. It can document eligible compliance activity, but additionality requires evidence that the activity improved outcomes beyond what would otherwise have occurred.

4. Why can a recycling rate rise while waste grows?

The share recycled can increase while the total amount of packaging placed on the market grows faster or remains high. Rates and absolute quantities answer different questions.

5. What should a brand check before changing packaging?

Material and weight, product protection, local collection and recycling, legal targets, full cost, reuse options, and the likely effect on absolute virgin material use and pollution.
