Why Do Governments Insure Bank Deposits—and What Risks Does That Create?

How can insurance prevent bank runs without encouraging greater bank risk?

Executive summary

Banks promise depositors quick access to money, but use much of that money to finance loans and securities that cannot always be sold quickly. This creates useful credit and a risk: if many people withdraw together, even a bank with valuable long-term assets can run short of cash. Deposit insurance promises to reimburse eligible depositors when an insured bank fails. Credible protection can reduce panic and prevent damaging bank runs. Yet it changes incentives. Protected depositors may monitor banks less closely, while some banks may take more risk unless capital rules, supervision, and premiums constrain them. India covers eligible deposits up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank in the same right and capacity. Official figures for March 2026 show that 97.4% of deposit accounts were fully protected, but only 39.7% of assessable deposit value was insured. These figures describe different parts of the system. This guide explains the mechanism, evidence, payment rules, moral hazard, competing designs, and wider consequences for savers, businesses, and governments.

Central question and decision rule

Central question: How can governments protect ordinary depositors without encouraging banks to take excessive risks or making public promises too broad to finance?

Depositors need access to money for payments and emergencies. Banks use deposits to fund loans and other assets. A bank may be able to repay its customers over time yet lack enough cash if everyone demands repayment today. A credible insurance promise changes the reason to withdraw early. But the promise needs a legal limit, dependable funding, accurate bank records, and fast payment procedures.

Deposit insurance is only one part of the safety net. Capital absorbs losses. Liquid assets meet withdrawals. Supervisors examine bank risks. Central banks can supply eligible emergency liquidity. Resolution authorities handle failure. A sound policy must connect these tools without confusing them. [1] [2]

1. History: from bank panics to legal protection

Bank failures and depositor panic were severe during the Great Depression. The United States established the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) in 1933, and insurance began in January 1934. Its present standard limit is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. The limit protects defined deposit claims, not every product sold by a financial firm. [3]

India established deposit insurance in 1961. Its present insurer is the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC). India's cap increased to ₹5 lakh in February 2020. The rules cover eligible deposits at insured commercial and cooperative banks, subject to exclusions and legal ownership conditions. [4]

In 1983, Douglas Diamond and Philip Dybvig published a model showing that useful demand-deposit contracts can also make banks vulnerable to self-fulfilling runs. A depositor may rush to withdraw because other depositors might do so, even when the underlying loans are productive. Credible insurance can change that incentive. Their model explains a mechanism; it does not determine the best insurance limit for every country. [5]

2. Terms that must not be confused

Deposit insurance is a statutory promise to protect eligible deposits under specified conditions. Coverage limit is the maximum payable under those rules. Liquidity means the ability to make payments when due. Solvency means assets are sufficient to meet liabilities after losses are recognised. A bank can be illiquid without being insolvent, and can be insolvent while still holding cash.

Bank run means unusually fast withdrawals by many depositors. Capital is the part of a bank's funding that absorbs losses before deposit creditors. Resolution is the legal process for handling a failing bank and maintaining essential services. Premium is the charge that banks pay to finance insurance. Moral hazard means protection can encourage extra risk because someone else may bear part of the loss.

Market discipline is pressure from investors, creditors, and depositors that makes risky funding more expensive. Insured deposit ratio measures insured deposit value relative to assessable deposit value. It is different from the share of accounts fully covered. These definitions prevent common errors in policy debates. [6]

3. How a run develops, step by step

Step 1: A bank announces losses, a large customer withdraws, or information raises doubts. The problem may be a real loss or uncertainty about other customers' actions.

Step 2: Depositors compare waiting with withdrawing. If they believe the bank may run out of cash, early withdrawal seems safer. That belief can spread quickly through connected customers and digital payment channels.

Step 3: The bank uses reserves and liquid securities. If withdrawals continue, it may sell assets quickly, borrow against collateral, or restrict new lending. Selling long-term securities after interest rates rise can cause losses.

Step 4: Visible withdrawals and asset sales can increase fear. A cash shortage may become a solvency problem if losses reduce capital. Other banks may face pressure even when their risks differ.

Step 5: Credible deposit insurance removes much of the reason for covered customers to rush. Uninsured customers can still leave, and insurance cannot repair poor loans. Authorities still need supervision, liquidity support, and a way to resolve failing banks. [5] [7]

4. Diagram: two paths after a shock

Insurance acts on expectations before a failure and on reimbursement after a legal trigger. The first effect may be more important for preventing panic. The second requires accurate records and prompt access to funds. These roles are related but different. A lender of last resort provides liquidity under its rules; a deposit insurer protects defined claims. Neither promises to save shareholders or every uninsured creditor.

5. Worked example one: India's ₹5 lakh cap

Illustrative account balances, not a real claim. A person holds ₹3.2 lakh in savings, ₹1.6 lakh in a fixed deposit, and ₹0.7 lakh in eligible accrued interest at Bank A. All are in the person's own name and legal capacity. The combined balance is ₹5.5 lakh. Under the ₹5 lakh cap, ₹5 lakh is insured and ₹0.5 lakh exceeds the insurance limit.

Suppose the person also has ₹2 lakh at a different insured Bank B. That balance is assessed separately and is fully insured under Bank B's limit. Opening more accounts or branches at Bank A in the same right and capacity does not create extra protection. Other ownership categories, offsets, and legal conditions may change an actual claim. The DICGC includes eligible principal and interest in the same cap. [4]

The uncovered amount is not necessarily lost. Recovery depends on what happens to the failed bank's assets and on the resolution process. Insurance coverage, payment timing, and recovery of uninsured funds are separate questions.

6. How claims and premiums work

A functioning system needs several stages. The law defines eligible banks, accounts, ownership rights, and exclusions. The insurer collects premiums and holds funds. Banks maintain depositor records. A failure or another specified event activates a claims process. The insurer or receiving institution then makes insured balances available under the legal rules.

DICGC covers eligible savings, current, fixed, and recurring deposits. It excludes specified categories, including inter-bank deposits and certain government deposits. Claims may be handled through a liquidator or other statutory arrangements, depending on the case. The maximum covers eligible principal and interest together. A deposit in a mutual fund, a stock, or an unqualified non-bank product does not become insured because it was bought through a banking app. [4]

Payment speed matters. Households need money for food, rent, and emergencies. Businesses need to pay wages and suppliers. International guidance from the IADI, updated in September 2025, stresses funding, coordination, public awareness, and timely reimbursement. A legal promise that customers cannot use promptly during failure offers less protection than it appears to provide. [6]

7. India's latest official numbers

On 14 October 2026, DICGC released figures for the year ended 31 March 2026. It reported 1,950 insured banks: 124 commercial and 1,826 cooperative banks. Fully protected accounts numbered 293.5 crore, or 97.4% of accounts. Insured deposits were ₹1,09,33,948 crore, equal to 39.7% of assessable deposits of ₹2,75,43,642 crore. [1]

The 97.4% figure counts accounts with balances within the insurance cap. The 39.7% figure measures the insured share of all assessable deposit money. A small share of large accounts can contain a large share of the system's money. The two measures can therefore differ sharply without contradiction.

The same report states that the Deposit Insurance Fund held ₹2,61,823 crore, with a 2.39% reserve ratio. During 2025–26, DICGC received ₹29,947 crore in premiums and settled ₹1,988 crore in claims. These are dated fund and activity figures. They are not a guarantee that the fund could cover every imaginable simultaneous bank failure without other support. [1]

8. What the 2023 US failures show

Silicon Valley Bank failed on 10 March 2023. An FDIC account published in 2024 says it had $209 billion in assets at year-end 2022 and more than 90% of deposits were uninsured. On 9 March, $42 billion in deposits left the bank, with further large withdrawals queued. The case shows how concentrated depositors and fast payment systems can accelerate a run. [7]

The FDIC's May 2023 reform study found that more than 99% of US deposit accounts were below the $250,000 insurance limit in December 2022, yet uninsured deposits were a much larger share of total deposit value. It compared three policy options: limited coverage, unlimited coverage, and targeted higher coverage for business payment accounts. It regarded targeted coverage as promising, while recognising serious implementation problems. The study was an assessment, not a law that changed the standard cap. [2]

The failure also involved interest-rate exposure, concentrated funding, and risk management. Greater insurance might have reduced some withdrawal pressure, but would not by itself have corrected underlying asset losses or weak governance.

9. Why insurance can create moral hazard

Insurance protects depositors from a defined loss. That can reduce the incentive to study a bank's financial health or demand extra interest from a risky bank. The same reduced incentive helps prevent panic. This is the policy trade-off: fewer destabilising runs can come with weaker depositor monitoring.

Banks may also have an incentive to take greater risk when insured funding remains available at similar prices. But the effect is not automatic. Shareholders can lose their capital. Managers can lose their jobs. Supervisors can require corrective action. Other creditors can demand higher returns. The strength of moral hazard depends on all these controls.

DICGC's 2025–26 report identifies a move from flat-rate to risk-based insurance premiums, effective 1 April 2026. Risk-sensitive premiums seek to make riskier banking more costly. They need accurate risk measures, fair rules, and supervision; otherwise they may charge weak banks more precisely when those banks are already under stress. [1]

10. What original research finds and its limits

Diamond and Dybvig's 1983 model shows how a bank can provide valuable liquidity while remaining exposed to self-fulfilling withdrawals. It establishes a theoretical possibility, not a prediction of a particular bank's failure. [5]

Demirgüç-Kunt and Detragiache studied 61 countries over 1980–1997. Their research associated explicit deposit insurance with greater banking fragility, especially where institutions were weak and interest rates were deregulated. Their IMF working paper appeared in January 2000; a journal version followed in 2002. The study provides evidence for moral-hazard concerns, but country-level comparisons cannot remove every effect of supervision, crisis history, and political choices. [8]

The FDIC's 2023 evidence shows the risk of large uninsured balances and rapid withdrawals. It cannot prove that unlimited coverage would prevent every run. The most defensible conclusion is conditional: credible protection can improve stability, while weak rules and incentives can increase longer-term risk. A good system evaluates both effects instead of treating either as universal. [2]

11. Three serious policy choices

Limited coverage: A clear cap protects small savers and preserves some pressure on large creditors to monitor risk. Its weakness is that firms may need large balances for ordinary payroll and trade. These deposits can leave quickly during a crisis.

Unlimited coverage: Covering all eligible deposits could reduce depositor loss fears and support payment continuity. It would enlarge the insurance commitment and weaken one form of market discipline. Stronger supervision and risk-sensitive pricing would become more important.

Targeted coverage: Higher protection for genuine transaction accounts could protect payroll and supplier payments without covering all large investment-like balances. The difficulty is defining an eligible account and preventing people from moving funds into protected categories solely to gain cover.

The FDIC's 2023 report compared these options and found targeted coverage worthy of further consideration. Countries differ in banking structure, fiscal capacity, deposit concentration, and supervisory quality. The best design therefore cannot be copied without examining local conditions. [2]

12. Worked example two: a liquidity gap

Illustrative bank; all figures are invented. A bank has ₹100 crore of deposits and ₹15 crore in immediately available cash and liquid assets. Customers request ₹25 crore of withdrawals in one day. Without fresh funding, the immediate liquidity gap is ₹10 crore: ₹25 crore requested minus ₹15 crore available. The gap does not prove insolvency; the bank's longer-term loans may still be valuable.

Suppose credible insurance reduces withdrawals by covered depositors, and the total request falls to ₹12 crore. The assumed ₹15 crore liquid stock can now meet it. This shows how expectations can change short-term funding needs. It is not a measured estimate of insurance's effect on withdrawals.

The bank may still have real losses, large uninsured customers may still leave, and its liquid assets may fall in market value. A liquidity calculation cannot replace an asset-quality review or a plan for resolving an insolvent bank.

13. Designing a stronger system

Begin with a clear public objective: protect small savers, preserve payment services, reduce contagion, or combine these goals. Then define who is covered and how much, and identify who bears losses above the cap. Rules must be understandable before a crisis.

Next, require banks to maintain accurate depositor and ownership records. Test payout systems, not only legal documents. Build financing capacity through premiums and recovery procedures. Align bank risk with capital, liquidity, supervision, and insurance pricing. Plan how authorities will transfer deposits or close a bank while preserving essential services.

Finally, test severe but plausible events, including fast digital runs and several banks failing together. Publish coverage statistics, claims data, and the scheme's limits. The IADI's September 2025 Core Principles offer a framework for reviewing governance, funding, contingency planning, and reimbursement. They are international benchmarks that need adaptation to each country's law. [6]

14. Wider effects and unresolved questions

Households gain protection for balances needed for daily life. Firms gain more dependable access to payment funds. Banks may benefit from a stable deposit base that supports lending. Yet the promise can shift risk to the insurance fund and, in extreme cases, create pressure for public support. Wider coverage can benefit large depositors more directly, even when it also prevents wider harm to workers and suppliers.

Several uncertainties remain. Coverage ratios do not measure bank health. Historical cross-country evidence may not fully describe today's digital banking. A depositor can be legally covered and still face a delay in access during resolution. The right coverage amount depends on inflation, typical balances, business payment needs, and the strength of supervision.

The correct test is wider than claims paid. It asks whether the system reduces damaging runs, preserves credit and payments, limits risk-taking, and distributes costs fairly. Insurance is valuable only as part of a credible financial safety net.

15. Eight lessons to retain

  1. Demand deposits provide liquidity but expose banks to fast withdrawals.
  2. A run can result from real losses, fear of other withdrawals, or both.
  3. Credible insurance changes the incentive for covered depositors to rush.
  4. Legal coverage depends on the bank, depositor, ownership rights, and eligible product.
  5. The share of fully covered accounts is different from the insured share of deposit value.
  6. Insurance does not repair bad loans or promise protection to every creditor.
  7. Broad protection can improve stability while reducing depositor monitoring.
  8. Capital, supervision, funding, and prompt resolution are necessary partners to insurance.

Five knowledge-check questions and answers

1. Why can a solvent bank face a run?

Its loans may repay slowly while deposits can leave today. Withdrawals can exceed available cash and force costly asset sales.

2. Does opening several accounts at one Indian bank multiply the ₹5 lakh limit?

No. Eligible deposits in the same right and capacity at the same insured bank are combined, including eligible principal and interest.

3. Why do India's 97.4% and 39.7% insurance ratios differ?

The first counts fully protected accounts; the second measures the insured share of assessable deposit money. Large accounts hold substantial value.

4. What is the central objection to unlimited coverage?

It can weaken monitoring of bank risk and expand the funding promise, making strong supervision and risk pricing more important.

5. Why does the insurer need a resolution process?

Insurance defines eligible claims; resolution and payout systems determine how customers regain access and how a failing bank is handled.
