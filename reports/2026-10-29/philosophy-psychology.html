Why Do People Reject Money They Think Is Unfair?

When people reject a positive payment, what does that tell us about fairness, intention and cooperation?

Research checked 29 October 2026 · Both numerical examples are illustrative · Original publication dates below

Executive summary

In a simple bargaining game, one person offers another a share of money. If the second person rejects the offer, both get nothing. A narrow model of self-interest predicts that the second person should accept any positive amount. Many people instead reject very unequal offers. This result helped researchers study fairness as part of human choice. Some people dislike unequal outcomes. Others respond to what they believe the proposer intended. Anger, reputation, social learning and the rules of the setting can also affect decisions. Experiments with punishment show that people sometimes pay a cost to penalize a person who breaks a cooperative rule. Cross-cultural studies find large differences in offers and responses, which limits claims about one universal fairness rule. Economic theories of inequality aversion, reciprocity and concern for total welfare explain different parts of the evidence. None explains every decision. This guide follows the logic of the experiments, compares the main theories, gives two illustrative calculations and considers what the findings mean for contracts, teams and institutions. Fairness matters, but its meaning and its cost depend on context.

1. The central question: why refuse a gain?

Imagine that two people must divide a sum of money. One person proposes the division. The other can accept it or reject it. If the second person accepts, both receive the proposed amounts. If the second person rejects, neither receives anything. Why would a person reject a positive offer? Rejection reduces the responder's own immediate earnings. It also prevents the proposer from keeping a large share.

The question matters because many economic and political rules assume that people respond to incentives. They do, but an incentive is not only a payment. People can also care about the distribution of gains, the process that produced it and the way they are treated. A decision that looks costly in a one-time money calculation can express a preference for fair treatment or impose a penalty on another person.

The research does not establish that every rejection is morally correct. It asks what people do, which conditions change their decisions and which theories predict the pattern. The original ultimatum-bargaining experiment by Güth, Schmittberger and Schwarze appeared in December 1982 [1]. It remains a useful starting point because its rules are simple enough to compare predictions.

2. Define the terms before judging the results

An ultimatum game is a one-round bargaining task with an offer and an accept-or-reject response. The proposer chooses the split; the responder can block it. A dictator game removes the responder's power to reject. The allocator simply decides how much the other person receives. A public-goods game asks people to contribute to a shared fund whose benefits can reach others. These tasks measure related but different choices.

Self-interest, in a narrow money-only model, means choosing the action that maximizes one's own payment. Inequality aversion means disliking unequal outcomes. Reciprocity means responding to perceived kind or unkind actions with a corresponding response. Costly punishment means paying a personal cost to reduce another person's payoff. Social welfare means the total or weighted well-being of people affected by a choice.

A subgame-perfect equilibrium is a strategy that remains optimal at every decision point, given the assumed preferences. It is a prediction conditional on those preferences, not a description of every human being. An experiment's external validity is how well its findings apply outside the test setting. These definitions help keep observation, theory and moral judgment separate.

3. From rational bargaining to social preferences

Classical game theory gave researchers a precise way to predict decisions under stated preferences and information. If both people care only about their own immediate money, the responder in a one-round ultimatum game should accept any positive offer. Knowing this, a proposer should offer the smallest permitted positive amount. This prediction is logically consistent under the stated assumptions. It is also easy to test.

The 1982 bargaining experiment helped show that actual behavior often differs from this prediction [1]. Researchers then tested alternative explanations. Fehr and Schmidt's 1999 model added dislike of unequal outcomes to the payoff calculation [2]. Bolton and Ockenfels proposed a model in which relative standing matters alongside personal earnings in March 2000 [3]. Charness and Rabin later tested whether people care about the total gains of a group as well as fairness and reciprocity [7].

This history changed the research question. The aim was no longer to show that people sometimes act against a narrow payoff prediction. It became important to find which motives are present, how strong they are and when the setting changes their effect. A useful model must predict new observations, not merely explain an old result after it occurs.

4. Step one: understand the ultimatum game

The proposer receives control over a fixed sum. They must offer a share to the responder. The responder sees the offer and chooses accept or reject. Acceptance makes the proposed division final. Rejection gives both players zero. The game is often anonymous, and the researchers can vary the stake, the rules, the available information and whether players expect to meet again.

Under the narrow money-only model, a responder who receives any positive sum is better off accepting than rejecting. This gives the proposer a reason to make a very small offer. But a responder may care about more than their own payment. If accepting a very unequal split causes strong dissatisfaction, rejection may be the preferred choice even though it pays zero.

The experiment has a key strength: it makes a trade-off visible. The responder can keep some money or impose a penalty by rejecting. Its weakness is also clear. The task is artificial. Participants may infer that the experimenter expects a particular behavior, and the meaning of an offer can depend on local ideas about sharing. Researchers therefore use different games and cultures to test whether the result is stable.

5. Step two: identify the decision process

A responder first observes an offer. They then compare it with a reference point, such as an equal split, a local custom or a prior expectation. Next they interpret the proposer: was the offer deliberately unfair, made under a restriction or chosen without full knowledge? The responder may then experience anger, disappointment or a desire to correct the outcome. Finally they weigh the money they would receive against the cost or benefit they assign to acceptance and rejection.

These stages are a useful analytical model, not a claim that people consciously perform a fixed calculation. Some judgments are quick. Others involve thought about the experiment, the proposer and future interactions. The same offer can therefore produce different choices in different settings. A responder may accept a low amount when the proposer had no better option, but reject it when the proposer deliberately selected it over a fair split.

This distinction matters for evidence. If researchers only record accepted and rejected offers, they can observe the final choice but cannot directly see the motive. To test motive, they must change the available alternatives, collect beliefs, vary anonymity or compare related games. Even then, more than one explanation can remain consistent with the result.

6. Explanation one: people dislike unequal outcomes

Inequality-aversion models add a cost when one person receives more or less than another. Fehr and Schmidt proposed that some people dislike being behind others and may also dislike being ahead, with the two reactions allowed to differ [2]. A person may reject a low offer because the disutility of being treated unequally is greater than the money received. A proposer may offer a more equal split because they expect rejection or because they dislike benefiting from an unequal division.

Bolton and Ockenfels offered another model in which a person's share of total payoff affects utility [3]. These theories are useful because they can explain patterns across bargaining, public-goods and other laboratory games with a limited number of assumptions. They also show that the same visible action can have more than one cause. A proposer can offer half to avoid rejection, to reduce inequality or to follow a learned rule.

The limitation is that an outcome-only model cannot fully explain why people react differently to identical distributions produced by different intentions. It may also miss concern for the total amount earned by a group. This is why researchers test intentions, available choices and welfare preferences separately.

7. Explanation two: intentions and reciprocity

Reciprocity models treat an action partly as a signal about another person's intentions. A low offer can look hostile when the proposer could easily have offered more. The same low offer may seem less hostile when the proposer had no other choice. In this view, the responder is reacting to the action's meaning, not only the final distribution. A punishment can communicate that deliberate exploitation is unacceptable.

Charness and Rabin's August 2002 experiments examined social preferences with simple allocation tasks [7]. Their results suggest that concern for social welfare, especially the position of those with low payoffs, can be more important than pure equality in some settings. They also found evidence of reciprocity: willingness to sacrifice for another person can fall when that person behaves badly.

This evidence challenges a simple division between selfish and fair people. The same participant may support an unequal outcome if it greatly increases total welfare, yet punish a person whose action appears deliberately unfair. It also shows why contracts and institutional procedures matter. People may respond to the way an outcome was produced. Researchers must distinguish a fair outcome, a fair intention and a fair process rather than treating them as the same variable.

8. Step three: test costly punishment

In a public-goods task, people can benefit from a shared contribution even when they contribute little themselves. This creates a free-rider problem. Researchers can add a punishment stage in which participants spend some of their own money to reduce the payoff of a person who contributed little. The punishment is costly for the punisher, so it is difficult to explain using only immediate personal earnings in a one-time anonymous setting.

Fehr and Gächter's Nature paper, published on 10 January 2002, reported that cooperation was higher when participants could punish defectors and fell when punishment was unavailable in their experimental design [4]. The study supports the claim that some people will pay to enforce cooperation. It does not prove that punishment always improves welfare. Punishment itself consumes resources, and different settings may permit retaliation or punishment of cooperative people.

A complete assessment therefore needs two outcomes. First, did contributions rise? Second, did the net benefits after punishment costs rise? Researchers also need to ask whether the threat of punishment would remain credible when relationships are repeated, identities are known or the institution is poorly trusted. The mechanism can support cooperation, but it can also create conflict.

9. What brain studies add—and what they cannot prove

Sanfey and colleagues used functional magnetic resonance imaging to examine people responding to fair and unfair ultimatum offers. Their Science article was published on 13 June 2003 [5]. Unfair offers were associated with activity in brain areas that the authors linked to emotional and cognitive processes, including the anterior insula and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Stronger anterior insula responses were associated with rejection of unfair offers in their study.

This result supports the idea that bargaining decisions involve multiple processes. It does not identify a single fairness center in the brain. Brain regions take part in many functions, and a signal associated with a choice does not establish a unique cause. Imaging data also depend on task design, analysis and the participant sample. It is a mistake to conclude that emotion and reason are separate opponents, or that a brain scan can determine whether a person's choice is morally right.

The useful conclusion is narrower: a bargaining choice is not adequately described by a simple money calculation alone. Brain evidence can add information about processes involved in decisions, while behavioral experiments remain necessary to test the effects of changed offers, intentions and institutions.

10. Cross-cultural evidence: fairness is not one fixed number

A major limitation of early experimental economics was the heavy use of university participants. A pattern observed among students in one country might not describe people in societies with different forms of exchange, cooperation and social obligation. Henrich and colleagues addressed this problem by conducting bargaining and related experiments in 15 small-scale societies. Their article appeared in Behavioral and Brain Sciences in December 2005 [6].

The researchers reported deviations from the narrow self-interest prediction across the societies, but also substantial differences between groups. Patterns of market integration and the importance of cooperation in everyday life were associated with some of the variation. This is evidence that social experience and institutions matter. It is not proof that culture alone determines an individual's decision. The study also had limits in sample sizes, game interpretation and the comparability of settings.

Cross-cultural results should make readers cautious about claims such as 'people always demand an equal split.' Some groups may regard particular offers as generous, insulting or binding in ways that differ from a standard laboratory interpretation. Fairness research needs both a general theory of social choice and close attention to local meaning.

11. Diagram: how a rejection can arise

The diagram separates what an experimenter controls from what a participant may infer. An offer is observable. A reference point, perceived intention and emotional response are partly hidden. Acceptance or rejection is observable again. Different theories place different weight on the hidden stages. A test that changes the proposer's available options can help identify intention effects; a test that changes only the payoff split is better suited to measuring responses to outcomes.

The diagram is a conceptual map. It does not claim that all people follow the stages in the same order. It helps show why one behavioral result cannot prove a single psychological motive. Strong research changes the conditions of choice and compares predictions across competing explanations.

12. Worked example one: the price of rejecting an offer

Illustrative numbers, not results from a published experiment. Two players must divide ₹1,000. The proposer offers the responder ₹100 and keeps ₹900. If the responder accepts, the payoffs are ₹900 and ₹100. If the responder rejects, both receive ₹0. The responder gives up ₹100 by rejecting. The proposer loses ₹900. The responder can therefore impose a nine-times-larger loss on the proposer than the responder's own foregone payment.

Under a narrow model that values only the responder's own immediate money, acceptance gives ₹100 and rejection gives ₹0, so acceptance is preferred. Under a model that also assigns a negative value to an unequal outcome, the responder may reject. Under an intention-based model, rejection may depend on whether the proposer freely chose the 90:10 split or was forced to offer it.

The calculation does not establish the responder's motive. It shows the trade-off that the experiment makes visible. The experimenter must collect more evidence to distinguish anger, inequality aversion, reciprocity and misunderstanding of the rules.

13. Worked example two: punishment and group welfare

Illustrative numbers, not an estimate from Fehr and Gächter. Suppose four people each start with ₹100. Each can contribute money to a shared fund. Every ₹1 contributed creates ₹1.60 in total group value, split equally. If one person contributes ₹20, they lose ₹20 personally but receive ₹8 from that contribution, while the other three each receive ₹8. The group gains ₹12 overall from the contribution, because ₹32 is returned for ₹20 contributed.

Now suppose a member pays ₹5 to reduce a free rider's payoff by ₹15. The immediate combined payoff falls by ₹20 because both the punishment cost and the penalty remove money. If the threat makes future members contribute more, it could increase later group gains. Whether the system produces a net benefit depends on the size and duration of that change.

The key distinction is between the immediate cost of enforcement and the later benefit of cooperation. An institution should measure both. A rule that raises contributions can still waste resources if its enforcement costs are too high or if it punishes people who could not contribute.

14. Competing explanations and unresolved questions

There is a serious debate over how much fairness behavior reflects stable preferences and how much reflects context. Outcome-based theories offer clear mathematical predictions and can fit several games. Intention-based accounts explain why the same payoff can feel different when it comes from a different choice. Social-welfare models explain why some people prefer a larger total benefit even when the distribution is unequal. None of these views requires claiming that all people are either selfish or altruistic.

Another debate concerns the laboratory itself. Participants may respond to anonymity, the experimenter's presence, the way instructions are written or their beliefs about what the task means. Engel's meta-study of dictator games, published in Experimental Economics in November 2011, examined evidence from many experimental designs and showed why design differences matter [8]. A dictator game removes rejection, but it does not remove social expectations or concerns about being observed.

The strongest interpretation is therefore conditional. Experiments show that money-only self-interest is an incomplete description of many observed decisions. They do not show that every costly rejection is a demand for equality. The relative roles of inequality, intentions, welfare, reputation and emotion depend on the person and the setting.

15. Wider effects: contracts, teams and public institutions

Fairness research matters wherever people must accept a division of gains or burdens. In a workplace, a pay decision can affect cooperation through both the amount received and the process used to reach it. In a supply contract, a party may reject terms that appear exploitative even when accepting would create a small immediate profit. In public policy, people may support enforcement when they believe others are avoiding a shared duty. These are plausible applications of the mechanisms, not direct numerical forecasts from laboratory studies.

The findings also warn against treating punishment as an easy solution. A system needs clear rules, reliable evidence, a chance to correct mistakes and limits on penalties. Otherwise enforcement can become retaliation. People may disagree about what counts as a fair contribution because their costs, resources and obligations differ. Equal payments are not always equitable when circumstances differ.

Institutions can reduce conflict by making choices and constraints visible. Explain how an outcome was calculated, what alternatives were available and how complaints will be reviewed. These steps address both distribution and perceived intention. Their effectiveness must still be tested in the actual setting, because laboratory evidence cannot determine the best policy for every community.

16. Nine lessons to keep

  1. Money-only self-interest is a clear model, but it is not a complete description of observed bargaining.
  2. An ultimatum rejection has an immediate personal cost and imposes a loss on the proposer.
  3. Dislike of unequal outcomes can explain some costly decisions.
  4. Perceived intentions can change how people respond to the same distribution.
  5. Concern for total welfare can differ from concern for strict equality.
  6. Costly punishment can support cooperation, but it also uses resources.
  7. Brain activity is evidence about processes, not proof of a moral judgment.
  8. Fairness judgments vary across social and cultural settings.
  9. Good institutions make both outcomes and procedures open to review.

A practical reader should ask three questions before applying this research: What behavior was measured? Which alternative explanations were tested? How similar is the real decision to the experimental task? These questions prevent a useful research finding from becoming an unsupported rule about every person or every society.

Knowledge check: five questions and answers

1. Why does a money-only model predict acceptance of a positive ultimatum offer?

Because a positive payment exceeds the zero payment from rejection, if the responder cares only about their own immediate money.

2. What is the difference between inequality aversion and reciprocity?

Inequality aversion concerns the distribution of outcomes. Reciprocity also responds to perceived intentions and actions.

3. What does a costly-punishment experiment test?

Whether people will spend their own resources to reduce another person's payoff after a perceived violation, and how this changes later cooperation.

4. Why do cross-cultural studies matter?

They test whether results from a narrow participant group generalize and show how social and economic settings may affect behavior.

5. Does a brain scan prove that rejecting an offer is morally correct?

No. Imaging can show activity associated with a decision. It cannot by itself establish the cause of a choice or the moral status of the outcome.
