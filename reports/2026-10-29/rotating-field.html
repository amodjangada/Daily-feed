How Can We Measure the Distance to a Star?

From a tiny change in angle to a measurement of the universe's expansion—and why the answers still disagree.

Executive summary

The distance to a star cannot usually be measured by sending an object to it, timing a radio echo, or placing a ruler beside it. Astronomers instead measure angles, changes in brightness and the spectra of light. The most direct stellar method is parallax: a nearby star appears to change position as Earth moves around the Sun. Its small apparent shift gives a distance by geometry. The European Space Agency’s Gaia mission used this method to map enormous numbers of stars, with careful corrections for the movement of stars and the spacecraft. [1] [2]

Parallax cannot reach every galaxy with useful precision. To measure greater distances, astronomers calibrate the real brightness of certain objects whose observed features reveal their luminosity. Cepheid variable stars have a relation between their pulse period and intrinsic brightness. Type Ia supernovae, after empirical corrections, are bright enough to measure much farther away. This linked set of measurements is called the cosmic distance ladder. Each step depends on a well-tested previous step. [3] [4]

The method has changed our view of the universe, but it also raises a live scientific dispute. Some local measurements of cosmic expansion give a Hubble constant near 73 kilometres per second per megaparsec, while a value inferred from the early universe under the standard cosmological model is about 67.4. Other local calibration methods give intermediate values. The difference is called the Hubble tension; its cause is not settled. This guide explains the measurement chain, its history, two transparent calculations, and what would count as strong evidence for a resolution. [8] [9] [10]

1. What does it mean to measure distance in space?

A measurement is useful only when we know what quantity it represents. A light-year is a unit of distance: the path light travels in one year. It is about 9.46 trillion kilometres. A parsec is about 3.26 light-years. A megaparsec, written Mpc, is one million parsecs. Astronomers use these units because ordinary kilometre figures become difficult to read. [2]

The distance to a nearby star can be treated as an ordinary spatial separation at the time of observation. For a distant galaxy, several distance definitions are possible in an expanding universe. Luminosity distance links an object's true power output to the light flux we receive. Angular-diameter distance links physical size to observed angular size. Redshift indicates how much the universe expanded while the light travelled. These measures differ at large redshift and should not be exchanged without a cosmological model.

An observed brightness is not a direct distance. A faint object could be weak and nearby, or very luminous and distant. Dust can absorb and redden its light. A star may also change its output. A reliable method needs an independent way to learn the true luminosity or a geometric measurement that does not require it. This is why the ladder begins with geometry.

2. From a small angle to other galaxies

In the nineteenth century, astronomers finally measured the annual parallax of nearby stars. The effect was predicted long before instruments could measure angles that small. It gave direct evidence that stars were at immense distances and made the size of our stellar neighbourhood measurable. Later instruments in space removed atmospheric distortion and could repeatedly observe the same stars across the sky.

A second breakthrough came from Henrietta Swan Leavitt. She studied photographic plates of variable stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud. In 1912 she reported that the longer-period variables were brighter. Because those stars lay at roughly the same distance from us, the relation could be detected without first knowing their exact distances. The relation alone gave relative luminosities. A separate geometric calibration was still needed to turn it into an absolute distance scale. Harvard's archival account and the original circular document the work. [3] [4]

Cepheids then helped establish that the Andromeda nebula lies outside the Milky Way. In the 1920s, work by Edwin Hubble and others connected galaxy distances with the observed shifts in their spectra. The resulting distance–redshift relation became a key observation behind the expanding-universe model. These advances were collective. They depended on Leavitt's measurement method, earlier spectroscopy, better telescopes, and the later interpretation of redshift.

The history matters because it shows two separate acts: discover a pattern, then calibrate it. A relation between pulse period and brightness does not by itself give a distance in kilometres. The distance scale is established by connecting that pattern to measurements grounded in geometry. That distinction still matters in current arguments about the expansion rate.

3. Parallax: the first geometric measurement

Imagine observing the same nearby star in January and July. Earth has moved to the other side of its orbit. The star appears in slightly different directions against a more distant background. Astronomers fit a repeating annual pattern to many measurements. The parallax angle is conventionally half the maximum angular shift across the six-month baseline. This definition is important: using the full shift in the standard formula would double the angle and halve the inferred distance.

One arcsecond is one three-thousand-six-hundredth of a degree. One milliarcsecond, or mas, is one thousandth of an arcsecond. For a star with a well-measured parallax p in arcseconds, the distance d in parsecs is approximately d = 1 / p. The geometry assumes the Earth–Sun distance is the baseline. A star at one parsec has a parallax of one arcsecond. A star at 100 parsecs has a parallax of 0.01 arcseconds, or 10 mas. [1]

A telescope does not simply take two images and subtract coordinates. Stars move through the galaxy. This real angular movement, called proper motion, adds to the repeating parallax pattern. The spacecraft also moves, the optical instrument has errors, and measurements have noise. Repeated observations over several years let scientists fit position, proper motion and parallax together. Gaia's global method compares observations across widely separated parts of the sky, with distant quasars helping define a stable reference. [1]

A small angle is difficult to measure. At a thousand parsecs the parallax is only one mas. At ten thousand parsecs it is one tenth of a mas. A systematic offset of even a few tens of microarcseconds can matter for distant or faint stars. ESA's documentation describes Gaia EDR3 parallax zero-point variations with magnitude, colour and position on the sky. Good analysis corrects known effects and carries remaining uncertainty forward. [5]

Worked example 1: a star's parallax

Illustrative values, not an actual Gaia record. Suppose a nearby star has a parallax of 20.0 mas with a measurement uncertainty of 0.2 mas, after relevant calibration corrections. Convert the angle to arcseconds: 20.0 mas = 0.0200 arcseconds. Then d = 1 / 0.0200 = 50 parsecs, or about 163 light-years.

The relative parallax uncertainty is 0.2 / 20.0 = 1%. For a small relative error, the relative distance uncertainty is approximately 1% too. So a first-order result is about 50 ± 0.5 parsecs. The exact interval from inverting 19.8 and 20.2 mas is slightly asymmetric. This example works because the measured angle is precise and positive.

Now suppose the parallax were 0.2 ± 0.3 mas. Direct inversion would produce a nominal distance of 5,000 parsecs, but that result would be misleading. The error is larger than the signal. Noise can even produce a negative fitted parallax, which does not mean a negative physical distance. Researchers instead use a statistical model that combines the measured angle, its error and reasonable prior information about stars. Luri and colleagues explain why naive inversion is unsafe for uncertain measurements. [6]

The lesson is broader than astronomy. A mathematical formula can be correct while a number inserted into it is too uncertain for the result to be useful. Before converting an observation into a distance, examine the signal-to-noise ratio and the calibration.

4. Brightness: how a calibrated star extends the reach

Light spreads across a larger area as it travels. For an isolated source in ordinary local space, observed flux is intrinsic luminosity divided by 4π times the distance squared. If two identical sources are at distances in the ratio 1:2, the farther one appears one-quarter as bright. But the key word is identical. We need to know the intrinsic power output before using the inverse-square law to calculate distance.

Astronomers use a logarithmic brightness scale called magnitude. Apparent magnitude describes observed brightness; absolute magnitude describes how bright an object would look at a standard distance of ten parsecs. The distance modulus is m − M = 5 log10(d / 10 pc), before adding corrections such as extinction by dust. A difference of five magnitudes corresponds to a factor of 100 in flux. The formula is compact, but every calibration and dust correction must be checked.

Cepheids are useful because their pulse period provides information about their intrinsic luminosity. They are not all equally bright. In a well-calibrated period–luminosity relation, a longer-period Cepheid is usually more luminous. Astronomers measure its changing light, infer the period, estimate its true brightness from the calibrated relation, and compare that with its apparent brightness. The result is a distance estimate. [3] [7]

The relation needs careful treatment. Different Cepheid populations do not necessarily share identical calibrations. Chemical composition can affect their light. Dust changes colours and brightness. In crowded galaxies, neighbouring stars can add light to the measured source and make it appear too bright, which biases its distance downward. Observations in several wavelengths and higher spatial resolution help separate these effects. NASA describes how Webb observations test the crowding problem that affected some Hubble measurements. [7]

5. Why independent geometric anchors matter

The ladder becomes more reliable when its first calibration comes from more than one kind of geometry. Gaia measures parallax in our galaxy. Astronomers also measure the orbit of stars in detached eclipsing binary systems, where repeated eclipses and orbital velocities can constrain physical size and distance. Another method uses water masers—intense natural microwave emission—in the disk around the central black hole of galaxy NGC 4258.

In NGC 4258, astronomers observe the angular positions, orbital speeds and accelerations of maser spots. A model of their rotating disk gives a physical scale, which can be compared with the measured angular scale. Reid, Pesce and Riess reported a geometric distance of 7.576 Mpc with separate statistical and systematic errors in 2019. That distance can calibrate Cepheids and other stellar indicators in the same galaxy. [11]

This is a powerful test because the underlying physics differs from stellar brightness calibration. A parallax is an angular change caused by the observer's motion. A maser distance comes from orbital dynamics. If both anchors lead to compatible calibrations, it becomes less likely that a single geometric error controls the result. But agreement is not absolute proof: instruments, sample choices and the later supernova calibration can still introduce shared errors.

The scientific value of an independent anchor is therefore specific. It narrows a class of explanations. It does not make the entire ladder free of assumptions. A good paper shows the uncertainty contributed by each rung and explains how errors are correlated.

6. Supernovae carry the scale farther

A Type Ia supernova is a thermonuclear stellar explosion. These events are exceptionally luminous and can be detected in galaxies far beyond the practical reach of individual Cepheid observations. Their peak brightnesses are similar but not identical. Researchers standardize them using observed light-curve shape, colour and other corrections. Calling them perfect identical candles would hide the central calibration work.

The link is made in galaxies that contain both Cepheids and a recorded Type Ia supernova. Cepheids estimate the host galaxy's distance. The supernova's apparent brightness at that known distance then helps establish the supernova luminosity scale. Once calibrated, Type Ia supernovae in more distant galaxies can provide distances even when their Cepheids cannot be observed. NASA and ESA show this sequence in their distance-ladder explanation. [7] [12]

The method depends on the sample. Astronomers must consider how supernova properties change with host galaxy, dust, detection limits and redshift. They also correct for selection: at greater distances, brighter events are easier to find than dimmer events. A calibration based on the detected population can be biased if it ignores the events that the survey missed.

At sufficiently large distances, the expanding universe also changes the relation between flux, redshift and distance. The calculation then uses a cosmological model, rather than treating the entire universe as a static Euclidean space. Local expansion measurements use a redshift range where a calibrated relation can be fitted, with corrections for the galaxies' motions relative to the average expansion.

7. The Hubble constant and why scientists disagree

The Hubble constant, H₀, is the present-day rate at which the scale of the universe expands. Its conventional unit is kilometres per second per megaparsec. Near the present epoch and at modest distances, a galaxy's recession speed is approximately H₀ times its distance, after accounting for local motions. The unit is a rate per distance, not a claim that every galaxy moves away from us at the same speed.

A local distance ladder estimates H₀ by measuring distances to galaxies and relating those distances to redshifts. A different approach measures patterns in the cosmic microwave background, light from the early universe. The Planck collaboration inferred H₀ = 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s/Mpc in its standard six-parameter ΛCDM model, published in 2020. This is a model-dependent inference, not a direct parallax-like measurement of today's expansion. [8]

A 2025 paper led by Adam Riess used Hubble and Webb Cepheid observations and reported a local result around 73.5 km/s/Mpc, with an uncertainty below 1 km/s/Mpc for the quoted combination. The team argued that improved resolution did not reveal the hidden crowding bias required to explain the discrepancy. Their evidence makes one specific proposed explanation less likely, but it does not eliminate every possible calibration problem. [9]

A different team led by Wendy Freedman reported a 2025 TRGB-based estimate of H₀ = 70.39 km/s/Mpc, with separate statistical, systematic and supernova contributions to uncertainty. Their methods also examined J-region asymptotic giant branch stars. These results are closer to the Planck value than the higher Cepheid-based result, and the authors did not find compelling evidence that new physics was required by their own data. Different teams' data, analysis choices and calibrations need to be compared carefully. [10]

The existence of a discrepancy is real in the sense that published methods return different estimates. Its statistical significance and cause depend on which results and correlated errors are included. Scientists have proposed residual measurement biases, unusual local motions, changes to early-universe physics and modifications to the standard cosmological model. None should be presented as established solely because the headline values differ.

Worked example 2: interpreting expansion rates

Illustrative calculation, not a measurement of a particular galaxy. Consider a galaxy at a distance of 100 Mpc. If H₀ = 67.4 km/s/Mpc, the simple nearby-universe relation predicts a recession contribution of about 6,740 km/s. If H₀ = 73.5 km/s/Mpc, it predicts about 7,350 km/s. The difference is 610 km/s, or roughly 9.1% of the lower prediction.

This does not mean that one individual galaxy can settle the dispute. Its measured spectral shift includes motion relative to nearby galaxies, and at greater redshift the simple linear formula becomes an approximation. Scientists fit many galaxies, model local motions, standardize the light sources, and estimate uncertainties. They also ask whether the sample covers enough sky and distance to avoid a local environmental bias.

A useful way to read a Hubble-constant paper is to ask three questions: What anchored the absolute distance scale? What astronomical objects transferred that calibration to distant galaxies? What assumptions converted observed redshifts into a present-day expansion rate? If the paper answers all three, a reader can locate where a new observation might change the conclusion.

8. The strongest competing interpretations

Interpretation A: a genuine change to cosmology may be needed. The case is that several precise local measurements remain above the standard-model inference from the early universe. New Webb images test one important weakness of Hubble Cepheid observations—crowded star fields—and still support the higher local scale in analyses by the Riess team. Independent probes, such as strong gravitational lens time delays, can add evidence without using exactly the same calibration chain. If the difference persists across independent, well-calibrated methods, the standard model may be missing a physical effect. [9] [13]

Interpretation B: remaining measurement and model choices deserve priority. The case is that distance indicators are not interchangeable and their errors are difficult to estimate. Red-giant-tip and JAGB measurements use different stellar populations and can yield lower values than some Cepheid analyses. Supernova sample selection, dust corrections, instrument calibration, population differences and shared anchors can alter the final value. The 2025 Freedman analysis illustrates why independent methods are still essential before declaring the standard model broken. [10]

Both interpretations make testable claims. The first predicts that increasingly independent measurements will converge on a value incompatible with the standard early-universe inference, even after known systematics are reduced. The second predicts that better calibration, larger samples or more complete uncertainty models will narrow the disagreement. New physics must also explain other observations, including the microwave background, structure growth and light-element abundances. A change that fixes only H₀ while damaging other successful predictions is not a complete solution.

There is a further distinction: a result can be statistically precise but inaccurate. Precision means repeated estimates have small spread. Accuracy means the estimate is close to the true quantity. A hidden common calibration error can make several measurements agree tightly with one another while all share a bias. Conversely, different methods may disagree because they measure slightly different populations or use different correction models.

9. What remains uncertain?

For parallax, the main questions include instrument calibration, colour-dependent offsets, correlations across the sky, and the statistical treatment of low-signal measurements. For Cepheids, they include dust, chemical composition, population selection and blending with neighbouring stars. For supernovae, they include host-galaxy dependence, the standardization model, calibration transfer between instruments and survey selection. Each issue has a research literature; none should be treated as a vague universal objection.

A key challenge is error propagation. Suppose an anchor distance is biased by 1%. A later luminosity calibration based on that anchor will inherit a related bias. Measuring thousands of supernovae can reduce random scatter, but it does not automatically remove an error shared by every object. Scientists therefore separate statistical uncertainty from systematic uncertainty, publish covariance models, and use independent calibration routes.

Another challenge is inference from the early universe. The Planck result is extremely informative under ΛCDM, but its value of today's H₀ comes from fitting a model to ancient light. Changing the model can change the inferred value. Yet a replacement model must match a broad set of measurements. This is why the Hubble tension is an active research problem rather than a simple disagreement between two telescopes.

The strongest future evidence will combine better geometry, improved Cepheid and red-giant observations, well-characterized supernova samples, independent lensing and standard-siren methods, and cross-checks against large-scale cosmology. Agreement across methods with different errors is more persuasive than repeating one method at greater precision.

10. How to read a cosmic-distance claim

When a news report says a star is a certain number of light-years away, identify the method. Was its parallax measured directly? Was a stellar model used? Is the quoted value a median with a wide probability interval? A precise-looking number without an error range may be a poor summary of the actual knowledge.

When a report gives a distance to another galaxy, ask which standard candle or other indicator was used. If the estimate depends on brightness, check how dust and stellar population were handled. If the galaxy is very distant, ask whether the distance is luminosity distance, angular-diameter distance, or a model-derived comoving distance. These are not synonyms.

When a report claims the universe expands faster than expected, check whether it compares a local distance-ladder measurement with a model inference from the early universe. Then inspect the quoted uncertainties, the paper's publication date, and the authors' treatment of shared errors. A difference between two central values is not itself a probability that a model is false.

Finally, keep the chain of reasoning visible. A raw angle becomes a distance through geometry. A calibrated period becomes a luminosity through a relation. A luminosity and observed flux become another distance. A set of distances and redshifts constrains expansion. Every arrow in this chain is a place to test, improve or challenge the result.

Eleven lessons worth retaining

  1. Parallax is an apparent position shift caused by a change in the observer's viewpoint.
  2. The standard parallax angle is half the maximum six-month displacement.
  3. One parsec corresponds to one arcsecond of parallax.
  4. High-uncertainty parallaxes should not simply be inverted into distances.
  5. Brightness alone cannot distinguish a faint nearby star from a luminous distant star.
  6. Leavitt's law connects a Cepheid's pulse period with intrinsic luminosity.
  7. Geometric anchors set the absolute scale for stellar brightness methods.
  8. Type Ia supernovae require standardization; they are not perfectly identical explosions.
  9. The distance ladder carries calibration uncertainty from one rung to the next.
  10. Early-universe estimates of H₀ depend on the cosmological model used.
  11. The Hubble tension remains an unresolved scientific question, with meaningful competing measurements.

Five knowledge questions and answers

1. Why does parallax decrease for more distant stars?
With the same Earth–Sun baseline, a greater distance produces a smaller viewing angle. For a precise positive parallax in arcseconds, distance in parsecs is approximately its reciprocal.
2. Why could Leavitt discover a period–brightness relation before knowing the stars' exact distances?
Her sample was in the Small Magellanic Cloud. The stars were at approximately the same distance, so differences in apparent brightness largely reflected differences in intrinsic luminosity. An absolute calibration still had to be added.
3. What is the role of a galaxy that contains both Cepheids and a Type Ia supernova?
Its Cepheids provide a distance. That distance helps calibrate the supernova's standardized luminosity. Similar supernovae in more distant galaxies can then extend the scale.
4. Why can more observations fail to fix a systematic error?
Repeated measurements reduce random scatter, but an error shared by all measurements, such as a calibration offset, can remain. Independent anchors and error models are needed.
5. Does the Hubble tension prove that the standard cosmological model is wrong?
No. Some local results disagree strongly with the standard-model early-universe inference, while other local calibrations give intermediate values. The cause, full significance and best interpretation remain under study.
