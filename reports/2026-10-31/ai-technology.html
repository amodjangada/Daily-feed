Can We Prove Where an AI-Era Image Came From?

How signed records, hidden watermarks and image detectors help trace media—and why they cannot prove the truth of a scene.

Research checked 31 October 2026 · Original source dates listed below · Examples are illustrative

Executive summary

AI-generated images have made visual evidence harder to assess. A realistic picture may come from a camera, a model, a composite or a later edit. Three technical approaches can help, but each answers a different question. Signed provenance records, including C2PA Content Credentials, can show who signed a claim and whether the bound file or record changed. Hidden watermarks can help identify output from a participating generator. Statistical detectors estimate patterns associated with generated images. Research shows that watermarks can survive some edits, while other studies demonstrate removal and forgery attacks. A valid signature does not prove that a pictured event happened. Missing credentials do not prove that an image is synthetic. The strongest method combines a documented creation history, independent evidence and clear error reporting. This guide explains how signatures, hashes, watermarks and classifiers work; what original experiments establish; and where each method fails. Two illustrative cases show how to interpret a signed camera record and how a low false-positive rate can still produce many wrong alerts. The aim is a defensible judgment with stated uncertainty, not an automatic true-or-false label.

1. The central question

When a picture reaches a news editor, an employer or a family group, can a machine show who made it and how it changed? AI systems can produce convincing images without a camera. Ordinary editing can also change real photographs. Visual appearance alone often cannot settle a dispute. The useful question is therefore narrower than “Is this picture fake?” It is: which parts of its recorded history can we verify, and which parts remain unknown?

There are three main approaches. A signed record can describe the source and edits. A hidden watermark can carry a signal within the pixels. A detector can estimate whether an image resembles known AI output. Each approach answers a different question. None independently proves that the event shown actually happened. This guide explains their mechanics, the research behind them and the failure modes that matter in practice. [3]

2. Terms that prevent false certainty

Provenance is a record of where a digital item came from and what happened to it. A manifest is a structured set of statements about an item. A digital signature is a cryptographic check made with a private key; a verifier uses a related public key to test whether the signed statement changed. A hash is a short value computed from data. A small change to the bound data normally changes its hash.

A watermark is a signal inserted into content so software can later detect it. It can be visible or hidden. A fingerprint is calculated from content that already exists, often to find near copies. A classifier is a statistical model that assigns a label or score. False positive means a system marks a genuine item as AI-made or watermarked. False negative means it misses an item that should be detected. These errors have different costs. [2]

3. How digital trust developed

Digital signatures were used to protect files and messages long before modern image generators. Newsrooms and camera makers also stored metadata such as capture time, device and edit history. Such metadata can help, but ordinary text fields can be changed without leaving reliable evidence. A signed record adds tamper evidence and a link to a signing identity. It still depends on how the record was created.

The growth of generative AI increased demand for media provenance. The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity, or C2PA, developed a standard for signed content records. Its version 2.2 technical specification is dated May 2025. Meanwhile, research teams developed watermarks that are added during image generation rather than afterward. NIST's 20 November 2024 report examined provenance, labeling, watermarking and detection as related technical methods. The field has moved from one detection score toward a layered evidence system. [1] [3]

4. Step one: a device makes a claim

A camera or image editor can create a C2PA manifest. The manifest contains assertions: for example, that a file was captured by a named device class, or that an editor applied a crop. The software can also record ingredients, meaning source files used to make the new file. These are statements from a particular system. The system must have a reliable process for recording them.

Consider the difference between two claims. “This camera captured these pixels” is a claim about an operation. “This picture shows a real flood in a named city” is a claim about the world. A valid camera signature can support the first statement when the capture system is trustworthy. It does not automatically establish the second. The device might photograph a printed image, a screen or a staged scene. Provenance gives a stronger audit trail; it cannot remove the need to check the subject and context. [2]

5. Step two: bind the record to the file

After the claims are assembled, the software computes information that binds the manifest to the media. A cryptographic hash can serve as a hard binding. The system signs the claim with a private key. A later verifier can test the signature and the binding. If the protected data or claims change in a way the standard does not permit, validation should fail.

This is stronger than an unsigned “camera model” field in a file. It is still a statement about a specific digital asset. Exporting a new image may change the pixels, encoding and hash. A compatible editor can create a new signed manifest that describes the transformation and links to an earlier ingredient. If an application removes the record, the file can lose its readily visible history. That loss is a gap in evidence, not a proof that the file was generated by AI. [1]

6. Step three: validate the signer and chain

A verifier first checks whether the manifest is well formed. It then tests the digital signature and the asset binding. It may also check certificates, time stamps, revocation status and the stated history of ingredient files. The verifier must decide which signing identities it trusts. A technically valid signature from an unknown signer is different from one linked to a trusted capture system.

The trust chain matters because cryptography protects a statement after it is signed; it does not force the signer to tell the truth. A compromised device, a weak identity check or a dishonest signing service can produce a misleading but technically valid claim. The C2PA standard distinguishes validity from trust. A responsible reader should see the signer, the declared actions and any validation warnings. A simple green check without explanation can make the evidence appear stronger than it is. [1] [2]

7. Step four: put a signal inside the image

Watermarking follows a different path. Instead of keeping the main evidence in a signed manifest, a generator places a signal into the image itself. An extractor later looks for that signal. A robust design tries to survive common changes such as resizing, compression or cropping while keeping the mark hard for people to notice.

In the Tree-Rings study, researchers placed a structured pattern into the initial noise used by a diffusion image generator. Detection attempted to reverse the generation process and recover the signal. The Stable Signature study changed a latent diffusion model's decoder so its outputs carried a binary signature. These are different methods with different assumptions. Both show that generated pixels can contain information about the generating system. Neither method makes the resulting image a verified record of a real event. [4] [5]

8. Step five: test what happens after edits

Real images travel through messaging apps, social platforms and editing tools. A watermark that works on untouched files may fail after a crop, color change, compression or generative edit. Researchers therefore test a watermark against a set of transformations. They measure how often a detector finds the mark in changed images and how often it reports a mark in images that never had one.

The 2024 ZoDiac paper used a diffusion model to place watermarks in a latent representation and tested several image collections. Its authors reported a detection rate above 98% with a false-positive rate below 6.4% under their evaluated conditions. These are research results for a defined method and test setup. They do not mean every watermark in the world has the same performance. Even strong average results can hide a failure on a specific editing path or image type. [6]

9. Step six: consider removal and forgery

A useful threat model asks what an attacker can do. Can they crop or re-encode an image? Can they run a different image generator? Can they collect examples of marked output? Can they change the application that embeds the mark? These abilities matter because a mark can be removed, weakened or copied. A forged mark can also create false attribution.

Müller and colleagues described black-box attacks on semantic watermarks in a December 2024 preprint. Their experiments used unrelated diffusion models to forge or remove certain watermark signals, sometimes starting from one marked reference image. This is important counterevidence to claims of universal robustness. The study does not show that all watermark methods fail in every setting. It shows why an adversarial test must include forgery as well as removal, and why independent checks remain necessary. [7]

10. Diagram: three evidence paths

Read the diagram as a decision process. A manifest offers a recorded history. A watermark offers a possible signal of origin. A detector offers a probability-like score only if its calibration is known. If these checks disagree, the answer is to inspect why they disagree, not to average them into a single unexplained number.

12. Worked example one: a verified camera record

Illustrative workflow, not a documented incident. A reporter receives a photograph with a C2PA manifest. The verifier finds a valid signature from a known camera system and a valid binding to the file. The record shows capture, then a crop by an editor that produced a second signed version. The reporter can state that the two recorded operations have a verifiable chain, assuming the signers and capture process are trustworthy.

Now suppose the accompanying message says the image was taken yesterday during a protest. The manifest has no reliable location assertion, and the device clock was not independently checked. The reporter cannot infer the protest date from the signature alone. They seek another source: a photographer interview, a distinct image, a landmark or a public record. The verified history narrows the investigation, but the caption still needs proof. This is the practical difference between file authenticity and factual truth. [2]

13. Worked example two: a rare false alarm

Illustrative arithmetic; these values are assumptions, not a published detector result. Suppose a service reviews 10,000 images. Assume 1,000 are generated and 9,000 are camera-made. A detector catches 90% of generated images and falsely flags 2% of camera images. It flags 900 generated images (1,000 × 0.90) and 180 genuine images (9,000 × 0.02). Its total flagged count is 1,080.

Of those 1,080 alerts, 900 are correct: 900 ÷ 1,080 = 83.3%. Thus 16.7% of alerts are wrong, despite a 2% false-positive rate on genuine images. If generated images fall to 1% of the incoming stream, the share of false alarms rises sharply under the same rates. The lesson is that detection precision depends on the base rate, meaning how common generated content is in the actual population. A vendor's single accuracy score cannot replace this calculation.

14. Why an AI-image classifier is a different tool

A classifier learns statistical differences between examples labeled as real and generated. It may find patterns in texture, compression or model-specific artifacts. It does not read a signed account of what happened. A confident score can be wrong when a new generator, editing process or camera changes the distribution of images. Testing only on one familiar dataset can overstate field performance.

For a meaningful deployment, a team should separate training and test sources, include new models and real cameras, and report false-positive and false-negative rates by image type. It should test altered and low-quality files because those are common in practice. It should also avoid presenting a raw score as a calibrated probability unless calibration has been measured. NIST's review identifies evaluation and auditing as necessary parts of synthetic-content transparency, alongside labeling and provenance. [3]

15. What missing credentials mean

Many files do not carry a manifest. Some devices never created one. Some services remove metadata during upload. A screenshot may preserve the visible pixels but not the original signature. A copied image may be separated from its signed history. The C2PA architecture also describes soft bindings and repositories that can help recover associated records in some circumstances. Recovery depends on compatible systems and available records.

The key logical rule is simple: no credential does not mean AI-generated, and a credential does not mean truthful. Treat an absent credential as unknown provenance. A signed manifest from a trusted source can provide positive evidence of a recorded process. It is not a universal test for all other images. Any product that turns “credential missing” into a red “fake” label risks harming ordinary photographers and small publishers. [2]

16. Competing views: detection first or provenance first?

One approach prioritizes provenance. Its supporters argue that recording origin at capture or creation provides a clearer audit trail than trying to guess from pixels later. This approach is strongest when devices, editors and platforms preserve compatible signed records. Its main weakness is incomplete adoption. An adversary can distribute content through channels that do not preserve the records, and an honest creator may use older tools.

A second approach invests in watermarking and statistical detection because it can examine material after distribution, including files with no visible history. The research on Tree-Rings, Stable Signature and ZoDiac supports useful technical capabilities under tested conditions. The forgery research shows important security limits. A third view gives priority to context checks and trusted reporting, because technical origin evidence alone cannot settle factual claims. The evidence supports combining these methods and stating uncertainty. [3] [4] [7]

17. Wider effects on journalism, business and rights

Newsrooms can use signed records to document editing and reduce time spent tracing a file. Businesses can use them to show how product media were made. Courts and investigators may find a verified record useful, but must still consider the device, chain of custody and context. Public institutions can reduce false accusations by explaining what their tools actually verify.

There are costs. Signed histories can reveal location, identity or work patterns that a creator did not intend to publish. Systems that rely on a few trusted certificate issuers may disadvantage small publishers or people using older equipment. Watermark requirements may be hard to enforce on open tools or modified generators. A system must balance transparency with privacy and access. These are governance choices, not properties that a cryptographic signature can settle on its own. [1] [3]

18. A practical verification protocol

First, preserve the original file rather than only a screenshot. Second, inspect any manifest with a validator that explains the signer, claims, binding and warnings. Third, compare the stated creation and edit history with the story attached to the image. Fourth, use a watermark or classifier only with documented error rates and a known threat model. Fifth, look for independent support for the event or claim. Record which parts are verified and which remain uncertain.

For a high-impact decision, include a route for review and correction. A false accusation of fabrication can damage a person as much as an undetected generated image can mislead an audience. Test the process on actual files from the intended channels, including compressed images and those without credentials. Review it again when tools and attackers change. This procedure follows the layered evaluation logic in the NIST assessment and the limits shown by watermark experiments. [3] [7]

Key lessons

  1. Ask what a tool verifies: a signed claim, a signal or a statistical pattern.
  2. A valid signature protects a claim against undetected change; it does not establish that the claim is true.
  3. Keep the original media file when checking provenance.
  4. Missing credentials mean that origin evidence is missing, not that the image is AI-generated.
  5. Watermark tests must cover ordinary edits, deliberate removal and deliberate forgery.
  6. Report false-positive rates and the base rate before using detector alerts.
  7. Treat a watermark score and a C2PA signature as different kinds of evidence.
  8. Use independent sources to verify the event shown, even when the file history is sound.
  9. Give people a way to challenge consequential errors.

Five questions and answers

1. A file has no Content Credentials. Is it AI-generated?

No. The file may come from a device or service that did not create or preserve a manifest. The correct conclusion is that this provenance channel gives no answer.

2. A camera signature validates. Does the photo show a real event?

It supports a claim about the signed capture process, subject to trust in the signer and device. It does not establish the truth of the caption or rule out a staged scene.

3. Why can a watermark survive a crop yet fail against an attacker?

A watermark can be designed to resist a known class of image changes. An attacker may use a different model or exploit the watermark's structure. Robustness is specific to the tested threat.

4. If a detector has a 2% false-positive rate, are only 2% of its alerts wrong?

No. Two percent is the share of genuine images it flags. The share of wrong alerts also depends on how many genuine and generated images enter the system.

5. What is the safest decision when tools disagree?

Keep the original file, inspect the provenance and detector conditions, seek independent context, and record uncertainty. Do not collapse conflicting evidence into an unexplained single score.
