Executive summary

Factories use heat to wash, dry, pasteurise, cook, and process materials. Much of this heat still comes from burning fuels. An industrial heat pump uses electricity to move heat from a lower-temperature source to a hotter process. Under suitable conditions, one unit of electricity can deliver several units of useful heat. This can reduce fuel purchases and emissions, but the result depends on the required temperature, waste-heat supply, electricity tariff, grid emissions, and the cost of installing the system. The International Energy Agency's July 2026 Heat Pump Monitor estimates that commercially available systems could technically meet around one-fifth of global industrial heat demand. Actual deployment remains much smaller. A factory also needs reliable production, safe refrigerants, maintenance skills, and a sound plan for backup heat. This guide explains the engineering mechanism, temperature limits, project economics, evidence, and competing choices. It includes two illustrative calculations: an energy-cost comparison and an emissions break-even test. The aim is to show when a heat pump is a practical investment, and when another approach may work better.

Central question and decision rule

Central question: Can a factory supply its required process heat with an electric heat pump at lower total cost and lower greenhouse-gas emissions than its fuel boiler, without increasing production risk?

Answer in order. Measure useful heat and its temperature. Find a dependable lower-temperature source. Estimate real annual efficiency. Compare total ownership costs, including electrical upgrades. Then calculate emissions using a stated electricity factor. Finally, test production reliability and safety. A project that fails one critical requirement is not ready for approval.

This question connects to earlier reports on Scope 3 emissions, data-centre water use, and green hydrogen. Here the focus is direct heat inside a factory. Corporate targets alone cannot determine which equipment is suitable.

1. Why industrial heat is hard to replace

Industrial heating has long relied on coal, oil, gas, and steam networks. Boilers are familiar, can serve many production lines, and often provide high temperatures quickly. Factories have built pipework, controls, and safety systems around them. Changing the heat source can affect production, not only the utility bill.

Heat pumps have served refrigeration and building heating for decades. A refrigerator moves heat away from food. An industrial heat pump uses the same basic cycle but delivers upgraded heat to a process. Warm wastewater, cooling circuits, and exhaust streams can become useful heat sources.

The IEA's Future of Heat Pumps, published 30 November 2022, identified near-term opportunities in paper, food, and chemicals. It described industrial machines supplying heat up to about 140–160°C in suitable applications, while common installations served lower temperatures. Those figures describe technology, not guaranteed output at every factory. [1]

The IEA's 28 July 2026 Heat Pump Monitor estimated that commercial systems could technically serve up to around 20% of global industrial heat demand. Actual deployment is far smaller. This gap is central to the business question: a machine can be technically capable yet too expensive or difficult to integrate. [2]

2. Terms that must be defined

Process heat is thermal energy used to make or treat a product. Useful heat reaches the process at the required temperature. Source temperature is the temperature of the stream from which a heat pump takes energy. Sink temperature is its heat delivery temperature. Temperature lift is the difference between them.

Coefficient of performance (COP) is useful heat output divided by electricity input over a defined period. A COP of three means three units of heat per unit of electricity. Annual COP includes the variation over a year and is more useful than a best-case test result. Boiler efficiency is useful heat output divided by fuel input.

Load factor measures how much a machine operates relative to its rated capacity. Heat recovery uses heat that would otherwise be rejected. Refrigerant is the working fluid in the machine. Its climate effect and safety properties matter. Net present value compares future costs and savings after accounting for the time value of money.

3. How a heat pump works in six steps

Step 1: A heat exchanger transfers energy from warm water, a cooling circuit, ambient air, or another source into the refrigerant. Source availability must match production hours.

Step 2: The refrigerant absorbs heat and evaporates at low pressure. The equipment keeps it separate from the product stream.

Step 3: An electric compressor raises the vapour's pressure and temperature. This usually takes most of the system's electricity.

Step 4: A condenser transfers heat from the refrigerant to process water, thermal fluid, or a suitable steam system. The delivery temperature limits the applications.

Step 5: An expansion device lowers the refrigerant pressure, allowing it to absorb heat again. Controls adjust the cycle as conditions change.

Step 6: Pumps, valves, storage, meters, backup heat, and the electrical supply connect the heat pump to production. A high laboratory COP has little value if the factory cannot use the heat when it is available.

5. Why temperature is the first constraint

A factory may need warm cleaning water, moderate-temperature drying, steam, and very high-temperature treatment. Map these needs separately. A machine efficient at moderate temperature may be inefficient when asked to deliver much hotter steam. Some processes can be redesigned to use lower temperatures, which may save more than buying a larger machine.

The IEA's 2026 review reports commercial industrial operation below 120°C and emerging commercial operation below 160°C. Higher-temperature applications are at different stages of development. These are broad technology ranges, not fixed physical limits. The buyer must check supplier guarantees at the actual source and delivery conditions. [2]

Separate the steady load from occasional peaks. A plant might need a short high-temperature cleaning cycle but operate at a lower temperature for most hours. A hybrid system can use the heat pump for the steady load and a boiler for peaks. This can reduce fuel use without forcing the compressor outside its efficient range.

A small temperature lift often helps efficiency. Recovering heat from a warm process stream is usually easier than taking heat from cold air and making high-temperature steam. The plant's heat map therefore comes before equipment selection.

6. Find reliable sources of recoverable heat

Food factories can have refrigeration equipment, warm cleaning water, and simultaneous cooling and heating needs. Paper mills may have warm process water or exhaust streams. Chemical plants may reject heat from one operation while buying fuel for another. The best match depends on temperature, flow, contamination risk, and timing.

Measure source heat by hour or shift. A wastewater stream may disappear during shutdown or cleaning. If source heat and process demand do not overlap, storage or backup heat is necessary. Seasonal air temperatures can also change performance. Use actual logs instead of assuming constant availability.

An original research article by Ahrens and colleagues studied heat recovery in a food-processing refrigeration system with both cold and hot demands. It shows why combined heating and cooling deserve a system-level analysis. The paper appeared in a 2024 journal issue, and its online English version was posted 29 September 2025. The specific plant configuration cannot be copied into a different factory without new engineering. [3]

7. Why a brochure COP can mislead

Suppliers report efficiency at specified source and sink temperatures. Real plants vary. The source cools, demand changes, compressors cycle, and pumps or fans consume electricity. Some ratings exclude auxiliary loads. A single high COP does not establish good annual performance.

Request a performance map showing electricity input and useful heat output at several temperatures and load levels. Weight each point by expected operating hours. Include pumps, controls, standby power, and maintenance. Meter useful heat at the process boundary. Heat made but rejected or lost is not useful output.

The IEA Heat Pumping Technologies Annex 58 Task 4 report, published 16 April 2024, gives recommendations for commercial performance specifications and testing. Its final report, dated 26 November 2024, reviews high-temperature technology and industrial integration. These reports support clear contract conditions and factory acceptance tests. [4] [5]

Annual COP can improve when a factory lowers its required supply temperature or raises its source temperature through better heat recovery. These changes may be cheaper than buying a higher-rated compressor.

8. What global evidence shows, and what it does not

The IEA's July 2026 monitor estimates that commercial heat pumps could technically serve around one-fifth of industrial heat demand. Japan had nearly 650 MW of installed industrial heat-pump capacity in 2024, enough for about 1% of light-industry heat demand. The figures show that industrial adoption is growing but remains limited. They do not establish a typical payback period. [2]

Under the IEA Stated Policies Scenario, heat pumps could supply around 8% of light-industry heat demand by 2035, compared with below 1% in 2025. Its Net Zero Emissions Scenario reaches around 20%. These are scenarios based on policy and technology assumptions, not measured future results. [2]

The IEA's Renewables 2023 heat chapter projected more electricity use for industrial process heat. That was a forecast at publication, not observed 2026 consumption. It also warned that growing industrial heat demand could offset some clean-energy progress. [6]

A June 2026 IEA chart compares the lifetime cost of industrial electrification at different electricity-to-fuel price ratios. It models heat pumps for processes up to 150°C. Its assumptions are useful for framing decisions, but they cannot replace a local engineering quote. [7]

9. Worked example one: annual energy cost

Illustrative data only. A factory needs 1,000 MWh of useful heat each year. Its gas boiler has 85% efficiency. The assumed gas price is ₹5 per kWh of fuel energy. A proposed heat pump has annual COP 3.2, and electricity costs ₹8 per kWh.

The boiler needs 1,000 ÷ 0.85 = 1,176.5 MWh of gas. Its energy cost is about 1,176,470 kWh × ₹5 = ₹58.82 lakh. The heat pump needs 1,000 ÷ 3.2 = 312.5 MWh of electricity, costing 312,500 kWh × ₹8 = ₹25 lakh. The indicative energy-cost saving is ₹33.82 lakh per year.

This excludes equipment, finance, grid upgrades, maintenance, auxiliary loads, downtime, backup fuel, and demand charges. It assumes identical useful heat output. It is not a forecast or a real Indian tariff. A company must use its own accounts and supplier guarantees.

10. How to evaluate the investment

Lower energy cost does not guarantee a profitable project. The heat pump may require new piping, heat exchangers, a transformer, controls, storage, and specialist maintenance. A low load factor can leave expensive equipment idle. Production downtime during installation may be material.

Compare the full ownership cost of the heat pump with a credible alternative over the same service life. Include installation, financing, maintenance, refrigerant management, backup, and replacement. Use net present value and test different fuel prices, electricity tariffs, operating hours, and annual COP values. A project that works only under optimistic assumptions is fragile.

Some plants can save more by combining refrigeration and heating. Recovering heat already rejected by a cooling system may reduce fuel purchases. But count the site's total electricity and fuel use before and after. Do not count the same heat recovery benefit twice.

The IEA's 2025 cost-ratio analysis used illustrative efficiencies of 350% for heat pumps and 87% for gas boilers, with stated regional prices. Those values are modelling assumptions, not guaranteed plant performance. [8]

11. Emissions need a clear boundary

A heat pump serving a process replaces some direct fuel combustion with electricity. The physical emissions result depends on the electricity used and the fuel baseline. Grid-average and marginal electricity factors answer different questions. An average describes a system's mix; a marginal factor estimates which generation changes when demand changes.

Refrigerant leakage can add greenhouse-gas emissions. Equipment manufacturing and installation also have impacts. Their size varies with refrigerant type, machine lifetime, utilisation, and electricity source. Include relevant impacts when comparing complete systems.

Separate physical changes from accounting claims. Buying renewable electricity certificates may change a company's reported emissions under an accepted method. It does not alone prove that every additional hour of electricity was generated without emissions. State the method, period, and factor. A good report distinguishes measured energy savings from modelled emissions savings.

12. Worked example two: the emissions threshold

Illustrative data only. For 1,000 kWh of useful heat, assume a boiler efficiency of 85%, direct fuel emissions of 0.20 kg CO₂ per kWh of fuel, heat-pump COP 3.2, and electricity emissions of 0.70 kg CO₂ per kWh.

Boiler fuel input is 1,000 ÷ 0.85 = 1,176.5 kWh. Direct boiler emissions are 235.3 kg CO₂. Heat-pump electricity input is 1,000 ÷ 3.2 = 312.5 kWh. Electricity emissions are 218.8 kg CO₂. The limited comparison favours the heat pump by about 16.5 kg CO₂.

The break-even electricity factor is 235.3 ÷ 312.5 = 0.753 kg CO₂/kWh. Above this factor, the heat pump has higher operating emissions under the stated assumptions. This omits upstream fuel emissions, refrigerant leaks, and manufacturing. All factors are invented for teaching and are not current Indian grid or gas data.

13. Strong alternatives and hybrid designs

Improve the boiler first. Better insulation, controls, condensate return, and heat recovery can save fuel with lower capital cost. These steps may be best when source heat is weak or the required temperature is too high for an available heat pump. They do not end fuel combustion.

Use direct electric heating. Resistance boilers and heaters can be simpler and may suit high-temperature or short peak loads. They generally need about one unit of electricity per unit of useful heat, so operating cost can exceed that of an efficient heat pump.

Consider suitable alternative fuels. Biomass can use local residues but needs secure supply, pollution controls, and sustainable sourcing. Hydrogen may have a role in hard-to-electrify high-temperature processes, but using it for modest-temperature heat can be energy-intensive. Evaluate the whole supply chain and cost.

Keep backup fuel. A hybrid system can let the heat pump cover steady demand while the boiler serves peaks and emergencies. This can improve reliability and economics. It also means residual combustion emissions must still be reported.

14. Production reliability and safety

Process heat is often essential for product quality. A temperature drop can spoil food, interrupt drying, or stop production. Design for source interruptions, compressor faults, power outages, and planned maintenance. Where production cannot pause, backup heat and storage need explicit sizing.

Refrigerants differ in pressure, flammability, toxicity, and climate effect. A low global-warming-potential fluid can still require strict safety controls. Check relevant codes, staff training, ventilation, leak detection, and end-of-life recovery. Supplier competence matters as much as the nominal equipment rating.

Water chemistry, fouling, corrosion, space, noise, and grid capacity can change performance. A dirty heat exchanger can reduce COP. A new electricity demand charge can reduce expected savings. The engineering plan must show what happens during shutdown and cleaning, not only normal operation.

The IEA's Heat Pump Taxonomy, published 22 May 2026, aims to improve technology classification and reporting. It can help buyers compare descriptions, but does not replace factory acceptance tests. [9]

15. A practical factory project sequence

Audit: Record useful heat, temperatures, flow, fuel use, and source availability by shift. Verify meter boundaries and remove avoidable heat losses.

Match: Find heating and cooling streams with overlapping hours. Lower process temperature where product quality allows. Decide whether the system should serve a local hot-water loop or an existing steam network.

Specify: Give all suppliers the same operating profile. Request a performance map, guaranteed output, electrical scope, maintenance plan, refrigerant details, and acceptance-test method. Define which auxiliary loads count in COP.

Test: Model high and low electricity prices, fuel prices, operating hours, annual COP, maintenance, and grid emissions. Include installation downtime and backup capacity.

Verify: Install heat and electricity meters. Record useful heat delivered, total electricity, backup fuel, outages, and product quality. Compare actual performance with the baseline and contract. Correct controls before claiming savings.

16. Wider effects and uncertainty

Industrial heat pumps can reduce imported fuel demand and local combustion pollution. They also increase electricity demand and may require grid investment. Thermal storage can sometimes shift operation toward cheaper or cleaner hours, but production quality sets limits.

More installations create work for compressor suppliers, controls firms, heat-exchanger makers, engineers, and maintenance teams. Existing boiler operators may need new training. These transition costs belong in project budgets.

Policy can help through clear electricity tariffs, finance, demonstration projects, and technical standards. The IEA's 2026 monitor identifies customised engineering, grid connections, and financing as barriers. Yet subsidies cannot make an unstable heat source reliable or change a process's physical temperature requirement. [2]

The key uncertainty is local: actual annual COP, source availability, tariff structure, capital cost, and uptime. Global technical potential identifies where to investigate. A metered pilot is often more useful than a general promise of efficiency.

17. Nine lessons to retain

  1. Measure useful heat and required temperature first.
  2. Heat pumps move heat; their COP depends on source and sink conditions.
  3. Steady, moderate-temperature processes are often the first candidates.
  4. Annual measured COP matters more than one ideal test rating.
  5. Technical potential is much larger than current deployment.
  6. Compare full ownership cost, not energy bills alone.
  7. Use explicit electricity emissions factors and account for refrigerant risk.
  8. Hybrid designs and basic heat recovery can be strong alternatives.
  9. Verify savings with meters and production records.

Five knowledge-check questions and answers

1. Why can a heat pump deliver more heat than the electricity it uses?

It moves heat from a source. The output includes transferred heat plus compressor energy, so COP can exceed one.

2. Why does a large temperature lift reduce efficiency?

The compressor generally needs more work to raise refrigerant pressure and temperature.

3. Does 20% technical potential mean 20% of factories should buy now?

No. The figure describes heat demand that commercial technology could technically serve. Costs, integration, and reliability decide actual investment.

4. What makes an emissions claim credible?

Meter useful heat and electricity, state the fuel baseline and electricity factor, and include relevant refrigerant and upstream impacts.

5. What must a supplier guarantee?

Useful output at required temperatures, performance across operating conditions, auxiliary loads, availability, safety, and acceptance-test methods.
