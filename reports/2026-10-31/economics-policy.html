Why Can a Higher Minimum Wage Raise Pay Without Cutting Many Jobs?

What do employers and workers change when a legal wage floor rises?

Executive summary

A minimum wage sets a legal floor under hourly pay. Standard supply-and-demand theory predicts that a binding floor can reduce hiring. Yet several major studies find that moderate increases raised low wages with little measured loss of jobs. This is a real research question, not proof that any wage floor is harmless. Employers can respond through prices, profits, staff turnover, work intensity, training, and hours. Workers also face search costs and limited choices, so employers may have some power to set wages below the value of the work. In that setting, a higher floor can improve pay without a large fall in employment. Evidence differs by country, worker group, wage level, and research method. Studies of United States state changes, an early New Jersey experiment, and the United Kingdom's Low Pay Commission offer useful but incomplete answers. This guide explains the competing models, tests the evidence, separates jobs from hours and earnings, and shows how to design and assess a wage policy. Both numerical examples are illustrative, not estimates of an actual policy.

Central question and decision rule

Central question: Under what conditions can a government raise the minimum wage, increase low-paid workers' earnings, and avoid substantial losses in jobs or hours?

A sound answer needs three tests. First, did pay rise for workers who were actually affected? Second, did the number of jobs, total hours, hiring opportunities, or job quality change relative to a credible comparison? Third, did the benefits reach households that need support, after price changes and other costs? A positive answer to one test does not settle the others.

This topic follows earlier reports on who bears taxes and who benefits from public policies. A wage floor is another way a rule can change how firms and households share economic value. Its effects cannot be read from the law alone. They must be estimated from what workers and firms do after the rule changes.

1. How minimum wages became a policy tool

Minimum wage laws arose from concerns about low pay, bargaining power, and basic living standards. Early wage boards set rates in selected trades. During the twentieth century, national and regional wage floors spread across many economies. Governments used them alongside collective bargaining, social insurance, and income support. The legal form varies: some countries set one national rate, while others use regional, age-based, sector-based, or occupational rates.

Economists first described a wage floor with a competitive labour-market model. In that model, many employers compete for workers and each firm accepts the market wage. A legal rate above the market level raises the cost of employing some workers. Employers may then demand fewer hours of labour. The size of that response depends on how easily firms can change staffing, output, and prices.

The debate changed when researchers began using policy changes as natural experiments. David Card and Alan Krueger studied New Jersey's 1992 increase from $4.25 to $5.05 an hour, comparing fast-food restaurants with neighbouring Pennsylvania. Their 1993 working paper, published in 1994, reported no relative employment decline in the surveyed restaurants. David Neumark and William Wascher challenged the result using payroll records in 1995. Card and Krueger later reanalysed the issue using administrative data in 1998. These studies made the quality of comparisons and measurement central to the debate. [1] [2] [3]

2. Define the terms before using the evidence

Nominal wage is the amount paid in money. Real wage adjusts that amount for prices. Minimum wage is the lowest legal rate for covered work. Binding floor means the legal minimum exceeds what an employer would otherwise pay for at least some jobs. Coverage describes which workers and jobs the law protects.

Employment counts jobs or employed people; these measures differ when one person holds two jobs. Hours records time worked. Labour earnings depend on the hourly wage multiplied by paid hours, before other adjustments. Wage spillover is a rise in pay above the new minimum because employers maintain pay differences or compete for staff.

Monopsony power is an employer's ability to influence the wage it pays because workers cannot switch jobs without cost. It does not require one employer in a town. Search time, transport, skills, schedules, and incomplete information can all limit choice. Elasticity measures how strongly one quantity responds to another in percentage terms. Counterfactual is the outcome that would have occurred without the policy. It cannot be observed directly and must be estimated.

3. How a wage increase works, step by step

Step 1 — Identify affected jobs. A higher floor changes pay directly only where the old wage was below the new legal rate. Firms already paying more may be little affected. Workers outside legal coverage may receive no direct gain.

Step 2 — Recalculate labour costs. The direct wage bill rises for affected hours. Payroll taxes and benefits may also change. Some employers raise pay for workers just above the floor to keep differences between grades.

Step 3 — Choose an adjustment. A firm can accept lower profit, raise prices, reduce vacancies, shorten hours, improve operations, change its product mix, or invest in equipment. These responses can occur together. A change in recruitment may appear before a change in current staff numbers.

Step 4 — Observe worker responses. Better pay can reduce resignations, absenteeism, or time spent looking for another job. Lower turnover may save recruitment and training costs. These savings are possible mechanisms, not guaranteed results.

Step 5 — Account for customers and rivals. If many competing firms face the same wage rule, some may pass part of the cost into prices. Customers may reduce purchases if prices rise. Firms that rely on international competition may have less room to do this.

Step 6 — Measure the whole outcome. Count jobs, paid hours, earnings, turnover, entry-level hiring, prices, and business survival over time. Compare them with places or groups that faced similar conditions but a different wage change. The result depends on the size of the increase and the economy around it.

5. The competitive model and its prediction

In a simple competitive market, a firm hires another hour of work while the value produced by that hour exceeds its cost. If the legal wage rises above the market rate, the firm may hire fewer hours. This prediction is strongest when employers can easily replace labour with machines, reduce output, or relocate work. It is weaker when staffing levels are hard to change or labour is a small share of total cost.

The model also predicts a distributional trade-off. Workers who keep their hours receive more pay. People who lose hours, fail to enter work, or move into informal jobs may lose. A study that counts only the wages of employed workers can miss those effects. A study that counts only job numbers can miss higher income for the large group that remains employed.

Short-run and long-run responses can differ. A restaurant may keep its current team after a wage rise but change hiring and equipment decisions over several years. At the same time, firms may learn to improve scheduling and productivity. A single short study cannot measure every adjustment. This is one reason careful researchers do not treat an insignificant job estimate as proof of exactly zero effect.

6. Why employer wage-setting power changes the answer

Workers cannot always move immediately to the highest-paying employer. They may need a bus route, a fixed shift, local childcare, or a particular language. They may also lack information about vacancies. An employer can therefore face an upward-sloping supply of labour: it must offer more pay to attract and retain more staff.

Under this condition, a firm may choose a wage below the value of the worker's output. A moderate wage floor can move pay closer to that value. It can sometimes raise both wages and employment, although this is not a universal result. If the floor becomes too high relative to productivity, hiring can still fall.

Alan Manning's 2020 review, published in ILR Review in 2021, summarises evidence that employers often have wage-setting power. This framework explains why researchers should examine worker mobility and local employer concentration. It does not prove that all low-wage jobs are subject to the same degree of power. [4]

Monopsony and competitive forces can exist in the same economy. A city with many shops may still have few employers offering a suitable night shift. A national average can conceal these local differences. Good policy therefore studies the groups most exposed to a higher floor, including young entrants, workers with few qualifications, and people in remote areas.

7. Worked example one: pay, hours and household earnings

Illustrative data only. A worker earns ₹100 an hour for 160 paid hours a month. The government raises the legal rate to ₹115. Before the change, monthly pay is ₹100 × 160 = ₹16,000. If hours stay at 160, pay becomes ₹115 × 160 = ₹18,400, a 15% rise.

Suppose the employer instead reduces this worker's paid hours to 148. Monthly pay becomes ₹115 × 148 = ₹17,020. Earnings still rise by ₹1,020, or 6.375%, despite a 7.5% reduction in hours. If hours fall to 139, pay is ₹15,985, slightly below the original monthly amount. Thus a higher hourly rate does not ensure higher monthly income for every worker.

These figures do not predict that hours will fall. They show why an evaluation must measure both wages and paid hours. It must also consider whether the worker wants more hours, whether benefits change, and whether consumer prices rise. The rupee values are invented to explain the arithmetic, not a proposed Indian minimum wage.

8. What the strongest employment studies actually find

The New Jersey study created a major challenge to the simple prediction of immediate job losses. Card and Krueger surveyed 410 fast-food restaurants before and after the 1992 increase and compared them with restaurants in Pennsylvania. Their original working paper reported relative employment growth in the treated restaurants. The study's strength was a clear policy change and nearby comparison. Its limits included survey-based employment measures, a short time window, and one industry. [1]

Neumark and Wascher's 1995 payroll-record re-evaluation reached the opposite conclusion using a different sample and data source. Card and Krueger's 1998 reanalysis using administrative records again found no relative employment decline. The dispute showed that results can depend on the sample, employment measure, and assumptions about a suitable comparison group. It is wrong to present the early experiment as an uncontested fact. [2] [3]

A broader study by Cengiz, Dube, Lindner and Zipperer, published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics on 2 May 2019, examined 138 prominent state-level minimum wage changes from 1979 to 2016. The authors compared jobs across wage ranges and found that the overall number of low-wage jobs remained essentially unchanged over five years, while pay increased. They reported some evidence of reduced employment in sectors exposed to trade. The study offers broader coverage than one restaurant experiment, but still studies observed US changes rather than every possible future wage floor. [5]

In a 2021 NBER working paper, Cengiz and colleagues used demographic groups that captured around three-quarters of minimum-wage workers and examined 172 changes. They found clear wage gains and little evidence of employment loss in those groups. This supports a small average employment response in the settings studied. It does not establish that an extreme increase or a weak economy would produce the same result. [6]

9. Serious competing findings and their limits

Neumark and Wascher's 2006 review, revised in January 2007, concluded that many studies found negative employment effects, particularly for the least-skilled workers. This is a serious competing assessment of the literature. Its conclusion differs from later studies that use larger policy datasets and newer comparison methods. Differences reflect both the evidence available at the time and researchers' judgments about which methods are most credible. [7]

One concern is that a wage floor may affect new workers more than current workers. A firm can retain experienced staff and stop hiring people who need training. Total employment may look stable for a while even if job entry becomes harder. Another concern is that studies of modest past changes cannot identify the effect of much larger future increases.

Researchers also disagree about statistical comparisons. Places that raise wages may have different economic trends, politics, or business cycles. A nearby region is useful only if it would have followed a similar path without the policy. Different weighting and time windows can change estimates. Good research reports these assumptions and tests how sensitive the answer is to them.

The reasonable synthesis is conditional: many observed moderate increases raised low pay without large measured job losses, while risks can be higher for some groups, sectors, or wage levels. Policy should track these risks rather than declare the debate closed.

10. What the United Kingdom's 2025 review adds

The UK's independent Low Pay Commission provides a different type of evidence. It reviews administrative data, research, employer submissions, worker experience, and sector conditions before recommending rates. Its 2025 report supported a National Living Wage of £12.71 from April 2026 for eligible workers aged 21 and over. It estimated about 2.02 million jobs within its minimum-wage coverage measure in April 2025. These are UK policy and measurement facts, not results for India. [8]

The Commission judged that recent increases had not produced robust evidence of a significant negative effect on overall employment. It also reported pressure in retail and hospitality, more employer accounts of reduced recruitment or hours, and difficulty separating minimum-wage effects from higher employer National Insurance costs and weak demand. The careful conclusion is that the labour market can absorb some increases while individual firms and workers still face pressure.

Employer responses in the report include higher prices, lower profit, less hiring, more intensive work, and delayed investment. These responses are not equally common. The Commission found price increases and lower profits remained prominent. It also warned that surveys reporting employers' intentions are not the same as a causal estimate of economy-wide job loss. [8]

11. The effect on prices, turnover and productivity

A firm may pass higher labour costs to customers. How much it can pass through depends on the labour share of costs, competition, and customer demand. A 2022 study by Ashenfelter and Jurajda examined wages and Big Mac prices at almost all US McDonald's restaurants during 2016–2020. It found substantial price pass-through associated with minimum-wage changes, with real wage gains smaller than nominal gains when expressed in burgers. This is evidence from one chain and product, not a national inflation coefficient. [9]

Lower worker turnover can offset some costs. A firm that retains trained employees may spend less on hiring, induction, and mistakes. But improved retention does not automatically cover the entire wage increase. A business may also reduce non-wage benefits, use more part-time shifts, or ask workers to perform more tasks. Job quality and health therefore belong in the evaluation.

Productivity matters, but it is easy to misuse the word. Producing the same output with less wasted time is a productivity gain. Asking workers to rush without more equipment may increase measured output per hour for a period while raising fatigue and error risk. Analysts should distinguish durable process improvement from work intensification.

12. Worked example two: what a small business can absorb

Illustrative data only. A small café sells 20,000 items per month at an average price of ₹100. Monthly revenue is ₹20 lakh. It pays 10,000 covered staff hours at ₹100 per hour, a wage bill of ₹10 lakh. A 10% legal wage rise, with unchanged hours and no spillovers, adds ₹1 lakh a month.

If the café passes the full added wage cost to customers and sales volume stays unchanged, it needs an average price increase of ₹1 lakh ÷ 20,000 = ₹5 per item, or 5%. If it passes half the cost through prices, it needs ₹2.50 per item and must find the other ₹50,000 through lower profit or genuine efficiency gains.

This calculation omits payroll charges, input-cost changes, sales-tax effects, price-sensitive demand, and wages above the floor. It is not a forecast that cafés will raise prices by 5%. It shows why the effect on product prices depends on the share of costs that the wage rule changes. Real firms should use their own accounts and test how demand responds.

13. How to design a more reliable wage policy

Start with a clear aim. Is the policy intended to prevent extremely low hourly pay, reduce inequality, protect purchasing power, or improve bargaining conditions? One legal rate cannot achieve every goal equally well. For poverty reduction, household income and access to work matter as much as hourly pay.

Use reliable wage data to estimate how many workers would be affected. Compare the proposed floor with typical pay in each region and sector. A rate that affects only a small share of workers differs from one that changes pay across much of the labour market. Consult workers, small employers, and independent researchers, while checking claims against data.

Phase in large changes when the evidence is uncertain, and announce dates early enough for planning. Enforce the law. A wage floor that employers routinely ignore will not deliver the intended benefit. The UK Low Pay Commission's September 2025 note identified underpayment and enforcement as continuing concerns. [10]

Combine wage rules with training, better job matching, transport, childcare, and income support where appropriate. These policies address different problems. For example, a wage floor cannot by itself help someone who has no paid work, while a tax credit may not protect a worker from an illegally low hourly rate.

14. How to test a policy after it starts

Before implementation, publish the baseline: hourly wages, total hours, job counts, hiring, turnover, business openings, prices, and relevant household outcomes. Define the affected groups and the comparison groups in advance. If possible, use administrative payroll records rather than relying only on small employer surveys.

Check outcomes at several horizons. The first quarter may show direct wage gains. One year may show changes in turnover, prices, and recruitment. Several years may reveal automation or business entry effects. Track younger workers and low-experience entrants separately from established employees. Include informal work where it is significant.

Report uncertainty. A result that is statistically indistinguishable from zero may still allow modest gains or losses. Researchers should show confidence intervals, test alternative comparison groups, and make data and methods available where privacy permits. If the evidence indicates persistent harm in a specific group, adjust policy design rather than treating a national average as sufficient.

15. Wider effects: households, firms and public finance

Higher pay can improve household purchasing power and reduce the need for some income support. It can also raise payroll-linked tax revenue. These gains depend on hours, employment, prices, and benefit rules. Some low-paid workers live in households with higher earners, while some poor households have no wage earner. The International Labour Organization's 2024–25 Global Wage Report shows why wage inequality and household inequality should be analysed separately. [11]

Businesses with thin margins may face pressure, especially where buyers set prices or foreign competitors are unaffected. Other firms may benefit from reduced turnover or stronger local spending. Publicly funded services can face a budget gap if mandated wages rise but their contracts do not. Governments must decide whether to fund the added cost or accept lower service capacity.

Longer-term questions remain open: whether firms invest in useful technology, whether entry-level training changes, whether wage gaps between grades compress, and whether people move between regions or sectors. These effects can matter even when the headline employment number barely moves.

16. Eight lessons to retain

  1. A legal wage floor changes pay directly only for jobs below the new rate.
  2. Hourly pay, paid hours, monthly earnings, and job counts are different measures.
  3. The competitive model predicts potential job loss when a binding floor raises labour costs.
  4. Employer wage-setting power can make the employment response smaller or even positive for some moderate increases.
  5. Original studies disagree, so examine their data, comparison groups, and time periods.
  6. Firms can adjust through prices, profit, turnover, hiring, job quality, and investment.
  7. Average employment effects can hide risks for new entrants and particular sectors.
  8. Set rates with data, enforce them, and review both benefits and harms over time.

Five knowledge-check questions and answers

1. Why can hourly pay rise while monthly earnings fall?

Monthly earnings equal the hourly wage multiplied by paid hours. A sufficiently large reduction in hours can outweigh a higher hourly rate.

2. What does monopsony power mean?

It means an employer has some power to set pay because workers cannot change jobs freely or instantly. Search, transport, schedule and information costs can create that power.

3. Did the 2019 US study prove all wage increases are safe?

No. It found little overall change in low-wage job numbers across 138 historical state changes. Its results do not cover every possible rate, country, or economic condition.

4. Why must researchers study hiring as well as employment?

Existing employees can remain on payroll while firms reduce new vacancies. Entry-level opportunities may change before the total job count changes.

5. What is the most useful policy scorecard?

Real wages, paid hours, employment, recruitment, prices, household income, compliance, and outcomes for vulnerable groups, all compared with a credible counterfactual.
