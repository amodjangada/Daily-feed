Executive summary

Housing policy is often discussed as a choice between growth and neighbourhood stability. Economics asks a more precise question: what happens to rents, access to jobs, public services and existing residents when a city permits more homes? A housing shortage develops when demand for a place rises faster than its stock of homes can respond. Jobs, migration, smaller households and credit conditions can raise demand. Land geography, zoning, permits, finance and construction time can restrict supply. When new homes are difficult to build, more demand can lead mainly to higher rents and land values. Additional supply usually reduces rent pressure relative to what would otherwise happen, but effects differ across locations, time and household groups. New buildings may also change amenities, congestion and the tax base. Research finds evidence that added apartments can moderate nearby rents, while long project timelines delay benefits. A complete policy must pair supply reform with infrastructure, tenant protection and targeted support for people who cannot afford market rents. This guide separates measured effects from models, explains the adjustment process and shows how to test claims without treating one city as proof for every other city.

Central question and what counts as success

Central question: If a city permits more homes, who receives the benefit, who bears the cost, and how long does adjustment take? The question matters because a policy can increase total housing while leaving some vulnerable households worse off during construction or neighbourhood change. We must measure both outcomes. A rise in the number of homes is an input. Affordable access to secure housing is an outcome.

At least four measures are needed. First, track rents for comparable units, with attention to quality and location. Second, track the number and type of homes delivered, rather than permits alone. Third, track moves, eviction, crowding and the share of income spent on housing. Fourth, track access to employment and services. A city can improve average affordability while still failing very low-income renters. Equally, a policy that preserves current streets but blocks new residents has costs that are easy to omit from local debate.

This report follows the 26 October lesson on tax incidence. That lesson asked who ultimately pays a tax. Here the parallel question is who receives the value of scarce building rights and who pays when supply is limited. We do not repeat the tax calculation; we apply the same discipline to land and housing markets.

1. Why housing became a land-use policy problem

Homes are durable assets. A building can serve residents for decades, while the households that want to live in a city change much faster. Cities historically expanded through new land, transport links and higher-density buildings. As urban centres became more productive, they attracted workers and firms. Some places could build outward; others faced water, steep slopes or protected land. The supply response also came to depend on rules for building height, minimum plot size, parking, setbacks and approvals.

Geography and regulation must be kept separate. Albert Saiz used terrain and water-body data to study US metropolitan land constraints. His 2010 research found that geographic limits help explain differences in how easily housing supply expands, alongside regulation. This does not mean every high price is caused by zoning. High demand can raise prices even where construction is relatively easy. Nor does every land-use rule lack a purpose: fire access, drainage, safety, heritage and environmental protection can create real public benefits. The policy question is whether each restriction solves a problem at a cost proportionate to its benefit. [3]

The OECD's Brick by Brick, published 31 May 2021, describes housing policy as a balance among inclusion, efficiency and sustainability. It identifies the mismatch between demand and supply in jobs-rich areas as a central cause of affordability pressure, while warning that infrastructure, taxation and land governance interact. [1]

2. Terms that prevent confusion

Housing stock is the number of usable homes at a given time. Housing flow is the number completed over a period. Supply elasticity describes how strongly the stock or construction rate responds when prices change. Land value is the value of the location and permitted development opportunity, separate from the cost of the structure. Upzoning means changing a rule to allow more floor space or homes. Filtering is the movement of existing homes into lower price ranges as households move and buildings age; it is a long-term tendency, not a guarantee for every unit.

Incidence here means who experiences the economic effects of a rule. A homeowner may gain from scarcity because the land becomes more valuable. A renter may lose through higher payments or longer travel. A new entrant may be excluded without appearing in a local survey. Displacement means residents must leave because of cost, redevelopment or other pressure. It is not the same as voluntary movement, although the boundary can be difficult to measure.

Finally, distinguish levels from growth rates. A new project can slow rent growth while rents still rise. That outcome is economically meaningful. A comparison with the no-project scenario is needed before saying the project failed because rents did not fall in absolute terms.

3. The mechanism: from jobs to rents to construction

Step 1: demand changes. A new employer, better transport or improved amenities make an area more attractive. More households compete for the existing homes. Some accept smaller spaces, longer journeys or shared accommodation. Others bid more for scarce units.

Step 2: rents and land bids respond. Where few vacant homes exist, landlords can often raise asking rents. Buyers value land partly for its expected future rental income. Higher rents can therefore increase land values even when concrete, steel and labour costs do not change much. This is why construction cost alone does not explain urban home prices.

Step 3: developers consider supply. Higher expected rents can justify a new project, but only if the allowed building size, land cost, finance, approvals and expected sales support it. A permission for ten units where twenty could physically fit limits the possible response. A project may still fail because infrastructure or demand is weak.

Step 4: construction takes time. Developers acquire land, secure approval, install services and build. Oh, Yang and Yoon, in a Federal Reserve working paper published 13 February 2024, document an average development period of about three years in their US data, including preparation and construction. The period varies greatly by place. Their model suggests reforms may have little immediate effect on prices where development is slow. [5]

Step 5: the new stock changes competition. Once occupied, new units absorb some households that otherwise would have competed for older homes. Moves create vacancies elsewhere. The size of the benefit depends on demand growth, vacancy rates, location, quality and how many units actually arrive. If a city approves new homes but adds them more slowly than households arrive, affordability may continue to worsen.

4. Visual model: the housing adjustment

Two common mistakes follow from this diagram. The first is to treat the rent of a newly built luxury flat as the only relevant price. Its price may be high because it offers better features, while its existence changes competition for older flats. The second is to assume any new home produces a fixed percentage rent reduction. Markets are connected through moves, but the size and speed of that connection differ.

The correct comparison is between similar areas with and without an increase in supply, allowing for changes in local demand. That is difficult because developers usually choose places already expected to grow. A simple before-and-after rent chart can confuse a project's effect with the demand that led to the project.

5. Worked example: a growing city and two supply paths

Illustrative numbers, not observed data. A district starts with 100,000 homes and 98,000 occupied units. Over five years, 8,000 additional households want to live there. In scenario A, the district completes 2,000 net new homes. The stock becomes 102,000, while potential households reach 106,000. Some households must share, relocate, wait or bid for scarce homes. The shortfall relative to potential demand is 4,000 households, before accounting for vacancy needs.

In scenario B, the district completes 9,000 net new homes. The stock becomes 109,000. At the same potential demand of 106,000, there are 3,000 more homes than households seeking occupancy, leaving room for normal vacancies. This does not predict a specific rent reduction. Households may differ in size and income, and some units may be unsuitable or too far from jobs. It does show why a building programme must be compared with demand growth, not only with last year's completions.

A vacancy rate is also necessary for a functioning market. If every home is occupied, a household changing jobs or family size cannot move easily. A moderate stock of available homes supports mobility. The appropriate rate differs by market; the illustrative scenario does not set a universal target. The arithmetic is a stock-flow check, not a price model.

6. What research finds—and what it cannot prove

Asquith, Mast and Reed studied large market-rate apartment buildings in low-income areas in US cities. Their 2023 peer-reviewed paper in The Review of Economics and Statistics reports that nearby rents were about 6% lower relative to comparison locations, and that new buildings drew some movers from lower-income areas. The underlying study design compared places near new developments with areas farther away or with later development. This is evidence that new supply can moderate nearby rent pressure even when the new units are expensive. It is not proof that every development lowers every rent. The sample, local market and listing data impose limits. [4]

The OECD's 2021 housing report uses international policy evidence to argue that flexible supply is important for affordability and mobility. It also addresses the role of property taxes, rental regulation and land-use governance. This is broader policy synthesis, not a single controlled experiment. [1][2]

Hsieh and Moretti's 2019 journal article uses a spatial economic model to estimate national productivity costs when workers cannot move to highly productive US cities. The published model reports a large counterfactual growth effect from housing constraints. That result depends on assumptions about mobility, productivity and local restrictions; it must not be quoted as a direct measurement of lost output or transferred to another country without a new model. Earlier working-paper versions reported different headline magnitudes, so cite the published version when using its result. [6]

Saiz's 2010 research supplies a second important caution: physical geography matters. A coastal city with limited buildable land cannot simply copy the construction pattern of a flat inland city. The policy options may involve denser development, better transport or new employment centres rather than unlimited outward expansion. [3]

7. Why new homes do not solve tomorrow's rent bill

Housing is slow to produce. A zoning change can alter future land values immediately, but it does not deliver finished homes on the same day. Landowners may wait for higher offers. Developers may need capital, skilled workers and infrastructure capacity. Local government may need to expand water, drainage, power and transit. If these are missing, approval alone can create land speculation without much new housing.

Financial conditions matter as well. Higher interest rates can make new projects less profitable and reduce the number that begin. This can limit the supply response even when zoning permits more homes. At the same time, higher mortgage rates may reduce buyer demand. The net effect on prices depends on whether the demand effect or construction effect is stronger, and on the rental market.

The 2024 Federal Reserve study of land development documents long and dispersed project times. It supports a practical conclusion: evaluate reforms over a period long enough for completions, while providing short-term support where hardship is immediate. [5] A renter who faces eviction this month cannot wait three years for a supply programme. Emergency assistance, legal process, temporary housing and targeted subsidy may be needed. These policies have their own costs and design risks, but they address a different time horizon.

8. Who benefits, and who may bear costs?

Future renters and new residents: They usually benefit when more homes create options and reduce bidding pressure. Some would otherwise be excluded entirely, so surveys of current residents miss their gains. Existing renters: They may gain from slower rent growth, but can face construction disruption or displacement if their own building is demolished. Homeowners: They can gain from new amenities or from greater redevelopment rights, but some may lose part of the scarcity premium built into land prices. Landowners with new building rights: They may receive a windfall before any homes are built.

Workers and employers: Easier access to housing near jobs can widen labour markets. Firms may recruit more easily, and workers may avoid long commutes. Hsieh and Moretti's model explores these productivity channels. [6] Local government: More homes can increase the tax base, but also require investment in schools, transport, water and public space. The net fiscal result depends on the form and location of development.

Equity depends on who gets the new units. A market-rate apartment may be unaffordable to the poorest households even if it eases competition elsewhere. Public or non-profit housing, rent support and accessible design may be necessary. The supply argument is therefore incomplete if it treats average market rents as the only social objective.

9. Worked example: compare a local rent trend correctly

Illustrative numbers, not a real study. Suppose a new building opens near a group of existing apartments. Average comparable monthly rent in both the project area and a comparison area is ₹20,000 before construction. Two years later, rent near the new building is ₹22,000, while rent in the comparison area is ₹23,000. The local rent rose by 10%; the comparison rent rose by 15%. A simple difference-in-differences estimate is 10% minus 15%, or minus 5 percentage points of rent growth. In rupee terms, the difference in the final rents is ₹1,000 per month.

This estimate suggests that the new supply moderated growth, even though the observed local rent increased. But it is only credible if the comparison area would have followed a similar trend without the building. If a new railway station opened near the project at the same time, or if one area changed its housing quality, the difference might reflect those changes. Researchers test earlier trends, alternative comparison groups and data quality. They also study moves and new listings because asking rents and rents paid by continuing tenants can behave differently.

The calculation illustrates a method. It is not an estimate of the effect of any real building, city or policy. A robust study would also report uncertainty ranges and examine the effect over several years.

10. Competing views that deserve serious treatment

Case for liberalising supply: When demand is strong, restrictions prevent households from responding through construction. The burden falls on renters, young households and people who cannot move near productive jobs. More units create choices. Empirical studies such as Asquith, Mast and Reed provide evidence that even expensive new apartments can reduce rent pressure nearby. Restrictions can also encourage outward sprawl if they prevent homes in accessible locations. [4]

Case for cautious, planned growth: A sudden increase in allowed density may raise land values and redevelopment pressure before new homes are available. It may disrupt existing tenants, overload water or transit, remove green space or change a valued neighbourhood. Local residents may distrust developers because past projects have not delivered promised public benefits. These concerns are substantive. They call for enforceable infrastructure plans, transparent development rights, protections against demolition-driven displacement and careful environmental review.

The strongest policy combines more supply in suitable locations with safeguards. This can include predictable approval for projects that meet clear rules, funding for utilities, stronger rental security and direct support for low-income households. There is no reason to assume that an unrestricted market will provide adequate housing for everyone. There is also no reason to assume that blocking new homes protects affordability when demand continues to grow.

11. Applying the logic in Indian cities

Indian urban housing markets have additional features. Land title can be fragmented. Building permissions involve several agencies. Floor-space index rules, road widths, parking standards, fire safety, infrastructure capacity and informal housing can shape the feasible supply. The formal rental market may be thin or poorly recorded. A change that works in a US city cannot be copied without studying these conditions.

For an Indian city, begin with ward-level data: household growth, occupied and vacant units, completed homes, approval times, commute patterns and rental price changes by dwelling quality. Measure both formal and informal housing, while protecting privacy. Then map areas where transit and utilities can support more residents. The next step is to estimate the net new homes possible under each rule change, after subtracting demolitions and expected delays. Only then should officials forecast affordability effects.

One overlooked issue is who receives the value created by new development rights. If a zoning change increases land value, public authorities may consider lawful, transparent ways to finance the infrastructure required by new residents. Poorly designed charges can also reduce construction. The same incidence logic studied in the previous edition applies: the legal payer is not always the person who ultimately bears the cost.

12. A policy package, not a single switch

A practical housing strategy has several linked parts. First, permit more homes in places with jobs and transport, subject to safety and environmental standards. Second, reduce uncertainty by publishing clear approval criteria and time limits. Third, invest in infrastructure before or alongside occupancy. Fourth, protect tenants against unlawful eviction and provide targeted support for people whose incomes cannot meet market rents. Fifth, use public land or direct public programmes where market supply cannot serve essential groups.

Measure the policy after implementation. Count completed net units, not only announced projects. Track rent per square metre and unit quality. Record whether travel times improve. Compare similar districts and report displacement separately from ordinary mobility. Review whether a large share of approved units remains unbuilt because of finance or land assembly. These measures help identify the real bottleneck rather than treating all construction delays as regulatory.

There is a political economy problem: current homeowners and residents can vote locally, while future residents are absent. Officials should include regional labour-market needs and the interests of younger and lower-income households in decisions. Public consultation matters, but its design should not allow only the most organised current owners to represent everyone affected by a rule.

13. What the evidence does not settle

The evidence does not establish a universal elasticity, a universal rent reduction per 1,000 homes or an ideal density for every neighbourhood. Short-run construction disruption can differ from long-run benefits. Price changes can spill across districts. A new luxury project can change local shops and amenities, which may increase demand at the same time as supply rises. Studies that measure listed rents can miss informal leases, concessions or people who never found a home.

Economic models can clarify mechanisms and explore policy options, but their results depend on assumptions. The large national output estimate in Hsieh and Moretti is a model counterfactual, not a direct observation. The local apartment evidence in Asquith, Mast and Reed covers particular US developments, not a random sample of every world city. The Federal Reserve development-time study describes a US data set and a model; it does not imply every project takes exactly three years. [4][5][6]

These limits should improve decisions, not stop them. A city can pilot clear rules in suitable districts, collect credible data, publish results and adjust. Strong evaluation should look for effects on households with the least bargaining power, not only average prices. Where land or transport capacity is scarce, planning and construction must be coordinated.

14. Eight lessons to retain

  1. Housing prices depend on demand, supply and the value of location; construction cost is only one part.
  2. Compare completed homes with household growth. Permits alone do not create housing stock.
  3. More supply usually eases rent pressure relative to the no-supply case, but the effect varies.
  4. Rents can still rise after construction even when the new homes prevent a larger rise.
  5. Long development times make short-run tenant support necessary.
  6. New development can create both benefits and costs for existing residents.
  7. Land-use rights distribute economic value, including to people who do not yet live in the city.
  8. Good housing policy combines suitable density, infrastructure, tenant safeguards and credible measurement.

Return to the central question: who gains when cities allow more homes? The answer depends on the counterfactual, timing and distribution. The relevant policy test is whether additional suitable homes improve access and security while accounting for local costs.

15. Knowledge check: five questions with answers

1. Why can rent rise after a new apartment building opens even if it improves affordability?

Demand may be growing. The building can slow the increase compared with what would have happened without it. Evaluate the difference against a credible comparison, not only the before-and-after price.

2. What is the difference between a permit and a completed home?

A permit allows construction under stated conditions. A completed, usable home adds to the housing stock. Financing, infrastructure and building time can separate the two by years.

3. Why can restrictions affect workers outside the city?

People who cannot afford housing near productive jobs may work elsewhere or accept long commutes. This can reduce access to employment and affect national output, though the size depends on model assumptions.

4. Does evidence that new market-rate buildings moderate rents prove that low-income housing support is unnecessary?

No. Market supply can ease general pressure while the poorest households still cannot afford available units. Direct housing support and tenant protections address separate problems.

5. What would make the illustrative rent comparison misleading?

Different prior trends, changes in transport or amenities, changes in unit quality, or developers choosing fast-growing areas can bias the estimate. Research needs careful comparison groups and uncertainty checks.
