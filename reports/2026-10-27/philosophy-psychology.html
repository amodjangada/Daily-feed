Why We Misjudge How We Will Feel in the Future

Why do predictions of joy, disappointment and regret differ from what people later experience?

Research checked 27 October 2026 · Both worked examples use illustrative numbers

Executive summary

People make decisions by imagining how they will feel later. They predict the joy of success, the pain of rejection, the comfort of a purchase and the regret of a missed chance. Psychologists call this affective forecasting. Research shows that forecasts can be useful, but people sometimes misjudge the intensity or duration of their future feelings. They may focus too much on one event, neglect the ordinary activities that continue around it, overlook their ability to adapt, or use vivid memories that do not represent typical experience. Current hunger can also influence choices about food to be eaten later. Studies of students, social events and daily experience show that the feeling people remember may differ from what they felt throughout an event. None of this means that emotions are trivial or that all predictions are wrong. The size and direction of error depend on the event, the person and how feelings are measured. This guide explains the mechanisms, evidence and limits, then shows how to make decisions with better information about future experience.

1. The central question: can we predict our future feelings?

People regularly make decisions for a future version of themselves. They choose jobs, homes, travel, relationships and medical treatments partly by imagining how each choice will feel. These forecasts matter because people often care about experience as well as income, status or physical outcomes. Yet future feelings cannot be observed directly at the moment of choice.

Affective forecasting is the prediction of one's emotional response to a future event. A forecast can concern valence (pleasant or unpleasant), intensity (how strong), duration (how long), or the mix of feelings. A person may correctly predict that a rejection will hurt but overestimate how long the hurt will dominate daily life.

This distinction prevents an important mistake. Evidence that people overestimate emotional duration does not imply that bad events have no serious effects. Some experiences cause lasting distress. The research asks whether predicted reactions match later measured reactions under particular conditions.

Psychologists also distinguish the self that makes a prediction, the self that lives through an event and the self that later remembers it. These perspectives can produce different evaluations. A decision based on remembered experience may therefore differ from one based on daily experience. The central question is how to use these differences to make choices that serve the person who will actually live with the result.

2. How the research developed

Earlier decision theories often treated preferences as stable and assumed that people could evaluate the consequences of choices. Psychology challenged this picture by examining how memory, attention and bodily states shape what people want. Studies of experienced pleasure and pain also found that a later summary of an event may give unusual weight to its most intense moments and its ending.

In 1993, Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier asked participants to experience two uncomfortable cold-water trials. In one, the unpleasant experience lasted longer but ended with a slightly less painful period. A significant majority preferred to repeat the longer trial. The result suggests that retrospective evaluation does not always follow total unpleasant duration. It does not establish a rule for every kind of event. [7]

In the late 1990s, Gilbert and colleagues studied why people overpredict how long emotional reactions will last. Their 1998 paper reported six studies across several negative outcomes. [1] Wilson and colleagues then tested the role of focusing attention too narrowly on a future event. [2]

Later studies connected forecasting to consumer choices, memories of travel and information about how other people felt. The field now covers several distinct mechanisms. It is best understood as a set of testable explanations, rather than a single claim that people always exaggerate the future.

3. Define the different kinds of error

A forecast can fail in more than one way. Impact bias is the tendency, in some settings, to overestimate how strongly an event will affect feelings. Durability bias is the tendency to overestimate how long a reaction will last. These are related but not identical. Someone may correctly predict a very painful first day and incorrectly expect the same intensity for months.

Focalism means giving too much attention to one event while leaving out other parts of future life. Immune neglect means overlooking the mental processes that help people explain, adjust to or recover from an experience. The term “psychological immune system” is a metaphor used in research; it does not refer to the body's biological immune system. [1] [2]

Projection bias occurs when a current state shapes a prediction about a different future state. Hunger, fatigue or excitement can change what a person expects to want later. A related idea is the hot-cold empathy gap: people in one motivational state can struggle to represent another.

Finally, remembered utility is a retrospective evaluation of an experience, while experienced utility concerns feelings during the experience. Neither is automatically the correct target for every decision. If a person values a lasting memory, remembered experience may matter. If they want comfortable daily life, moment-to-moment experience may matter more.

4. Stage one: the mind builds a simplified future

When a person imagines a future event, the mind selects a few details. These may include a vivid moment, a social reaction, a physical setting or a feared outcome. The imagined scene can be useful, but it is not a complete simulation of future life. Many ordinary details remain outside attention.

A forecast of a new job may emphasize the offer letter, salary or first day. It may omit commuting, meetings, family routines and the fact that attention moves among many concerns. The imagined event receives a larger share of mental space than it may receive in actual daily experience. This is a possible source of focalism.

Wilson and colleagues tested focalism in studies involving college football fans. When some participants considered other future activities before forecasting their feelings about a game outcome, they were less likely to overpredict the duration of its effect. The authors also tested alternative explanations and argued that expected distraction played an important role. [2]

The finding is specific: reminding people of the wider future can improve some forecasts. It does not show that every forecast should be reduced. Major events can alter many parts of life. A complete forecast must consider both the central event and its real consequences for daily routines.

5. Stage two: we underestimate adaptation

People often adjust to changed circumstances. They learn new routines, reinterpret events, receive social support, solve practical problems and shift attention toward other goals. These processes can change emotional reactions over time. Adaptation is neither automatic nor complete for every event.

Gilbert and colleagues used the term immune neglect for a specific forecasting error. In their 1998 experiments, participants tended to overestimate the duration of negative emotional reactions across several settings. The researchers argued that people did not fully anticipate the mental processes that would help them make sense of an unwanted outcome. [1]

This idea is easy to misuse. It does not justify dismissing a person's current distress. It does not prove that grief, disability, chronic pain or financial hardship will fade quickly. An individual's adaptation depends on the event, available support, ongoing constraints and other factors. Forecasting studies often involve relatively short observation periods and selected participants.

There is also a difference between adaptation and improvement in the external situation. A person might feel less distressed because the situation changes, because attention changes or because they find a new interpretation. Research designs must separate these explanations when possible. The safest conclusion is that forecasts should account for future coping and routine, without assuming that all harm disappears.

6. Stage three: the present state changes future preferences

A person can know that future conditions will differ and still let the present state influence the choice. Hunger provides a clear test. When people are hungry, food that gives quick satisfaction can seem more attractive. The difficulty is predicting what will be appealing at another time, especially when the future person may be less hungry.

Read and van Leeuwen published a 1998 experiment in which participants selected snacks for a later date while either hungry or satisfied. Their advance choices were affected by present hunger as well as the expected future state. The study also found differences between advance choices and immediate choices. [3]

The general mechanism is state dependence: the value of an option can change with bodily or emotional conditions. When forecasting, people may start with their present feelings and adjust too little for the future state. This is a plausible explanation for some choices, but it is not a universal law. Some people learn to anticipate predictable changes in appetite or mood.

The practical issue is to identify when the decision and the experience will occur under different conditions. If the gap matters, it may help to postpone the decision, gather information from previous comparable situations or set a rule while in a stable state. The goal is to match the choice to the conditions under which it will be used.

7. Stage four: unusual memories distort the forecast

People often use memory as evidence about future experience. That is sensible when the remembered event resembles the future one. But the events that are easiest to recall may be unusually intense. An exceptional day can be memorable precisely because it was not typical.

Morewedge, Gilbert and Wilson tested this problem in three studies published in 2005. Participants asked to recall an instance of an event often selected atypical instances. When they did not recognize that the memory was atypical, their forecasts about future reactions were more extreme. The researchers argued that accessible memories can distort affective forecasts when they are not representative. [4]

This mechanism differs from the memory reconstruction topic in an earlier Daily Feed edition. The question here is not whether a past event is recalled accurately. Even an accurate memory can be a poor sample of what usually happens. A rare extreme experience may be true and still mislead a future prediction.

A useful forecast therefore asks two questions: “What happened in the most memorable case?” and “What happened across ordinary cases?” The second question often needs records, repeated observations or information from other people. The mind's first answer may be vivid, but vividness is not a measure of how often an outcome occurs.

8. Stage five: the experiencing self and remembering self diverge

A person's evaluation of an event can change after it ends. While an event unfolds, feelings rise and fall. Later, memory compresses the experience into a shorter account. The summary may emphasize a peak, an ending or a meaningful turning point rather than the total time spent feeling each way.

In the 1993 cold-water study, participants often preferred to repeat a longer unpleasant episode when it ended less painfully. This supports the idea that retrospective evaluation can give special weight to the ending. It does not imply that longer pain is objectively better or that people should ignore total discomfort. [7]

Wirtz, Kruger, Scollon and Diener examined students' predicted, ongoing and remembered spring-break experiences in a study published in September 2003. Predicted and remembered experiences were more extreme than reports collected during the break. In their path analysis, remembered experience was directly associated with desire to repeat the vacation, whereas predicted and online experience were not. The study is observational for that relationship and does not prove that memory alone causes future choices. [6]

These results raise a philosophical question: which evaluation should count? The answer depends on what the person values. A decision can aim to improve ordinary moments, create a meaningful memory, avoid a painful ending or achieve a goal beyond immediate pleasure. Those are distinct objectives.

The diagram is a conceptual model, not a measured pathway for every individual. The order can also vary. People may revise forecasts during an event, and some choices depend on expected practical consequences more than expected emotion.

Its main use is diagnostic. If a prediction fails, ask whether the error arose from current state, narrow attention, neglected adaptation, unrepresentative memories or a mismatch between the outcome measured and the outcome valued. Different errors require different corrections.

10. Worked example one: a career decision

Illustrative numbers, not study data. A person considers two jobs. Job A pays more but requires a long commute. Job B pays less but gives more control over daily time. At first, the person predicts a happiness score of 9 out of 10 for Job A because they focus on the excitement of the higher salary. They predict 7 for Job B. These are guesses, not measurements.

To make the forecast more complete, they write a typical future weekday for each job. They estimate commuting, time with family, daily work tasks and the frequency of high-pressure periods. They also ask people in similar roles about ordinary days rather than only career highlights. The revised forecast may still favor Job A, but it now includes conditions that will recur.

The person should also distinguish immediate joy from long-term fit. A job offer can create a strong short-term reaction while daily satisfaction depends on different features. Adaptation may reduce the excitement of either offer, but it may not remove the cost of a difficult commute. The practical question is not “Which choice sounds more impressive?” It is “Which life pattern best meets my goals and constraints?”

The numerical ratings here illustrate how the information considered can change a forecast. They do not imply that a seven-point rating is scientifically comparable across people or that the revised forecast must be more accurate in every case.

11. Worked example two: forecasting a repeated experience

Illustrative numbers, not study data. A person predicts that a new weekly activity will produce an enjoyment score of 8 out of 10 each time. They attend four sessions and record immediate scores of 6, 7, 5 and 7. The mean of those reports is 6.25. At the end of the month, they remember the activity as a 9 because the final session was especially meaningful.

Three values now exist: the initial forecast of 8, the mean recorded experience of 6.25, and the retrospective rating of 9. They answer different questions. The mean describes the selected immediate reports. The remembered score describes a later evaluation. The forecast describes what was expected before participation. None is automatically the sole correct measure of value.

If the person's goal is enjoyable weekly time, the immediate scores deserve weight. If the goal is a memorable achievement or a strong sense of progress, the later evaluation may matter more. The person should also consider whether the four sessions represent future sessions. A short record is better than no evidence, but it is not a guarantee.

This approach turns a vague forecast into a comparison of predictions and observations. It also prevents the most memorable session from silently replacing the full record. Repeated measurement can improve personal decisions, although keeping records can itself change how people experience an activity.

12. Can other people's experience improve a forecast?

People sometimes believe that detailed information about an event is the best basis for predicting their reaction. Another option is to learn how a similar person actually felt. Researchers call this surrogation: using another person's experience as a guide to one's own future feelings.

Gilbert, Killingsworth, Eyre and Wilson reported two experiments in a 2009 Science paper. Undergraduates predicted reactions to a short social encounter or a peer evaluation. In the tested conditions, information about another person's reaction produced more accurate forecasts than detailed information about the event itself. Participants and independent judges did not expect this advantage. The study was small and used specific tasks; it does not prove that any stranger is a good guide for every decision. [5]

The finding is useful because it challenges a common assumption that one's future response is too unique for another person's experience to help. People differ, but shared circumstances can still provide evidence. The quality of the comparison depends on how similar the other person is and whether their account describes ordinary experience.

For high-stakes choices, it is sensible to combine several kinds of information: facts about the option, reports from people in comparable conditions and the decision-maker's own values. None of these sources should be treated as infallible.

13. Evidence strength, sampling and uncertainty

Affective forecasting research uses experiments, questionnaires, daily reports and retrospective ratings. Each method has strengths and limits. Experiments can test a proposed mechanism by changing what participants consider. Daily reports can capture feelings closer to the time they occur. Retrospective reports reveal what people later remember and may predict their next choice.

Many influential experiments involved university students or relatively brief events. This limits how confidently results can be extended to major life changes, different cultures or long periods. Researchers must also decide what counts as forecast error. A person may forecast “I will feel sad for weeks,” but later surveys may ask about general life satisfaction. These are not the same measure.

Measurement timing matters. A reaction one hour after an event may differ from the reaction one month later. Repeated assessments can distinguish a short peak from a lasting change. Attrition also matters: if people with the strongest reactions stop responding, later averages can mislead.

Review work by Wilson and Gilbert in 2005 summarized several mechanisms, including focalism and the way people make sense of events. It provides a useful framework, but a review is not a replacement for checking the design and limits of each original study. [8]

Good evidence supports conditional claims: some forecasts are biased in identifiable ways under tested conditions. It does not support the claim that people never know what will make them happy.

14. Competing views: accurate preferences or biased forecasts?

One view emphasizes systematic errors. People focus too much on one event, neglect adaptation, and rely on unusual memories. This view predicts that broader information and feedback can improve decisions. Experiments on focalism and surrogation support parts of this account. [2] [5]

A second view emphasizes the information available to the person. The future may be genuinely uncertain. A forecast can be reasonable given what was known and still turn out wrong because the situation changed. Some people also have extensive experience with the choice at hand and may predict it well. This view warns against calling every mismatch a cognitive bias.

A third view asks whether researchers measure the right objective. A person might rationally choose an experience with moderate daily pleasure because it supports identity, learning, relationships or a meaningful memory. The fact that immediate pleasure and later evaluation differ does not establish that one is irrational. The 2003 spring-break study helps show why the distinction matters. [6]

These views can coexist. A decision-maker can have meaningful values, face genuine uncertainty and still use a biased forecast. Good analysis should identify the specific forecast, the outcome that matters and the evidence available before judging the decision.

15. A practical method for better forecasts

A more careful forecast begins by naming the target. Are you predicting your mood on the first day, average daily satisfaction, the time needed to recover, or whether you will later want to repeat the experience? These are different questions and should be measured separately.

Next, expand the imagined future. List the ordinary activities that will continue alongside the event. This counters excessive focus on one outcome. Then ask how the situation might change over time and what coping resources or constraints will remain. Avoid assuming that adjustment will always be complete.

Check whether your present state is distorting the choice. If hunger, stress or excitement is unusually strong, compare your judgment with a calmer time or with earlier records. Review typical cases, not only the most memorable one. When useful, ask several people who have lived through similar conditions about their ordinary experience.

Finally, record a prediction and compare it with later observations. A short log can reveal whether you repeatedly overestimate excitement, underestimate practical burdens or misjudge how long disappointment lasts. Treat the result as personal evidence, not as a universal rule. Repeated experience may improve forecasting, but changing circumstances can limit what the past predicts.

The aim is not to eliminate emotion from decisions. It is to give future feelings the same careful attention that people give costs, time and other measurable consequences.

16. Wider effects: consumer choices, work and public decisions

Affective forecasts shape decisions far beyond individual happiness. People may buy products for an imagined lifestyle, accept work because of anticipated status, or avoid difficult conversations because they expect lasting distress. In each case, the predicted emotion influences action before the actual outcome is known.

Businesses can use this knowledge responsibly by showing ordinary use conditions rather than only the most exciting moment. But it can also be used manipulatively: marketing can emphasize a vivid peak and leave out recurring costs. Consumers benefit when they compare expected everyday use with the exceptional image presented in an advertisement.

Workplace choices involve the same distinction. A promotion may offer real financial and professional benefits, while the daily role may change time, autonomy and relationships. Evaluating both the event and the repeated routine gives a more complete decision basis.

Public policy also raises questions about whose experience counts and how to measure it. Surveys of life satisfaction, momentary feelings and remembered experience do not measure identical things. Policymakers should be clear about which outcome a program is meant to improve and should not assume that one rating captures all aspects of well-being.

Philosophically, the topic highlights a limit of simple preference models. A person's present desire is evidence about what matters to them, but future experience can supply information they do not yet have. Sound decisions combine present values, realistic predictions and the ability to revise choices when new evidence appears.

Key lessons

  1. Affective forecasting predicts future feelings; it can concern direction, strength or duration.
  2. Correctly predicting sadness does not mean correctly predicting how long it will dominate life.
  3. Focalism leaves ordinary future activities out of the imagined picture.
  4. Adaptation matters, but it is neither complete nor guaranteed for every event.
  5. Present hunger, stress and excitement can change preferences for future situations.
  6. A vivid memory may be accurate yet unrepresentative of typical experience.
  7. Moment-to-moment experience and later memory can give different evaluations.
  8. Comparable people's actual experiences can sometimes improve forecasts.
  9. Define the outcome you care about before deciding which feeling measure to use.
  10. Compare predictions with later observations to learn where your own errors occur.

Knowledge check: questions and answers

1. What is the difference between impact bias and durability bias?

Answer: Impact bias concerns overestimating an event's emotional effect in some settings. Durability bias concerns overestimating how long a reaction lasts. A forecast can make one error without the other.

2. How did the focalism experiments change some predictions?

Answer: When participants considered other future activities, they were less likely to overpredict how long a football outcome would affect their happiness. This suggests that a broader view can correct some forecasts.

3. Why can a true memory still mislead a forecast?

Answer: The remembered event may be unusually intense or rare. Even an accurate memory can be a poor sample of ordinary future experiences.

4. What is the difference between experienced and remembered utility?

Answer: Experienced utility concerns feelings during an event. Remembered utility is a later evaluation. The two may differ because memory gives different weight to selected moments.

5. How could a person improve a forecast for a major decision?

Answer: Define the feeling and time period, consider ordinary future activities, account for adaptation and present state, examine typical comparable experiences, and compare later observations with the prediction.
