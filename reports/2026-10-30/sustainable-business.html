Can Data Centres Save Water Without Using More Energy?

How can cooling systems reduce freshwater pressure without shifting the cost to electricity and emissions?

Executive summary

Data centres need electricity to run computers and to remove the heat those computers produce. Some cooling systems use evaporation to reject heat. This can reduce electricity demand but consume water. Other systems use less on-site water and may need more electricity, especially in hot weather. A water-saving claim therefore needs two checks: how much water is consumed at the site, and what happens to energy use and water consumption in electricity generation. Location also matters. One litre used in a water-stressed basin can create a different risk from one litre used where supply is secure. A 2024 Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory report estimated 66 billion litres of direct water consumption by United States data centres in 2023. An original 2021 study found that part of the sector's water footprint was linked to stressed watersheds. New International Energy Agency analysis published in April 2026 shows that data-centre electricity demand continues to grow. This guide explains cooling systems, measurement, trade-offs, operating costs, water risk, and practical decisions. Both calculations are illustrative, and reported averages must not replace site-specific engineering.

Central question: what does a better cooling system mean?

Central question: Can a data-centre operator cut water use without increasing electricity costs, carbon emissions, or reliability risks? The answer depends on the facility, climate, power source, and water supply. A technology that performs well in one place can perform poorly in another.

A good decision has at least four parts. It measures direct water consumption at the site. It measures electricity used by computing and cooling. It estimates water consumed to generate that electricity. It checks whether the local water source is already under pressure. A low water figure at the building boundary does not automatically mean a low total water footprint.

The business question is equally important. Data centres must keep servers within safe operating conditions. Cooling failure can interrupt customer services and damage equipment. A solution must be reliable at the hottest relevant outdoor conditions, maintainable by local staff, and financially sensible over its useful life. The report uses official energy and water research and distinguishes measured historical figures from scenarios and illustrative calculations.

1. Why computing has become a water issue

Early computer rooms used air conditioning to protect equipment. As server density rose, operators developed larger facilities with dedicated air-handling systems, chillers, cooling towers, and better controls. Hyperscale data centres now house large groups of servers. Some AI systems also use high-power processors packed into dense racks. The heat must leave the building continuously.

The growth in computing demand has increased attention to electricity and water. The International Energy Agency estimated 415 terawatt-hours of global data-centre electricity consumption in 2024 in its April 2025 report. Its April 2026 update estimated about 485 terawatt-hours in 2025 and projected roughly 950 terawatt-hours in 2030 under its central outlook. These are global electricity figures, not water measurements, and the 2030 figure is a scenario. [1][2]

Water matters because many cooling towers reject heat through evaporation. Electricity production can also consume water, depending on the generation technology and location. A facility can therefore use little water directly while still being linked to water consumption elsewhere. The problem is physical and local: a dry region may face more severe effects from the same amount of consumption than a region with abundant water.

2. Six measures that should not be mixed

Water withdrawal is water taken from a source. Some of it may return. Water consumption is the portion no longer available for immediate reuse in the same local system, often because it evaporates. The distinction is essential: a plant can withdraw a large volume and return much of it, or withdraw less and consume a large share.

Direct water is water used at the data-centre site, such as cooling-tower makeup water. Indirect water includes water consumed in producing the electricity supplied to the site. Water stress describes pressure on available freshwater resources. A global litre total cannot show local stress by itself.

Power usage effectiveness (PUE) is total facility electricity divided by IT equipment electricity. Lower values usually mean less overhead energy for the same IT load. Water usage effectiveness (WUE) is annual site water use in litres divided by annual IT electricity in kilowatt-hours. The U.S. Department of Energy defines these measures in its federal cooling guidance. WUE is a site metric and does not capture indirect water use at power plants. [3]

Life-cycle assessment follows environmental effects across a defined system boundary. For a data centre, this may include equipment manufacturing, building construction, electricity, water, maintenance, and end of life. A report should say which parts are included and which are excluded.

3. First understand where the heat goes

Nearly all the electricity used by servers becomes heat within the facility. The operator must move that heat away from processors, memory, storage, and power equipment. Fans can move warm air to cooling coils. Liquid loops can take heat from a chip or rack to a heat exchanger. A chiller may then transfer heat to a condenser loop. Finally, the system must reject heat to outdoor air, a cooling tower, or another useful sink.

The final heat-rejection step is central to the water trade-off. In an evaporative tower, a small part of the circulating water evaporates. That phase change carries heat away efficiently. The system also drains some water to control dissolved minerals. It needs replacement water for evaporation and blowdown. In a dry cooler, heat moves to outdoor air without routine evaporative makeup water, but fans and compressors may need more electricity when outdoor air is hot.

Direct-to-chip liquid cooling does not by itself tell us how much freshwater a facility consumes. Many liquid loops are closed and recirculate the same coolant. The water outcome depends on how the facility rejects the captured heat. A liquid-cooled rack can connect to a dry system, a cooling tower, or a hybrid system.

4. The engineering decision in seven steps

Step 1 — Record the IT load. Measure hourly server electricity, peak rack power, and expected growth. A cooling design for today's load may be too small for future high-density processors. Avoid using installed electrical capacity as if it were the same as measured energy consumption.

Step 2 — Record the climate. Use local temperature, humidity, and seasonal extremes. Evaporative cooling works differently in dry and humid air. Dry cooling becomes harder during hot periods. The correct design condition is a reliability question, not merely an annual average.

Step 3 — Map the water supply. Identify municipal, groundwater, reclaimed, or other sources. Check drought rules, competing users, discharge quality, and permits. A facility should not assume that recycled water is always available or that using it has no local cost.

Step 4 — Compare heat-rejection options. Model air cooling, evaporative towers, direct liquid cooling, and hybrids under the same IT load and service requirement. Include pump and fan electricity, chillers, treatment, maintenance, and backup capacity.

Step 5 — Estimate direct and indirect water. Calculate site consumption and then estimate water associated with electricity production using relevant regional generation data. Do not use a single global water factor for every grid.

Step 6 — Test economics and reliability. Include water tariffs, electricity tariffs, capital cost, spare parts, skilled maintenance, downtime risk, and changes in power demand during hot weather. Evaluate more than one weather and demand scenario.

Step 7 — Measure after installation. Install meters, publish consistent boundaries, and review performance. Compare real outcomes with the model. If a change lowers site WUE but raises total electricity and grid-water consumption, report both effects.

The figure separates the two main paths. Water used at the facility is only one part of the question. Water used by electricity generation may occur far from the building. Some generation sources use little operational water; others use more. The size of each path depends on the local system.

6. Cooling choices and their trade-offs

Evaporative cooling uses water to remove heat. It can lower electricity demand in suitable conditions. Its risks include water availability, scaling, biological control, and discharge management. Better water treatment and higher cycles of concentration can reduce unnecessary blowdown. The U.S. Department of Energy notes that moving from three to six cycles can reduce cooling-tower makeup water by about 20% under the stated conditions. This is guidance for a particular change, not a universal data-centre saving. [3]

Dry cooling rejects heat to outdoor air. It can reduce on-site water consumption sharply, but may need larger heat exchangers and more electricity in hot weather. The effect on carbon emissions depends on the electricity source and the hour of use. Dry cooling also has capital and noise considerations.

Hybrid systems switch between dry and evaporative operation. They may reduce water use during cool periods while protecting performance in hot weather. Controls, sensors, maintenance, and reliable switchover become important. Liquid cooling can move heat efficiently from dense processors, but the final heat rejection still determines site water use. The correct comparison is the complete cooling system.

7. What the U.S. laboratory reports establish

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's 2024 United States Data Center Energy Usage Report, published on 19 December 2024, estimated 66 billion litres of direct water consumption in U.S. data centres in 2023. It also modelled electricity demand and future scenarios through 2028. The water figure is a modelled sector estimate for 2023, not a meter reading from every facility. [4]

A subsequent U.S. Department of Energy research record, published on 18 June 2026, describes the laboratory's 2025 update. It estimates that data centres could use 9.5% to 15.3% of U.S. electricity in 2030, with a central estimate of 11.8%. Those are electricity scenarios. They should not be converted into water forecasts without cooling and power-generation assumptions. [5]

The data show why facility-level action matters. A national total can rise even when individual sites become more efficient, because the number and scale of facilities may grow. Conversely, an efficient new site can increase water pressure if it is built in a stressed basin. National averages should guide attention, while local assessments guide decisions.

8. What original research finds about location

Siddik, Shehabi, and Marston published a spatial study in Environmental Research Letters in 2021. The authors estimated direct and indirect water footprints for U.S. data centres using a bottom-up method. They found that about one-fifth of the sector's direct water footprint was associated with moderately to highly water-stressed watersheds. They also found that nearly half of servers were at least partly supplied by power plants in water-stressed regions. [6]

These results matter because a litre is not a complete measure of local harm. The source basin, season, other users, and return flows affect risk. A site in a water-stressed area may need more careful controls even if its WUE looks better than an industry average.

The study also has limits. Its facility and electricity assumptions reflect the period studied. The data-centre fleet, cooling technology, and power grid have changed since then. Its percentages should not be presented as verified 2026 conditions. The method remains useful: locate water demand and electricity supply, then assess water stress at the relevant places.

9. Worked example one: compare site WUE

Illustrative data; no real facility is represented. Suppose a data centre uses 20 million kilowatt-hours of IT electricity in one year. Cooling design A consumes 12 million litres of site water. Its WUE is 12,000,000 ÷ 20,000,000 = 0.60 litres per IT kWh. Design B consumes 4 million litres for the same IT load. Its WUE is 4,000,000 ÷ 20,000,000 = 0.20 litres per IT kWh.

Design B cuts direct site water consumption by 8 million litres, or about 67%, under these assumptions. This is a clear improvement in the site-water measure. It is not yet proof of lower total water consumption or lower environmental harm.

Now suppose B uses more electricity for cooling. If that extra electricity comes from generation with a high water footprint, some of the direct saving may be offset elsewhere. If it comes from low-water electricity, the saving may remain large. A complete comparison needs the extra electricity, the regional generation mix, and local water stress. WUE is a useful meter, not the final decision.

10. The electricity side of the trade-off

The cooling system's power demand changes with outside conditions. Evaporative cooling can reduce chiller work by using water to reject heat. Dry cooling can reduce site water but increase compressor or fan electricity during hot hours. A hybrid system may reduce both for part of the year, but its control strategy matters.

The International Energy Agency's April 2026 report, Key Questions on Energy and AI, found that global data-centre electricity consumption grew by about 17% in 2025. It also described a central outlook of roughly 950 terawatt-hours in 2030. These figures indicate a growing load, but they do not specify which cooling design each facility will use. [2]

The source of electricity matters for both emissions and water. A kilowatt-hour from one plant can have different operational water requirements from a kilowatt-hour produced elsewhere. Grid mix also changes by hour and season. The most useful model therefore tests when cooling electricity is needed, not only how much it uses over the year.

Power reliability is a separate constraint. A design that saves water on average but cannot cool equipment during a heat wave is not acceptable. Operators must evaluate backup power, reserve cooling capacity, equipment degradation, and response to a supply interruption.

11. Worked example two: water savings versus energy cost

Illustrative data; prices and performance are assumptions. Consider the same 20 million IT kWh per year. Design A uses 24 million total facility kWh, so PUE = 24 ÷ 20 = 1.20. Design B uses 25 million total facility kWh, so PUE = 25 ÷ 20 = 1.25. Design B therefore uses 1 million extra kWh per year.

Assume electricity costs ₹8 per kWh and water costs ₹80 per thousand litres. The extra electricity cost of B is 1,000,000 × ₹8 = ₹80 lakh per year. From the first example, B saves 8 million litres. The annual water-bill saving is 8,000 × ₹80 = ₹6.4 lakh. The net direct operating-cost increase is ₹73.6 lakh before capital, maintenance, wastewater, carbon, or water-risk costs.

Under these assumptions, the water bill alone does not justify design B. The decision could change if water is scarce, drought restrictions threaten operations, power prices differ, or cooling technology performs better. The example shows why the operator needs a multi-year, risk-adjusted comparison rather than a single efficiency ratio.

12. Business economics beyond the utility bill

A data-centre cooling investment has capital cost, financing cost, operating cost, and failure risk. Water treatment chemicals, pumps, filters, maintenance labour, spare parts, and disposal of concentrated wastewater can be material. The local price of water may be low even when the community faces a high scarcity cost. That difference creates a policy and reputation risk.

An operator can compare designs with a discounted cash-flow model. It should include expected electricity and water costs, major replacement cycles, maintenance contracts, and possible downtime. Scenario tests should cover drought restrictions, heat waves, rising electricity tariffs, and changes in computing load. The output should show ranges, not a false single-number forecast.

The value of reliability is often much greater than the value of a small energy saving. A cooling failure can interrupt customer contracts and critical digital services. Redundancy may therefore be rational even if it increases equipment cost. This is a reason to improve controls and backup systems, not a reason to ignore water consumption.

Investors and customers can ask for measured PUE, measured WUE, source-water information, and clear system boundaries. Those data allow better decisions than broad claims such as “water positive” without a defined accounting method.

13. Why water stress changes the decision

Water scarcity depends on local supply and demand. A facility that consumes one million litres in a wet region can have a different impact from the same consumption during a dry season in a stressed basin. Municipal systems may also have competing needs from households, farming, hospitals, and industry.

The World Resources Institute's Aqueduct 4.0 methodology, published on 16 August 2023, provides indicators of current and future water risk. It is a screening tool. It cannot replace a local water study, permit review, or consultation with the water authority. [7]

Operators should map the origin of water, not only the pipe entering the site. Reclaimed wastewater may reduce freshwater withdrawals, but it requires reliable treatment and supply. Groundwater may be less visible than municipal water but can have serious long-term effects. A public assessment should state seasonal volumes, water quality, treatment, return flows, and restrictions during drought.

A good water-risk review also examines who bears the cost of supply upgrades. If a new facility requires public infrastructure, the operator and local government should explain how costs are allocated and how other users are protected.

14. Serious competing views

View one: prioritise on-site water savings. Supporters of dry cooling argue that local freshwater can be scarce and that data centres should minimise consumption where communities already face pressure. This view is strongest in water-stressed regions and when electricity comes from low-water, low-emission sources.

View two: optimise energy and water together. Engineers favouring evaporative or hybrid cooling argue that a water-only target can raise electricity demand and emissions. Where water is abundant and electricity is carbon-intensive, a carefully controlled evaporative system may have lower overall harm. This view requires clear evidence about source water and indirect effects.

View three: focus first on computing efficiency and siting. Some analysts argue that reducing the energy required for each useful computation and choosing a better location can matter more than switching cooling hardware. Efficient processors, high utilisation, and appropriate workload scheduling can lower heat production. However, rapid growth in computing demand can outweigh per-task efficiency gains.

These are real trade-offs. A facility should compare complete systems under the same service level, climate, grid assumptions, and water-risk conditions. There is no single best cooling technology for all sites.

15. How to test sustainability claims

A credible claim names the facility, period, metric, and boundary. For example, “site water consumption per IT kilowatt-hour decreased” is a testable statement when both quantities are metered consistently. It does not imply that indirect water use or carbon emissions also decreased.

A company should distinguish water withdrawal from consumption and report both when relevant. It should say whether the numbers include offices, construction, manufacturing, and off-site electricity generation. It should identify water sources and treatment methods. Annual averages should be accompanied by peak-season information when water risk is seasonal.

Comparisons need caution. Two facilities can have different climates, server loads, redundancy needs, and electricity sources. A lower PUE or WUE does not by itself prove better total performance. A credible report also explains whether data are measured, estimated, or modelled and whether a third party has checked them.

Claims about “replenishment” require separate evidence. Funding a water project elsewhere may provide benefits, but it does not necessarily replace water consumed from a specific stressed basin at a specific time. Physical consumption and voluntary projects should be reported separately.

16. A practical improvement programme

Start with metering. Measure IT electricity, total facility electricity, cooling electricity, makeup water, blowdown, and seasonal operating conditions. Record missing data and instrument errors. This baseline is necessary before promising a percentage reduction.

Next, fix avoidable losses. Review temperature and humidity settings within equipment limits, repair leaks, improve filters and water treatment, and check whether pumps and fans run unnecessarily. The U.S. Department of Energy identifies these operational measures as potential savings in suitable systems. [3]

Then test design options. Model dry, evaporative, and hybrid systems against the same IT load and weather data. Include hot-day reliability, future processor density, water restrictions, and grid-water impacts. Ask for independent engineering review before major capital changes.

Finally, publish a small dashboard with site water consumption, WUE, PUE, source-water mix, water stress, and changes in computing load. Explain why any metric improved or worsened. A transparent programme is more useful than a single headline target.

17. Wider effects: communities, grids, and policy

Water and electricity choices can affect local households and businesses. A new data centre may create jobs, tax revenue, and digital capacity, while also increasing demand on power lines, water pipes, and wastewater treatment. These effects vary greatly by location and should be assessed before a permit is granted.

A community may prefer a design that uses more electricity but little freshwater during drought. A grid operator may prefer a design that reduces hot-day electricity peaks. A regulator must weigh both concerns. Clear tariffs and infrastructure agreements can reduce the risk that existing customers pay for upgrades required by a new large user.

There is also an information problem. Operators may know more about their expected loads and cooling plans than local authorities. Publishing consistent performance and project assumptions can improve decisions. But disclosure must protect security-sensitive details. The goal is enough information to assess water, energy, and public costs without exposing critical infrastructure.

The main uncertainty is future demand. AI and other digital services may become more efficient per task while total use rises. The IEA's 2026 update highlights this tension. Better cooling helps, but it does not settle the effects of total sector growth.

18. Nine lessons to retain

  1. Servers create heat; every cooling system must reject it somewhere.
  2. Evaporative cooling can save electricity while consuming water.
  3. Dry cooling can save on-site water but may increase hot-day power use.
  4. Direct liquid cooling does not define the final water footprint by itself.
  5. WUE measures site water per IT energy; PUE measures total energy per IT energy.
  6. Indirect water from electricity production can change the comparison.
  7. Water stress and season matter as much as annual litres.
  8. National estimates and future scenarios must not replace facility meters.
  9. Good decisions combine reliability, capital cost, operating cost, water risk, and transparent measurement.

Five knowledge-check questions and answers

1. Why does a cooling tower consume water?

Part of its circulating water evaporates to remove heat. The system also discharges some water to control dissolved minerals and needs replacement water.

2. Does a lower WUE prove a lower total water footprint?

No. WUE measures site water per unit of IT electricity. It does not include water consumed to produce electricity or the local scarcity of the water source.

3. Why can dry cooling raise electricity demand?

When outdoor air is hot, fans and compressors may need more energy to reject the same heat. The effect depends on the design and climate.

4. What does the 2024 U.S. laboratory report's 66-billion-litre figure describe?

A modelled estimate of direct water consumption by U.S. data centres in 2023. It is not a 2026 observation or a measure of all indirect water.

5. What should a company compare before changing its cooling system?

Equal IT service and reliability, direct and indirect water, electricity and emissions, seasonal water stress, capital and operating costs, and performance during extreme heat.
