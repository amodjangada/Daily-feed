Executive summary

Before people built thermometers, Earth experienced long cold periods, warmer intervals, volcanic eruptions and large changes in atmospheric gases. Scientists can study part of this history by drilling into the thick ice sheets of Antarctica and Greenland. Snow falls, is buried, and slowly becomes ice. The frozen water preserves chemical evidence of the conditions in which it formed. Tiny air bubbles preserve samples of the atmosphere from the time their pores finally closed. The two records are related, but their ages are not identical. [1] [6]

Deep Antarctic ice has already given a well-established record extending about 800,000 years into the past. The European Beyond EPICA project drilled 2,800 metres at Little Dome C and reported a continuous record reaching at least 1.2 million years. Scientists continued to analyse these samples through 2026. The new material may clarify why the rhythm of major ice ages changed about a million years ago. [3] [11] [12]

This guide explains what is measured directly, what is inferred, how ice and gas are dated, and where uncertainty enters. It also separates three different questions: what happened, when it happened, and what caused it. Ice cores are exceptionally strong evidence for past atmospheric composition, but temperature estimates need calibration and interpretation. Two worked calculations are illustrative, not new measurements. [9]

1. What can a column of ice actually tell us?

A climate archive is material that preserves evidence of earlier conditions. Ocean sediment, tree rings, cave deposits and ice are different archives. Ice is special because it contains both a solid record and, in many places, enclosed air. The solid part includes the isotopic composition of water, mineral dust, salts, volcanic material and trace chemicals. The bubbles contain gases such as carbon dioxide and methane. The measurements come from physical samples, not a model's reconstruction alone. [2] [6]

Each signal answers a different question. Carbon dioxide measured in extracted bubble air estimates an atmospheric mixing ratio. A water-isotope ratio is a proxy, an indirect indicator of conditions during snowfall. Dust can suggest changes in winds, dry land, or transport paths. Sulfate spikes can help identify major volcanic events, but a chemical peak alone may not identify a unique volcano. Ice cores also help study pollution in recent centuries.

There is an important spatial limit. The isotope composition of snowfall at one Antarctic site describes processes affecting that site. It is not a direct thermometer for the global mean temperature. In contrast, well-mixed long-lived gases can support wider atmospheric inferences, subject to checks for preservation and measurement error. [1] [9]

2. How snowfall becomes a record

At a cold site, some snow remains after summer. More snow covers it the next year. The weight of new layers compresses the older snow. Its sharp crystals change shape and the open pore space becomes smaller. The partly compacted snow is called firn. Over time, pores close and the material becomes dense glacial ice. At suitable sites, this process continues for many thousands of years. [6]

Annual layers can sometimes be seen in chemistry or dust, much like seasonal patterns in other natural archives. But deeper ice is squeezed and thinned. A layer that began thick may become a very thin band. Flow can fold or disturb layers, and ice near the bed may melt. Thus depth is usually related to age, but one metre does not represent a fixed number of years everywhere.

Researchers choose drill sites carefully. A high snowfall rate can give excellent detail but may bury old ice beyond practical drilling depth. Very low snowfall can preserve an ancient record in less thickness, but the annual detail becomes poor. Ground heat and moving ice can damage the oldest material. The best site is a compromise among age, continuity, resolution and physical access. [10]

3. A short history of the measurements

Early polar expeditions recovered ice for physical study. As laboratory methods improved in the twentieth century, researchers could measure stable isotopes, particles and gases from smaller samples. The Vostok Antarctic core helped establish a long connection among temperature indicators, greenhouse gases and ice-age cycles. The international EPICA project extended the Dome C climate record to hundreds of thousands of years. Its 2004 paper described eight glacial cycles, initially using a record around 740,000 years long. Later studies extended the carbon dioxide and methane records to 800,000 years. [3] [4] [5]

These were not simply longer versions of the same experiment. Each extension required new drilling, more sensitive instruments, careful time scales and checks against independent sites. The research teams also archived numerical data so others could reanalyse the measurements. NOAA maintains a public collection of ice-core studies, which supports comparison and replication. [2]

The next target was older ice around the Mid-Pleistocene Transition. Beyond EPICA reached bedrock at Little Dome C in January 2025. By October 2025, the British Antarctic Survey reported analysis of a continuous sequence reaching at least 1.2 million years. In 2026, scientists were still assessing detailed gas and isotope results and how much ancient signal could be recovered. [10] [11] [16]

4. How scientists collect and protect the core

Drilling starts with maps, radar and studies of ice movement. Researchers look for layers that have stayed in order. A deep ice drill cuts a narrow cylinder, lifts it, and repeats the process. Every segment receives a depth and location record. A sample without this information would lose much of its scientific value. The Beyond EPICA drill reached about 2.8 kilometres below the surface, near bedrock. [10] [12]

Cold transport matters. If a core warms, it may fracture, melt or lose gas. Laboratories divide the ice into pieces for different measurements. One team may melt a narrow strip continuously to measure dust and dissolved ions. Another may crush or melt ice in a sealed system and analyse the released gas. Clean rooms and blanks help detect contamination. Instruments must be calibrated against known reference materials. [1] [11]

Scientists do not assume that every piece of very old ice is a trustworthy timeline. The lowest part of the Beyond EPICA core includes highly deformed ice whose origin is harder to determine. A sample can be ancient yet unsuitable for reading events in order. This is why a continuous, ordered record is more informative than merely finding the oldest isolated piece of ice. [10]

5. Air bubbles: a sample with a delayed seal

Fresh snow is full of air. While snow remains porous firn, gases can move between the atmosphere and the spaces among grains. When the pores finally close, bubbles trap air. The gas may be younger than the surrounding ice because the ice began as snow long before it sealed. This difference is called Δage, or the ice-age minus gas-age difference. [8]

The closing depth varies with temperature, snow accumulation and density. A study of Taylor Glacier explains that gas enclosure can occur tens of metres below the surface, and that age differences vary widely between sites and climate periods. Researchers model the densification of firn and compare gas records across sites to estimate the difference. It is incorrect to put a gas measurement and a water-isotope measurement at the same depth and automatically assign them the same date. [8]

Gas measurements also have limits. Some air can mix before the pores close, which smooths short changes. Diffusion, cracks and gas loss can affect some samples. Researchers check whether the composition of major gases and other indicators is consistent with intact air. Independent cores strengthen the conclusion. Even with these limits, trapped air gives unusually direct evidence for ancient atmospheric greenhouse gases. [4] [6]

6. Water isotopes: a temperature clue, not a direct thermometer

Water molecules contain oxygen and hydrogen. Most oxygen atoms are oxygen-16, but a smaller fraction are oxygen-18. Hydrogen also has a heavier stable form called deuterium. These forms have slightly different masses. Evaporation, condensation and transport therefore separate them in measurable ways. The process is called isotopic fractionation. Cold conditions generally change the ratios of heavy to light isotopes in snow at polar sites. [1]

A laboratory measures ratios such as δ¹⁸O or δD, usually expressed as a difference in parts per thousand from a standard. Scientists compare modern snowfall, instrumental temperatures, ice-core records and physical models to infer past temperature changes. The relation is affected by where moisture came from, the season of snowfall, atmospheric paths and changes in the ice sheet. It is therefore not a universal rule such as one isotope unit equals one degree everywhere.

Some older ice has lost fine detail through diffusion, a slow mixing of isotopic signals in solid ice. In May 2026, Beyond EPICA researchers presented work on this signal loss in the oldest parts of the core. Recovering a broad glacial trend can be easier than recovering a short climate event from the same compressed, smoothed layer. [16]

7. How do we know which year a layer represents?

At some sites and depths, scientists count seasonal patterns in chemistry and dust. They can also match volcanic sulfate layers across separate cores. In older Antarctic ice, annual layers are too thin or uncertain to count continuously. Researchers combine ice-flow models, known time markers, orbital signals, gas measurements and comparisons among multiple cores. The result is an age–depth model: an estimate of age for each depth, with uncertainty. [7]

AICC2012 is an example of a multi-site Antarctic chronology. It uses several physical and chemical constraints rather than one isolated clock. Published work compared its ages with earlier chronologies and found differences that can reach thousands of years at some old intervals. That does not mean the entire record is unreliable. It means a claim about an event leading another by a few hundred years needs much more care than a claim that both varied over tens of thousands of years. [7]

Researchers maintain separate age scales for ice and for trapped gas, then align them with a model of firn close-off and other markers. When a paper changes the age model, an apparent lead or lag between CO₂ and temperature can change too. A date is an inference built from evidence, not a label printed on the original snowflake. [8] [13]

8. Diagram: from snowfall to a climate finding

Worked example 1: ice age versus gas age

Illustrative numbers, not a measured ice core. Imagine that a layer of snow fell 10,000 years before the present. Its pores stayed connected to the atmosphere until a time corresponding to 9,400 years before the present. When the pores closed, the enclosed air became isolated. In this simplified case the ice is 10,000 years old, while the gas is 9,400 years old. Therefore Δage = 10,000 − 9,400 = 600 years.

If a water-isotope change and a carbon dioxide change occur at the same depth, comparing both at an age of 10,000 years would misplace the gas event by 600 years. Researchers would first assign the gas its own age and then examine whether the changes coincide. Real close-off is gradual, so bubbles can contain an age distribution rather than air from one exact instant. [8]

The calculation shows why time alignment matters. It does not prove that gas always lags climate by 600 years, nor that the true climate system has a fixed delay. The 600-year figure was chosen only to demonstrate the arithmetic. Real Δage depends on site conditions and can vary substantially across a core.

9. What the 800,000-year greenhouse-gas record shows

The EPICA and related Antarctic cores show repeated large swings between cold glacial periods and warmer interglacial periods. Lüthi and colleagues extended the CO₂ record to 800,000 years in 2008. Loulergue and colleagues analysed methane over the same broad period. The records show strong links among greenhouse-gas concentrations, Antarctic climate indicators and the pacing of glacial cycles. They do not show a perfectly uniform pattern, and not every gas follows every local temperature shift in the same way. [4] [5]

NOAA's 2025 historical chart reports that CO₂ in the last 800,000-year ice-core record remained below 300 parts per million, while the 2024 observed level was about 422.8 ppm. Parts per million describes the fraction of dry air made of CO₂ molecules. The modern value comes from direct air measurement, joined to the ancient ice-core record through overlapping and calibrated observations. [14]

The long record is strong evidence that recent atmospheric CO₂ concentrations are outside the range sampled by those eight glacial cycles. It does not by itself calculate future warming, nor does a single Antarctic temperature curve equal global temperature. Those further questions require energy-balance physics, multiple observations and climate models. [9]

Worked example 2: reading a CO₂ comparison correctly

Illustrative calculation using published rounded reference values. Take a pre-industrial concentration of 280 ppm and a 2024 concentration of 422.8 ppm, the latter reported by NOAA. The increase is 422.8 − 280 = 142.8 ppm. Relative to the pre-industrial baseline, the increase is 142.8 / 280 × 100 = 51%, rounded to the nearest whole percent. [14]

Now compare 422.8 ppm with 300 ppm, an upper bound for the ice-core range in NOAA's graphic. The modern value is at least 122.8 ppm higher, or more than 40% above that bound. These are two different comparisons with different denominators. They should not be mixed. The exact uppermost historical measurement in a particular dataset is not the same thing as a rounded 300-ppm reference line.

This arithmetic is a concentration comparison, not a calculation of climate sensitivity. It also does not mean that the same percentage change in CO₂ produces the same percentage change in temperature. To estimate the energy effect, scientists use the absorption properties of gases, feedbacks, and the response of oceans and ice. [9]

10. The new 1.2-million-year record: what changed by 2026?

The Beyond EPICA team drilled at Little Dome C, East Antarctica, and reached about 2,800 metres in January 2025. The British Antarctic Survey reported in October 2025 that the core contained a continuous sequence spanning at least 1.2 million years. By the start of 2026, the samples had been processed and distributed for further study, with additional ice arriving in Europe during 2026. Nature reported in May 2026 that the international collaboration had presented the extended climate record. [10] [11] [12] [15]

The difference between drilled, analysed and fully interpreted matters. A successful drill does not instantly provide a finished table of gas concentrations, exact dates and causal conclusions. Scientists must measure, calibrate, compare and publish those findings. The lowest, strongly deformed ice is particularly difficult to use as a continuous time series. [10]

The new core may help explain the Mid-Pleistocene Transition, when the dominant rhythm of large ice-age cycles changed. Its extra age range is scientifically valuable even if some oldest layers have lower time resolution than younger ice. Work presented at the 2026 European Geosciences Union meeting addressed gas components and isotope-signal preservation. These are active research problems, not settled results. [16]

11. Why did ice ages shift from 41,000 to about 100,000 years?

Earth's orbit and axial tilt vary slowly. These changes alter where and when sunlight reaches the planet. In particular, summer sunlight at high northern latitudes affects whether winter snow survives and can grow into a large ice sheet. Earlier in the Pleistocene, ice-volume cycles often followed a rhythm near 41,000 years, close to the cycle of Earth's axial tilt. Later, large glacial cycles became longer and more uneven, often described as roughly 100,000-year cycles. [3] [12]

Why the climate response changed remains debated. Possibilities involve the growth and stability of ice sheets, changes in the carbon cycle, the removal of old weathered material beneath ice, and nonlinear thresholds in how ice grows or retreats. These mechanisms can interact. The orbital changes are calculated well, but the same orbit does not always produce the same ice-sheet response.

A continuous record crossing the transition can test the order of events: when CO₂ changed, how Antarctic isotope signals responded, and whether dust or other environmental indicators changed with them. It will not by itself settle every mechanism. Researchers also need marine sediment records, ice-sheet physics and numerical models to test whether a proposed explanation works across the globe. [11] [15]

12. Does the ice prove that CO₂ caused every past warming?

Ice-core data show a strong relationship between greenhouse gases and temperature indicators over glacial cycles. A correlation is a measured association; it does not by itself identify the direction of cause. Orbital changes can start a regional climate response. Warming oceans can then release CO₂, and the additional CO₂ can amplify warming across the planet. In such a system, temperature can influence carbon dioxide and carbon dioxide can influence temperature. The order can vary by region and time scale. [9]

Claims that CO₂ always followed Antarctic temperature by a fixed number of centuries oversimplify the evidence. The age of ice differs from the age of gas. Parrenin and colleagues revised their relative age estimates using several Antarctic cores and reported no significant asynchrony at four rapid transitions during the last deglaciation. That finding does not prove that every glacial event was synchronous, but it shows why chronology must be tested before claiming a lead or lag. [13]

For modern climate change, the starting conditions differ from an orbital ice-age transition: people are adding greenhouse gases rapidly. Independent laboratory physics, modern atmospheric observations and Earth's energy budget establish the warming effect of added CO₂. Ice cores supply long historical context and tests of climate feedbacks; they are one part of the causal evidence. [9]

13. Two serious scientific debates

Debate A: what caused the Mid-Pleistocene Transition? One research approach gives more weight to the internal behaviour of ice sheets: their size, shape, bed conditions and threshold responses. Another gives greater weight to long-term changes in greenhouse gases and the ocean carbon cycle. Both can accept orbital pacing. They disagree about which feedback or slow change made the ice ages longer and larger. The new ice core can constrain the gas and temperature histories, but the final explanation must also match independent records and physical models. [12]

Debate B: how precisely can the oldest layers be read? Some analyses seek high-resolution detail by measuring more finely and modelling diffusion. Others emphasise the risk of reconstructing short changes that the ice has physically smoothed or deformed. This is a real measurement problem. More sophisticated restoration can recover information, but it can also amplify noise if assumptions are wrong. Researchers at EGU 2026 described this challenge for the oldest Beyond EPICA isotopes. [16]

These positions are compatible in part. A broad 1.2-million-year climate trend can be well supported even when the timing of a brief event remains uncertain. Good science states the level of detail that the sample can support instead of treating every point on a chart as equally precise.

14. What could make an interpretation wrong?

Dating error: Layers thin and flow; age models depend on assumptions and reference points. A date may shift when new evidence improves the chronology. Gas-age error: bubbles seal after snow falls and may contain air from a range of years. Diffusion: molecules and isotopes can smooth rapid changes. Disturbance: folding, melting or cracks can mix old and young material. Contamination: handling and instrument problems can add or remove traces. [7] [8]

Proxy error: isotope ratios depend on moisture source and snowfall season, not only local temperature. Spatial error: an Antarctic record cannot be substituted directly for global mean temperature. Sampling error: a thin layer of very old ice may average over centuries or millennia, hiding short events. Selection error: if researchers choose only the most convenient samples, they can miss disagreement among cores.

Scientists reduce these risks through blanks, reference gases, duplicate samples, multiple laboratories, several ice cores, independent dating markers and open data. Uncertainty should appear in plots and published tables. The IPCC's assessment separates the direct ice-core CO₂ record from global temperature reconstructions that use additional evidence. That distinction is a useful test when reading simplified climate charts. [9]

15. What should the next measurements answer?

First, can the new record place the change in ice-age rhythm on a reliable time scale? Second, did greenhouse gases and Antarctic climate indicators change in ways that distinguish competing explanations? Third, how much detail survives in the compressed deepest layers? Fourth, can independent laboratories reproduce the gas measurements? Fifth, do marine sediments and ice cores agree on the broad timing of major transitions? [11] [16]

Scientists should also state where the data end. Older isolated ice has been found in Antarctica, but an isolated ancient sample is not the same as an unbroken, dated series. A record can be long without being uniformly detailed. The strongest conclusions will come from combining the new ice with existing cores, ocean sediments, physical theory and transparent age models.

For a reader, the practical method is simple: identify the measured variable, its age scale, the place it represents, the calibration method and the size of uncertainty. Those five checks prevent many errors in interpreting dramatic charts about ancient climate.

Ten key lessons

  1. Snow can preserve chemistry long after it becomes ice.
  2. Air bubbles contain samples of ancient atmosphere, subject to checks for preservation.
  3. Gas trapped in a layer is generally younger than the ice around it.
  4. Water isotopes are calibrated temperature indicators, not thermometers.
  5. Deep ice layers can be thin, mixed or smoothed.
  6. Dating uses multiple clocks and physical models, with uncertainty.
  7. Antarctic temperature indicators are not global mean temperature.
  8. The established ice-core greenhouse-gas record spans about 800,000 years.
  9. Beyond EPICA has extended the continuous archive to at least 1.2 million years, with detailed analysis continuing through 2026.
  10. Correlation, timing and causation are different scientific questions.

Five knowledge questions and answers

1. Why can the air in a bubble be younger than its surrounding ice?
Snow becomes firn before pores close. Air continues to exchange with the atmosphere during part of that interval. The enclosed gas therefore records a later time than the original snowfall.
2. What is measured directly when scientists report ancient CO₂?
They extract and analyse gas from sealed bubbles. They then place the measurement on a gas-age scale and check that the sample has preserved its composition.
3. Why does δ¹⁸O not provide one universal temperature formula?
Its value also depends on moisture source, transport, snowfall season and local ice-sheet conditions. Calibration and modelling are needed.
4. What new question can a 1.2-million-year continuous core help test?
It crosses the Mid-Pleistocene Transition and can help examine how greenhouse gases and climate changed as dominant ice-age cycles shifted from roughly 41,000 to 100,000 years.
5. If CO₂ and temperature rise together, what additional evidence is needed to claim cause?
Reliable relative dating, a physical mechanism, independent measurements and models that reproduce the observed changes. Correlation alone is insufficient.
