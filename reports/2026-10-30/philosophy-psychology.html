When Do Rewards Reduce the Desire to Do the Work?

Why can a prize or bonus increase immediate effort yet reduce later interest, and when do incentives support both?

Research checked 30 October 2026 · Both worked calculations are illustrative · Original publication dates below

Executive summary

Rewards can increase effort, but they can also change why a person does a task. A payment, prize or target may signal appreciation, competence or useful feedback. It may instead signal control, low trust or that an activity is only worth doing for money. Psychology calls interest in an activity for its own sake intrinsic motivation. Research on reward effects began with laboratory studies in the 1970s and later expanded into field experiments and large reviews. The evidence does not support a simple claim that rewards always help or always harm. Expected tangible rewards for doing an already enjoyable task can reduce later voluntary interest under some conditions. Positive feedback can increase it. Incentives often help with measurable output, while internal interest remains important for quality and sustained engagement. Studies also disagree about how large and general the negative effects are. This guide explains the main theories, the strongest experiments, the disagreements and the limits of each finding. Two illustrative calculations show why short-term output and long-term motivation need separate measures. The practical question is how to reward useful work without weakening choice, competence, fairness or trust.

1. The central question: when can a reward reduce effort?

Organizations often use bonuses, grades, prizes and penalties to change behavior. The usual economic logic is clear: if an action brings an extra benefit, people have more reason to do it. Yet some experiments find that an expected reward can reduce a person's later interest in an activity they already liked. Other experiments find no broad reduction, or show that rewards increase performance. The central question is which features of a reward change behavior, and through what mechanism?

We must distinguish a person's immediate response to an incentive from what they choose to do after the incentive ends. A reward can raise output while it is available, but change how people view the activity. It can also provide welcome feedback and support a sense of progress. The same payment can have different effects when it is offered as control, recognition or fair compensation.

This question concerns philosophy as well as psychology. It asks how much choice people should retain, what institutions communicate when they attach prices to actions, and when a person's reason for acting becomes part of the outcome. The evidence cannot answer every moral question, but it can show why simple incentive rules sometimes fail.

2. Define the terms: interest, incentive and control

Intrinsic motivation means doing an activity because it is interesting or satisfying in itself. Extrinsic motivation means acting for a separable result, such as pay, a grade or approval. These reasons can coexist. A person can enjoy skilled work and also need a salary. Incentive is a condition that changes the expected benefit or cost of an action.

An expected reward is promised before the action. An unexpected reward arrives afterward without prior promise. A task-contingent reward depends on participation or completion. A performance-contingent reward depends on meeting a standard. Free-choice behavior is what people do when no reward or requirement is present. Researchers use it as one indicator of continued interest, although it is not the only measure.

Crowding out means that an external incentive reduces an internal or social reason to act. Crowding in means the external action supports such a reason. Autonomy is the experience of having meaningful choice. Competence is the experience of being capable and improving. These concepts help explain why the same reward amount may have different effects depending on how it is presented.

3. From reinforcement to the motivation debate

Earlier behavior research often studied how rewards and punishments change observable actions. If an action is followed by a desirable outcome, people may repeat it. This remains a useful principle. It does not, by itself, explain how a reward changes the meaning of an activity or how people act after the reward disappears.

Edward Deci's 1971 experiments examined how externally mediated rewards affected interest in tasks [1]. In 1973, Lepper, Greene and Nisbett studied children who already enjoyed drawing. Those who expected an award later showed less interest in the activity than children in the comparison conditions [2]. These findings helped develop the overjustification idea: a person may begin to explain their own action mainly in terms of the promised reward.

The debate then moved beyond individual studies. Cameron and Pierce's 1994 meta-analysis found little overall evidence of harm, apart from certain expected tangible rewards [3]. Deci, Koestner and Ryan's 1999 review of 128 studies reported negative effects for several reward types, especially on later free-choice behavior [4]. The different conclusions made reward design, outcome choice and statistical method central questions rather than minor details.

4. Step one: a reward changes the reason people notice

People do not respond only to the amount of a reward. They also interpret why it was offered. If a manager says that a bonus proves the team has done skilled work, the payment may communicate recognition. If the manager says that the same bonus is needed because workers cannot be trusted, the payment may communicate control. The money is similar, but the social meaning differs.

Self-determination theory distinguishes rewards that support competence and autonomy from those experienced as controlling. The theory predicts that a controlling reward can shift attention away from enjoyment and toward compliance. When the reward ends, the person may have less reason to continue. Informative feedback, by contrast, can strengthen a sense of competence and sustain interest.

There is a related self-perception account. People infer their motives partly from observing their own behavior and its setting. If they see themselves doing an activity only when a prize is offered, they may conclude that the activity was not worth doing on its own. These accounts overlap but are not identical. Experiments must vary the reward's timing, expectation and wording to identify which explanation fits best.

5. Step two: separate the immediate effect from the later effect

Suppose researchers observe a task during three periods. Before any incentive, they measure voluntary participation. During the incentive period, they measure performance while a reward is available. Afterward, they remove the reward and measure free-choice activity again. This sequence distinguishes a short-term increase in effort from a later change in interest.

The distinction matters because the two effects can move in opposite directions. A reward can make people complete more tasks today and spend less voluntary time on the activity tomorrow. Alternatively, a well-designed reward may improve performance and leave interest unchanged. A study that measures only the paid period cannot settle the question of long-term motivation.

Researchers must also ask whether participants had a genuine choice after the reward ended. A person may stop because they are tired, busy or satisfied with their progress, not because their interest fell. Self-reported enjoyment provides another measure, but it can be affected by demand effects and social expectations. Strong evidence uses several measures and compares randomized groups under similar conditions.

6. Step three: distinguish expected prizes from unexpected thanks

The early drawing study compared an expected award with an unexpected award and a no-award condition. The researchers selected children who had already shown interest in drawing. The important result concerned what the children did later, when no award was promised [2]. This design made the reward's expectation part of the causal test.

Expected tangible rewards can turn a voluntary activity into a task performed to obtain something else. An unexpected thank-you after completion is less likely to guide the original decision. That does not mean every surprise gift is harmless or every promised prize is damaging. The size of the effect depends on the task, participants, reward rules and outcome measured.

The distinction also explains why research about enjoyable activities cannot be applied without care to unpleasant or necessary work. If a task has little initial intrinsic appeal, a reward may create participation that would otherwise be absent. In such cases, the relevant comparison includes the benefit of getting the task done. A good study therefore records baseline interest instead of assuming that all participants begin with the same motivation.

7. Step four: treat feedback as information, not only payment

Feedback can tell a person that they are improving. It can also signal that their work is being judged or controlled. This dual role helps explain why verbal praise and tangible payment do not always produce the same result. In their 1999 meta-analysis, Deci, Koestner and Ryan reported that positive feedback increased free-choice behavior and self-reported interest on average, while several expected tangible reward conditions reduced them [4].

That result does not imply that praise always helps. Empty praise, constant ranking or feedback used to threaten a person can feel controlling. Useful feedback identifies what was done well, what needs work and how the person can improve. It gives information without making the entire activity depend on approval.

The same distinction applies to formal incentives. A performance bonus may recognize a clear achievement and help a person understand their progress. It may also make a narrow target more important than the quality of the whole job. A careful design gives people a clear standard, room to use judgment and feedback on aspects of performance that the target does not capture.

8. What the large reviews actually found

Meta-analysis combines results from many studies, but its conclusion depends on which studies and outcomes it includes. Cameron and Pierce reviewed 96 between-group experimental studies in 1994. They reported that rewards did not reduce intrinsic motivation overall, with a small negative pattern for expected tangible rewards given simply for doing a task [3].

Deci, Koestner and Ryan reviewed 128 studies in 1999. Their analysis found negative average effects on free-choice interest for engagement-contingent, completion-contingent and performance-contingent rewards. They also found positive average effects from verbal feedback [4]. The two reviews differ in inclusion rules, categories, measures and statistical choices. Their disagreement is part of the evidence, not an error to hide.

A later 40-year meta-analysis by Cerasoli, Nicklin and Ford examined intrinsic motivation, incentives and performance. It found that intrinsic motivation was especially relevant to quality, while incentives were more predictive of quantity. The authors concluded that the two sources of motivation can operate together [5]. These findings support a conditional view: outcome type and incentive design matter more than a general verdict on rewards.

9. Step five: add the economic view of incentives

Economic models often start with a direct effect: an extra payment raises the benefit of effort. This effect is real in many settings. But an incentive can also send information about the task, the person offering it or the worker's ability. Bénabou and Tirole developed a formal model in which a manager's choice of reward changes what the worker believes about the task or their own capacity [8].

Their approach helps connect psychological findings with rational choice. A person may reasonably infer that a large bonus means a task is unpleasant, risky or difficult. They may also infer that a manager does not trust them to act without control. The payment can therefore have two effects at once: it adds money and changes beliefs. The total effect can be positive, neutral or negative.

This is a theoretical explanation, not direct proof of every mental process. A model is useful when it makes predictions that experiments can test. In practice, managers should state why an incentive exists and avoid sending conflicting signals. Fair pay for labor is also distinct from a prize used to manipulate an activity that people previously chose freely.

10. Field evidence: pay, penalties and social meaning

Gneezy and Rustichini tested monetary incentives in experiments published in 2000. In Pay Enough or Don't Pay at All, they found that some small payments produced worse performance than no payment, while larger payments improved performance within paid conditions [6]. The result challenges the assumption that any positive payment must improve output.

In a separate field study, the same authors introduced a fine for parents who arrived late at day-care centers. Late pickups increased after the fine was introduced, and the effect did not reverse immediately when it was removed [7]. The authors argued that the fine changed how parents interpreted an informal social obligation. A penalty may become a perceived price for an action rather than a clear warning against it.

These are specific settings. The findings do not mean that all fines increase violations or that low pay is always worse than no pay. Penalty size, enforcement, prior norms and the value of time all matter. The stronger lesson is methodological: when an institution adds a financial rule, it may also change the meaning of the action being regulated.

11. When money interacts with identity and public purpose

Some activities carry a social meaning beyond private gain. People may donate, volunteer or help because they see the action as part of their identity or duty. Introducing payment can change that meaning. Mellström and Johannesson tested compensation for blood donation in a field experiment reported in 2008. Their results differed by gender: a payment reduced willingness to donate among women in their sample, while the same significant effect did not appear among men. Allowing the payment to go to charity offset the reduction among women [9].

This is evidence that social framing can matter. It is not a universal law about donors or gender. The setting, sample and payment options limit what can be inferred. A policy for another place or time requires new evidence. Researchers should also distinguish the number of people who agree to help from the quantity and quality of the help actually delivered.

The philosophical issue is whether every valuable action should be described as a market transaction. Some people welcome compensation as respect for their time. Others may view the same payment as changing the character of a civic duty. Institutions should consider both access and meaning rather than assuming one interpretation for everyone.

13. Worked example one: an immediate gain and a later loss

Illustrative data; not a result from a published study. Suppose two similar groups spend 30 minutes on a voluntary task during a free-choice period before any reward. One group later receives a promised prize for doing the task. After the prize ends, that group spends 20 minutes on the task, while a comparison group spends 30 minutes.

The rewarded group's later free-choice time is 10 minutes lower, or (30 − 20) ÷ 30 = 33.3% below the comparison group's time. This is the kind of difference a researcher might study. It is not evidence that the prize caused a 33.3% loss in real life. A valid experiment would randomly assign groups, record baseline interest and estimate uncertainty around the difference.

The calculation also shows what is missing. We do not know how much work each group completed while the prize was available. We do not know whether the groups produced work of equal quality or whether the change lasted. A useful report would show all these outcomes and avoid turning a short-term observation into a claim about a permanent loss of motivation.

14. Worked example two: quantity can rise while quality falls

Illustrative data; not a result from a published study. A team completes 100 units of work without a piece-rate bonus. Suppose 4% have defects, leaving 96 acceptable units. With a bonus tied only to unit count, the team completes 130 units, but 12% have defects. The number of acceptable units is then 130 × 0.88 = 114.4, or about 114.

Output rises, and acceptable output also rises in this example. Yet defects increase sharply, from 4 to 15.6 expected defective units. Whether the bonus is worthwhile depends on the cost of repair, customer harm, worker strain and future behavior. The calculation does not prove that bonuses cause defects. It illustrates why a quantity target can miss important costs.

The 2014 performance meta-analysis found that incentives and intrinsic motivation can predict different aspects of work [5]. For a real decision, measure quality, output, retention and the effects after the incentive changes. A system that improves one target while damaging an unmeasured goal should not be called successful without a fuller account.

15. Evidence limits: measurement, context and causation

Laboratory tasks often give researchers tight control over rewards and timing. That makes causal tests easier. But short tasks with students or children may not reflect long-term paid employment. Field studies improve realism but introduce other limits, such as local norms, policy differences and smaller control over outside influences. A strong interpretation uses both types of evidence.

Meta-analyses are also sensitive to definitions. A study may measure free-choice time, stated interest, task completion or performance. These outcomes are related but not identical. A negative effect on voluntary time does not automatically mean lower work quality. A positive effect on output does not establish stronger internal interest. Study selection and statistical treatment can change the pooled estimate.

Finally, a theory of autonomy or social signaling is not proof of what each participant felt. Researchers need manipulations and measures that distinguish competing mechanisms. The 1994 and 1999 reviews show why careful classification matters [3][4]. The most defensible conclusion is narrower than either slogan: rewards sometimes reduce later interest, often increase measured effort, and work differently across tasks and people.

16. Competing views: reinforcement, autonomy and signaling

The reinforcement view emphasizes that people respond to consequences. Its strength is practical: incentives can make a difficult or dull action more likely, especially when the reward is clear and meaningful. It is less complete when a payment changes how the person understands the task. A policy can work during the paid period and fail after it ends.

The autonomy view emphasizes the need to feel choice and competence. Its strength is explaining why controlling rewards can reduce later interest and why constructive feedback can help. Its limit is that autonomy is difficult to measure consistently and is not the only reason people act. Some participants value predictable pay more than an abstract sense of freedom.

The signaling view emphasizes what incentives reveal about institutions and tasks. It helps explain why a fine can change a norm and why a bonus can imply distrust or difficulty. Its limit is that people interpret signals differently. No theory explains every study. A fair synthesis keeps the direct material benefit, the experience of choice and the social meaning in the same analysis.

17. Wider effects: schools, workplaces and public policy

In schools, prizes can help students start a task, but educators also care about curiosity after the prize disappears. They need to measure later voluntary learning, not only completed assignments. Feedback that describes progress may support competence without making every lesson a contest. Different students will respond differently, so one reward rule may not serve everyone.

In workplaces, fair pay is a basic condition for a legitimate agreement. It should not be confused with an experimental prize for an enjoyable task. Employers should pay people fairly and then design bonuses that do not make a narrow measure more important than the full job. A target that ignores quality, teamwork or safety can distort decisions even when it increases the measured output.

In public policy, fines and subsidies can alter norms as well as prices. Policymakers should test how people interpret a new rule and what happens after the rule changes. A 2011 review by Gneezy, Meier and Rey-Biel surveys these context-dependent effects across education, public goods and behavior change [10]. The wider principle is to evaluate behavior, meaning and distributional effects together.

18. How to design and test a reward system

Start by defining the behavior and the reason it matters. Decide whether the main goal is participation, quantity, quality, learning or sustained voluntary effort. Do not use a single measure for all these outcomes. Record baseline interest before introducing a reward, especially if the activity is already enjoyable.

Next, choose whether the incentive is expected, unexpected, task-based or performance-based. State its purpose clearly. Use feedback that describes real progress. Leave room for judgment where possible. Ensure that the reward does not replace fair compensation or punish people for factors they cannot control.

Then test the design against a comparison group when feasible. Measure immediate output, error rates, self-reported interest and free-choice behavior after the reward ends. Inspect whether effects differ across groups and whether they last. If the incentive raises a narrow target but harms an important unmeasured outcome, revise it. If the evidence is mixed, report that uncertainty. Reward design is an empirical question and an institutional choice, not a fixed rule that applies to every person.

Nine lessons to keep

  1. Do not confuse fair compensation with a prize used to control behavior.
  2. Measure interest after an incentive ends, as well as performance during it.
  3. Expected tangible rewards can affect later voluntary interest in already enjoyable tasks.
  4. Feedback that supports competence can work differently from controlling rewards.
  5. Separate output quantity from quality, safety and sustained learning.
  6. Check how a payment or fine changes the social meaning of an action.
  7. Use results from both field studies and controlled experiments, with their limits stated.
  8. Test reward designs for the actual people, task and time horizon.
  9. Treat autonomy, competence and fair treatment as design conditions, not slogans.

These lessons are conditional. A reward may be helpful in one setting and harmful in another. The outcome to protect must be specified before the incentive is introduced.

Knowledge check: five questions and answers

Are all rewards bad for intrinsic motivation?

No. Studies show different effects by reward type, expectation, task and outcome. Positive feedback can support interest, and incentives can improve output.

What is the difference between performance during a reward and later free-choice behavior?

The first measures behavior while an incentive is active. The second measures voluntary activity after it ends. The two outcomes can change in different directions.

Why did the major meta-analyses disagree?

They used different study sets, reward categories, outcomes and statistical decisions. Both found that context and reward design matter, even though their overall conclusions differed.

What does the day-care fine study establish?

In that setting, introducing a fine increased late pickups. It suggests a financial rule can change the meaning of a social obligation. It does not show that every fine will have this effect.

How should a team evaluate a bonus?

Compare similar groups or periods. Measure quantity, quality, safety, interest and behavior after the bonus ends. Include fair pay and differences among workers in the decision.
