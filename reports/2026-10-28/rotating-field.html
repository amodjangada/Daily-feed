Why Can We Warn About an Earthquake but Not Predict It?

Why fault rupture is hard to forecast and how a warning can still arrive before damaging waves.

Executive summary

Earthquake science can identify dangerous faults, estimate the chance of future shaking, and sometimes warn people seconds before strong motion reaches them. Yet scientists cannot reliably state that a particular earthquake will begin at a precise time and place tomorrow. The apparent contradiction disappears when we separate four tasks. Prediction specifies an earthquake before it starts. Long-term hazard assessment estimates the likelihood of shaking over years or decades. Aftershock forecasting estimates probabilities after an earthquake. Early warning detects an earthquake that has already started and sends an alert ahead of some of its waves. The USGS makes these distinctions explicitly. [1]

Faults are difficult to predict because they are complex underground systems. Friction, rock structure, fluids, stress and interactions between fault segments are only partly measured. Proposed warning signs sometimes appear before large earthquakes, but often appear without them, and many earthquakes have no clear precursor. In contrast, the first seismic waves can be detected after rupture begins. Computers and communication networks can transmit alerts faster than later damaging waves travel through rock. People far enough away may gain useful seconds; those close to the rupture may get no advance warning. [2] [3]

This guide explains the physical mechanisms, the failed but useful Parkfield prediction experiment, early-warning networks, long-term hazard maps and aftershock models. It compares the real trade-offs in alert design and research investment. Two worked calculations are clearly illustrative, not forecasts for any location. The central conclusion is practical: reliable protection depends on building quality, clear public guidance and tested alert systems even when precise prediction is unavailable.

1. Four questions that sound alike

A structural engineer wants to know how much shaking a hospital must survive over its life. A scientist wants to know whether a specific fault will break next Tuesday. An emergency manager wants the chance of damaging aftershocks during the coming week. A train operator wants to know whether the train can slow before strong shaking reaches the track. These people ask about the same physical hazard, but they require different kinds of evidence.

A prediction must identify time, location and magnitude before the event. A long-term probability is a chance over a defined period. An aftershock forecast uses the fact that an earthquake has occurred and that further events are more likely for a time. An early-warning system detects the event already under way. A public message should name which type of information it contains. Otherwise a statement about a 30-year probability can sound like a countdown, or a phone alert can appear to prove that scientists knew the future. [1]

These categories also have different tests. A specific prediction can be checked against a fixed time and place. A probability model must be checked over many comparable forecasts. An early-warning system must be assessed for detection accuracy, delivery delay, shaking estimates and useful action. The same single event cannot test all three methods fairly.

2. A history of hopes and failed clocks

People have long watched for unusual animal behavior, changes in water, gases or small tremors before earthquakes. These observations can suggest physical hypotheses. They become reliable predictors only when researchers establish how often the signs occur before an event and how often they occur without one. The second number matters: a signal that appears every week is not a useful warning of a rare earthquake. The USGS reports that unusual animal behavior has not provided a consistent predictive method. [4]

Parkfield, California, became a major test of earthquake prediction. Several historical earthquakes along a section of the San Andreas fault appeared to follow a fairly regular pattern. Researchers supported a high-confidence forecast in the 1980s that a moderate event would occur before 1993. The predicted event did not arrive within that window. A magnitude-6 earthquake occurred in 2004. The result exposed weaknesses in the assumed regular cycle and in the selection of historical observations. It also showed why a prediction must be judged by the original rules rather than revised after the outcome. [5] [6]

Parkfield still produced scientific value. Instruments installed to observe the fault recorded motion and improved knowledge of earthquake processes. The lesson is that a failed prediction experiment can produce good data while the prediction itself remains unsuccessful. Researchers can learn from a failed model without treating its original claim as correct.

Attention also moved toward a different technology: rapid detection after an event begins. Japan began a public earthquake early-warning service through broadcast media on 1 October 2007. The USGS later developed the ShakeAlert network for the western United States. These systems exploit known differences in wave and communication speeds. They do not require a reliable signal before rupture. [7] [2]

3. The physics of a fault

Earth's outer rigid shell is divided into tectonic plates that move slowly. Some boundaries contain faults: fractures where rock can slide. Friction can hold parts of a fault in place while surrounding rock continues to deform. Stress builds. At some point, a patch begins to slip and may trigger more slip nearby. The sudden release of stored elastic energy creates an earthquake.

The hypocenter is where rupture begins underground. The epicenter is the point at the surface directly above it. Large ruptures can extend far beyond their starting point. This is why distance from the epicenter alone cannot fully explain shaking. The direction of rupture, depth, local soil and rock, and the structure of buildings also matter.

Seismic energy travels in several kinds of waves. P-waves arrive first and move material mainly by compression. S-waves travel more slowly and shear material sideways. Surface waves can also produce damaging motion. The USGS gives representative speeds of about 3.7 miles per second for P-waves and 2.5 miles per second for S-waves in its early-warning overview. Actual speeds depend on rock and depth. [2]

Magnitude measures the size of the earthquake source. Intensity describes the shaking experienced at a specific place. A large earthquake can produce different intensities in different towns. Magnitude is logarithmic: an increase of one magnitude unit corresponds to about 32 times the released seismic energy under a standard approximation. This does not mean that every building experiences 32 times the shaking. [8]

Earthquake hazard describes potential physical effects. Exposure describes the people and assets in harm's way. Vulnerability describes how badly those exposed elements may be damaged. Risk depends on all three. Good building design can reduce losses even where the geological hazard cannot be changed.

4. Why precise prediction is so difficult

The first difficulty is that the important physical conditions are largely hidden. Scientists can map faults, measure surface movement with satellite methods, record small earthquakes and study rock friction in laboratories. They cannot directly measure every stress, fluid pressure or friction condition along a deep fault. The relevant changes may occur on scales smaller than a sensor network can resolve.

The second difficulty is that rupture develops through time. A small initial slip may stop, or it may grow into a major event. Even after an earthquake has begun, the first seconds of a large rupture can resemble those of a smaller event. A warning system that waits for certainty may lose valuable time; one that acts on limited evidence may send unnecessary alerts. The USGS describes this problem when examining warning thresholds. [9]

The third difficulty is the lack of a reliable precursor. A small earthquake can be called a foreshock only after a larger one occurs. Many small events are never followed by a larger event. Groundwater changes, gas readings and animal behavior can have causes unrelated to fault failure. A predictor must work prospectively, on events it has not already seen, and must report missed events and false alarms. Selecting only apparent successes creates a misleading record.

The fourth difficulty is interaction. One rupture changes stress nearby, and the next event depends on fault geometry and material properties. The rates of aftershocks can be estimated statistically, but their exact times remain uncertain. A 2024 review found that established statistical models remain strong baselines for aftershock forecasts. New physical and machine-learning methods have promise, but have not consistently displaced them. [10]

Finally, an apparently accurate statement may be too broad to be useful. Predicting that a large earthquake will occur somewhere in a vast region over many decades is different from predicting an event at a specific place tomorrow. The latter needs much stronger evidence and a clearly defined test.

5. How early warning actually works

First, a fault ruptures. Nearby seismometers detect the first waves. Several instruments help algorithms distinguish an earthquake from noise and estimate the developing event's location, size and expected shaking. A processing system sends a message through communication networks. Phones, broadcasters or automatic controls receive the alert. Because electronic messages travel much faster than seismic waves, the warning may reach some locations before the strongest shaking. [2]

The word early describes the arrival of shaking at the recipient, not the start of the earthquake. People close to the source may feel strong motion before sensors and networks can deliver a message. This is the blind zone. Detection time, sensor spacing, communication delay and human response time all affect its size. A USGS analysis warns that places with the strongest shaking can have the least advance notice. [11]

Japan's meteorological agency says warning time may range from a few seconds to a few tens of seconds. It also identifies limits: noise can cause false alerts, magnitude estimates can change, and several nearby earthquakes can confuse analysis. These are engineering limits that must be measured, not reasons to describe the system as a prediction service. [3]

Seconds can still be useful. People may move away from glass, drop, cover and hold on. Automated controls can slow trains, protect equipment or perform other actions for which they were designed. The action must be safe, fast and understood before an event occurs. An alert is valuable only when it reaches its intended recipient in time to reduce harm.

7. Worked example: how much warning time?

Assume a fictional earthquake starts 100 kilometres from a town. For this calculation, let a first detectable wave travel at 6 kilometres per second and a later strong wave at 3.5 kilometres per second. Assume detection, processing and message delivery take eight seconds after the rupture begins. These numbers are illustrative assumptions, not measurements from an actual earthquake.

First-wave arrival: 100 ÷ 6 = 16.7 seconds
Strong-wave arrival: 100 ÷ 3.5 = 28.6 seconds
Illustrative lead time: 28.6 − 8 = 20.6 seconds

The alert might provide about 21 seconds of lead time under this simplified model. But the estimate ignores complex wave paths, a long rupture, sensor geometry and changing magnitude estimates. A real system may deliver a later message or no message if the alert threshold is not reached.

At a site only 15 kilometres away, the assumed strong wave arrives in about 4.3 seconds. The same eight-second processing and delivery delay is too slow. This explains why adding a faster app cannot eliminate the physical blind zone. The full system must be evaluated from first detection through protective action. [11]

8. Long-term hazards and aftershock forecasts

Long-term hazard maps estimate the level of ground motion that may be exceeded with a stated probability over a defined period. Researchers combine known faults, past earthquakes, geological movement, possible magnitudes, distances and the effect of local ground conditions. Engineers use these estimates in structural design and infrastructure planning. A map does not identify the next earthquake's date. [12]

Aftershock forecasts begin after a mainshock. Further earthquakes are then more likely for a period, and their rate usually falls over time. Statistical models use patterns from past sequences and measurements from the current sequence to estimate the chance of further events over a day, week, month or year. The USGS describes forecasts for expected smaller aftershocks and the probability of larger, potentially damaging ones. Forecasts change as new data arrive. [13]

A probability of 20% does not mean that an event must happen or cannot happen. A well-calibrated model giving 20% probabilities should see the event in about one-fifth of many comparable situations. One event cannot prove that a probability forecast was wrong. Scientists test performance across many forecasts and compare with simpler baseline models.

Historical records are incomplete, especially for rare large events. Faults also interact, and local soil can change shaking. Good hazard studies therefore state their assumptions and uncertainty. The public decision may depend as much on the consequences of a failure as on the estimated probability.

9. Worked example: interpreting a 50-year probability

Imagine a fictional location with a 10% chance of exceeding a specified shaking threshold at least once during the next 50 years. This is not a current estimate for any Indian city. Under the simplified assumption of a constant independent annual hazard, the annual probability can be derived from the chance of no exceedance over 50 years.

Annual probability = 1 − (1 − 0.10)1/50
≈ 0.002105 = 0.2105% per year
Equivalent annual rate ≈ −ln(0.90) ÷ 50 = 0.002107

The result is roughly two-tenths of one percent per year. Its inverse is about 475 years, often called a return period. This is an average rate implied by a model, not a countdown. An event can happen tomorrow or much later. Also, a 10% chance of a shaking threshold is different from a 10% chance of an earthquake of one specific magnitude. Several faults and ground conditions may contribute. The USGS explains this distinction in its technical guidance. [14]

Designers must then decide how to use the estimate. A hospital may need to function after rare strong shaking; another structure may have a different performance target. Engineering standards and the consequences of failure guide the choice.

10. What research supports

Early-warning systems can be evaluated against observed and simulated earthquakes. Researchers measure whether sensors detect the event, whether the system estimates shaking accurately, how fast alerts reach users, and whether the message arrives before damaging motion. A 2025 study of ShakeAlert version 3 used offline tests to examine expected warning times during large earthquakes. It found useful warning times possible at many sites, while showing that alert thresholds and shaking levels strongly affect performance. Offline tests do not guarantee identical live performance for every user. [15]

Aftershock forecasts also have a substantial evidence base. A 2024 review found that statistical models built on observed earthquake sequences remain competitive. Physics-based and machine-learning approaches are being developed, but they must prove their value in independent testing. A model that fits historical data can still fail in a new region. [10]

Exact short-term prediction remains unsupported as a reliable public service. The Parkfield experiment exposed the limits of regular recurrence models. The USGS and British Geological Survey both state that reliable forecasts of an exact future earthquake's time, location and magnitude are not available. This does not rule out future discoveries; it describes the present evidence. [1] [16]

11. India: monitoring, buildings and warnings

India faces different earthquake hazards across the Himalayas, the northeast, the Andaman–Nicobar region and other areas. Ground conditions and building quality affect the consequences. The National Centre for Seismology, under the Ministry of Earth Sciences, is the national agency responsible for monitoring earthquake activity. Its official site describes a network with more than 170 stations and continuous monitoring. Station counts and services can change; the official site is the source for current information. [17]

Monitoring and publishing earthquake locations does not itself prove that a public early-warning service can deliver advance notice to every phone. That requires dense sensors, tested algorithms, fast networks, accessible messages and clear action rules. A notification that arrives after shaking is different from an alert that arrives before it. This article makes no claim that any Indian city has a guaranteed number of warning seconds.

Long-term safety depends heavily on earthquake-resistant design, local soil investigation, building-code compliance and structural assessment. A historical Indian probabilistic hazard study illustrates how earthquake catalogues and ground-motion models can estimate regional risk. Historical maps should not replace current standards or site-specific engineering. [18]

Tsunami warnings are a separate system. A large undersea earthquake may generate a tsunami, but the hazard travels differently and has its own monitoring and response process. India's ocean information service operates a tsunami early-warning system. This is not evidence that the earthquake itself was predicted. [19]

12. Real scientific and policy debates

Fast alerts or fewer false alarms?

An alert sent quickly may provide more time for action, but its shaking estimate is based on limited initial data. Waiting for more stations can improve confidence but reduce the available seconds. The best choice depends on the cost of false alerts, the risk of missing a damaging event and the recipient. A train-control system may use a different threshold from a phone notification. The USGS has studied this trade-off directly. [9]

More prediction research or more resilience?

Fault physics and possible precursors remain legitimate research subjects. Scientists should test new signals rather than assume that prediction is impossible forever. However, a speculative method must show repeatable results before public authorities rely on it. Strong buildings, tested alerts and clear public education already have a more direct route to reducing harm. Funding choices should consider evidence, expected benefit and the cost of delayed safety improvements.

Can artificial intelligence solve the problem?

Machine learning can find small earthquakes in noisy data and help improve monitoring. It may also assist aftershock models. But it can learn local patterns that do not hold elsewhere, or accidentally use information unavailable at the moment of prediction. Valid tests require future or genuinely held-out events, transparent false-alarm and missed-event rates, and comparison with established statistical methods. The 2024 aftershock review describes promise without claiming reliable exact prediction. [10]

Who receives protection?

Warning coverage is not equal. Poor mobile networks, older devices, language barriers and accessibility needs can prevent people from receiving or understanding an alert. Public systems need multiple delivery channels and independent evaluation. The goal is reduced injury and disruption across the population, not simply a large count of notifications.

13. Uncertainty and open questions

Researchers still do not know whether any fault has a consistent, measurable precursor that could support precise prediction. They also cannot fully model every interaction among stress, fluids, friction and fault structure. For early warning, hard problems include the first seconds of a large rupture, multiple nearby events, sensor failures, communication delays and human response. Japan's meteorological agency lists several of these limits. [3]

Public language matters. The terms likely, possible and imminent can mean different things to different readers. An official forecast should state its location, event threshold, time window, probability and update date. An alert should make the action clear without claiming false precision about the seconds remaining.

New equipment also requires maintenance, reliable power and independent tests. Better sensors do not guarantee better public outcomes unless messages reach people in time and lead to safe action. Evaluation must cover the whole chain from measurement to response.

14. Practical consequences

Individuals do not need a short-term prediction to prepare. Know safe actions during shaking, secure heavy objects and follow current local emergency guidance. If an official warning arrives, take the protective action immediately. If shaking starts before an alert, act without waiting for the phone.

Building owners should focus on structural safety and the condition of the actual site. A hazard map is an input to engineering, not a substitute for qualified assessment. Retrofitting weak structures and protecting critical equipment can reduce losses under many possible scenarios.

Public agencies should test the full warning process and publish results for latency, coverage, false alerts, missed alerts and response. Aftershock probabilities should be updated and communicated with clear limits. When evidence is weak, the correct response is to state the uncertainty rather than imply a level of precision the data cannot support.

15. Ten lessons

  1. Prediction, hazard assessment, aftershock forecasting and early warning are different tools.
  2. Early warning detects an earthquake after rupture starts.
  3. Fast P-waves and faster electronic communication create possible warning time.
  4. People near the source may be in a blind zone.
  5. Magnitude describes the source; local intensity describes shaking at a place.
  6. Hidden fault conditions and unreliable precursors limit exact prediction.
  7. Parkfield shows why a pattern in past events is not a dependable clock.
  8. Return periods are statistical averages, not scheduled dates.
  9. Aftershock forecasts are useful probabilities that must be updated.
  10. Strong buildings, tested alerts and clear action rules reduce risk now.

16. Five knowledge questions and answers

1. A phone alerts you ten seconds before strong shaking. Was the earthquake predicted?
No. The earthquake may already have started near the fault. The system detected early waves and transmitted a message ahead of later waves at your location.
2. Why may a person close to the epicenter get no warning?
Strong waves may arrive before sensors detect the event, computers process the data and communication networks deliver the message. This is the blind zone.
3. Does a 475-year return period mean the next earthquake is 475 years away?
No. It represents an average annual rate under a model. Events are not scheduled at regular intervals, and the model contains uncertainty.
4. What did scientists learn from Parkfield?
The prediction's time window failed, showing the limits of a simple recurrence model. The monitoring experiment still provided valuable observations about fault motion and earthquakes.
5. What must a proposed AI predictor demonstrate?
It must define time, location and magnitude targets in advance; beat simple baselines in independent future tests; and report both false alarms and missed events. Fitting past data is not enough.
