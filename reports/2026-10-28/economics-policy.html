Executive summary

A public project can cost money today and produce benefits for many years. To compare these flows, economists use discounting. A discount rate converts a future benefit or cost into its value today. The choice matters greatly for rail systems, flood protection, health, and climate policy because their effects can last for decades. Yet there is no single rate that answers every question. A government must decide whose welfare counts, whether the numbers include inflation, how risk is treated, and how the policy affects people with different incomes. The United Kingdom’s 2026 Green Book uses a real social time preference rate of 3.5% for years one to thirty, with lower rates for later years. United States regulatory guidance has changed: a 2023 revision was revoked in February 2025, restoring the 2003 guidance. These differences reflect both evidence and policy judgments. This guide explains the arithmetic, the ethical choices, and the limits of the method. It shows why a careful decision needs transparent assumptions, sensitivity tests, and attention to effects that money cannot measure.

Central question: what should count as value?

Central question: When a government spends resources now to create benefits many years later, how should it compare those benefits with immediate needs?

The practical question is not whether the future matters. It is how to make a decision with scarce resources, uncertain forecasts, and unequal effects across people. A bridge, an early-childhood programme, a vaccine, and a flood barrier have different timing and different risks. A simple rule that treats all future benefits as if they arrive today will usually overstate the value of very distant payments. A rule that discounts them too sharply can undervalue protection for future people.

Good appraisal starts with a clear public goal. It then compares credible options, including doing nothing new. The analyst records the expected costs and benefits in each year, makes prices consistent, applies a documented discount rule, and tests what happens when assumptions change. The result is evidence for a decision. It is not an automatic order to approve or reject a project.

1. History: from interest rates to public welfare

Merchants and lenders have long understood that receiving money today differs from receiving it later. Money available now can finance production or earn a return. Early finance used this idea to value loans and investments. Public economics then asked a harder question: what is the value of future consumption, health, safety, or environmental quality for society as a whole?

In the twentieth century, economists formalised intertemporal choice: choices across time. Public agencies adopted cost-benefit analysis to compare projects with different lives. The United States Office of Management and Budget issued Circular A-4 in 2003, advising regulatory analysts to show results using both 3% and 7% real discount rates. The 2023 revision moved to a 2% default. In February 2025, OMB revoked that revision and restored the 2003 circular. A reader must therefore check which version applied on the date of an analysis. [2] [3]

The UK followed a social-time-preference approach in its Green Book. Its February 2026 edition retains a 3.5% real rate for years one to thirty, then 3.0% for years thirty-one to seventy-five, and 2.5% thereafter. The rules also treat health and life effects differently. These are published appraisal conventions, not natural constants. [1]

2. Essential terms, with careful boundaries

Present value (PV) is the value assigned today to a future amount. Discount rate is the rate used to make that conversion. Real values remove general price inflation; nominal values include it. A real discount rate must be used with real cash flows. Mixing the two creates an error.

Net present social value (NPSV) is the present value of social benefits minus the present value of social costs. Social means the analysis includes effects on people beyond the organisation paying the bill. Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative use of a resource. Counterfactual is the credible outcome without the intervention.

Social time preference describes how society values consumption at different dates. Opportunity cost of capital considers the return from investments that public action may displace. Distributional weights give different importance to gains and losses depending on who receives them. Risk means outcomes can differ from forecasts; uncertainty also includes gaps in our knowledge of the probabilities themselves.

These terms answer different questions. An investment's financing interest rate, its internal rate of return, the inflation rate, and a social discount rate must not be treated as interchangeable.

3. The mechanism, step by step

Step 1 — Define the service and the baseline

Specify what changes if the government acts. A flood barrier might reduce expected damage to homes and roads. Its benefits are avoided losses relative to a no-barrier baseline, not the total value of the protected property. Include construction, maintenance, and eventual replacement or removal.

Step 2 — Put effects in the correct years

Record each cost and benefit when it occurs. Construction often has high early costs. A transport project may produce travel-time savings over decades. Some effects may start late because delivery is delayed. A realistic timetable can change the result as much as the chosen rate.

Step 3 — Make values comparable

Choose one price year and remove general inflation from nominal forecasts. Record relative price changes separately where justified. Monetise effects when sound methods exist, and describe important non-monetised effects openly. Do not assign a zero value to an effect merely because it is difficult to price.

Step 4 — Discount each year's real flow

For a benefit B in year t and a constant rate r, present value equals B ÷ (1 + r)t. The discount factor declines as time passes. For rates that change by period, multiply the appropriate annual factors instead of applying the final rate to every year.

Step 5 — Add, compare, and test

Sum discounted benefits and costs for each feasible option. Then check the distribution of gains, fiscal affordability, delivery risk, and unpriced outcomes. Repeat the calculation with plausible changes to demand, costs, timing, and discount rates. A result that reverses under small changes needs careful explanation.

4. Diagram: from public purpose to decision

Notice that the rate enters only after the analyst defines what is being valued. A precise discount calculation cannot correct a weak baseline, an unrealistic demand forecast, or missing environmental damage. The quality of the inputs remains the main limit.

5. Worked example one: a benefit thirty years away

Illustrative values only. Assume a programme produces a single real social benefit of ₹1,000 in year thirty. There is no other benefit or cost in this example. Using the constant-rate formula, the value today depends on the assumed real rate.

Table 1. Present value of ₹1,000 in year 30
Assumed real rateCalculationValue today, approx.
2%1,000 ÷ 1.0230₹552
3.5%1,000 ÷ 1.03530₹356
7%1,000 ÷ 1.0730₹131

The underlying future benefit is identical in all three rows. The change comes entirely from the valuation rule. The 3.5% and 7% rates here are illustrative choices for sensitivity analysis, not instructions for Indian public projects. This is why published reports must show the rate, the price year, and the time horizon. It is also why disagreement about discounting can be substantive rather than a mathematical mistake.

At a fixed rate, later benefits receive smaller weights. Yet it does not follow that all long-term projects fail. A project can produce large, repeated benefits, avoid catastrophic losses, or provide important outcomes that cannot be monetised with confidence.

6. What official guidance actually establishes

The UK Green Book, updated on 5 February 2026, treats appraisal as a structured comparison of objectives, options, costs, benefits, risks, and distributional effects. It explicitly warns against selecting projects only by benefit-cost ratios. It recommends real values and a social time preference rate of 3.5% for the first thirty years, 3.0% for years thirty-one to seventy-five, and 2.5% after that. It uses a 1.5% rate for specified health and life effects. The exact boundaries matter. [1]

The United States shows that discount guidance can change with administrations. OMB's 2003 Circular A-4 recommended presenting both 3% and 7% real-rate results. A 2023 revision adopted a different approach, including a 2% default. OMB memorandum M-25-15, dated 12 February 2025, explicitly revoked the 2023 circular and reinstated the 2003 version. An article that presents the 2023 revision as the current US rule in October 2026 would be misleading. [2] [3] [4]

These official documents establish what analysts are told to do in those jurisdictions. They do not prove that one rate is objectively correct for all societies. Nor do they supply universal estimates of the benefits of a particular road, hospital, or climate policy. Those must come from separate evidence and local evaluation.

7. Why the rate also contains an ethical choice

One argument for discounting is opportunity cost: resources used now cannot be invested elsewhere. Another is that future generations may have higher incomes, making an additional unit of consumption less valuable to them. A third is pure time preference: assigning less weight to utility simply because it occurs later. These are distinct reasons, and analysts should not hide them inside one unexplained percentage.

The classic Ramsey approach separates the pure rate of time preference from expected consumption growth and the sensitivity of welfare to consumption. It helps show why a rate can be low even when market returns are higher. However, its inputs require judgments about future growth, inequality, and how welfare changes with income. It does not remove the ethical problem.

Intergenerational fairness is central for climate adaptation and long-lived infrastructure. Future residents cannot vote on today's investment. A low rate can make their welfare more visible, but it can also direct scarce current resources away from people with urgent needs. A high rate can reflect competing investment opportunities, but may make distant harms appear unimportant. The proper response is to show the assumptions, examine alternatives, and identify who gains or loses.

8. Serious competing views: Stern, Nordhaus, and uncertainty

The debate after the 2006 Stern Review is a useful case. Stern's analysis supported strong early climate action and used a low pure rate of time preference. William Nordhaus argued in a 2007 Journal of Economic Literature article that the Review's strong policy timing depended heavily on its discounting assumptions. He favoured greater consistency with observed market returns and savings behaviour. [5]

Martin Weitzman's 2007 review also questioned parts of Stern's formal discounting argument, yet stressed that deep uncertainty and the possibility of severe climate outcomes could justify stronger protection than a simple expected-value model suggests. His position was not a blanket endorsement of either side. [6]

These positions are not a dispute over how to calculate compound interest. They differ over the right welfare function, how to interpret market evidence, and how to treat severe uncertain damage. There is also a practical tension: a single official rate improves consistency across public agencies, while a rate tailored to each risk may better represent a particular problem but reduce comparability and invite selective assumptions.

A sound analysis can report a central official case, a lower-rate case, and a higher-rate case without claiming that the arithmetic settles the ethical disagreement.

9. Worked example two: should a city build a protection system?

Illustrative project, not a real tender or forecast. A city can spend ₹100 million now on a protective system. Assume it produces real expected benefits of ₹6 million at the end of every year for thirty years. Ignore maintenance, tax effects, and other impacts solely to isolate discounting.

The present value of an equal annual flow is annual benefit × [1 − (1 + r)−30] ÷ r. At 3.5%, the thirty-year annuity factor is about 18.39. Benefits have a present value of about ₹110.3 million. Net present social value is about +₹10.3 million after the initial cost. At 7%, the factor is about 12.41. Benefits have a present value of about ₹74.5 million, so the net value is about −₹25.5 million.

The sign changes because the rate changes. But a real decision also needs estimates of the probability and severity of avoided damage, maintenance costs, alternatives such as zoning or relocation, and the effect on vulnerable households. A single positive NPSV cannot replace those checks. A negative monetised NPSV does not automatically settle the decision if material unpriced effects exist. The UK Green Book specifically calls for a balanced judgment beyond one ratio. [1]

10. Why the same project can help some people more

Suppose a new transit line saves time for higher-income commuters but displaces informal traders near a station. Summing monetary values may hide the difference between those groups. The ability to pay can influence the monetary value of time or risk in an appraisal. Distributional analysis asks which households and places receive benefits, and which bear costs.

Some appraisal systems apply weights that give greater value to gains received by people with lower incomes. Others show the distribution separately and leave the final judgment to elected officials. Both approaches require transparency. A model can make a social choice visible; it cannot decide that choice on behalf of the public.

Geography also matters. Benefits may concentrate in a city centre while construction costs, pollution, or land acquisition fall elsewhere. An analysis that covers only the budget of the agency paying for a project can miss these effects. The UK Green Book's 2025 review and 2026 guidance place explicit emphasis on place, distribution, and value for money as a balanced decision. [7] [1]

11. Risk, uncertainty, and the limits of one discount rate

Discounting and risk adjustment solve different problems. A future payment can be certain but delayed. A project can also have an uncertain payoff even next year. If analysts use a higher discount rate as a rough penalty for every uncertain project, they may count risk inconsistently. Better practice is to model major uncertain outcomes directly where data allow.

For a flood barrier, analysts can use different climate and land-use scenarios. For a hospital, they can vary expected use and health outcomes. For a road, they can vary traffic demand and construction costs. Each scenario needs a clear explanation of its source. Where probabilities are credible, an expected-value calculation may help. Where probabilities are not credible, scenario analysis and robust options may be more useful.

Long horizons also raise questions about whether a constant rate is sensible. Some public frameworks use declining rates for very distant years. The UK's 2026 schedule is one example. Declining rates give relatively more weight to distant effects than applying the initial rate forever. This does not mean that every distant benefit is equally valuable; it means the rule recognises limits in long-run assumptions. [1]

12. How to use discounting in a real policy review

Start with the problem and the public objective. Demand an explicit baseline and at least one serious alternative. Ask for the appraisal horizon and why it matches the useful life of the intervention. Check whether all money values are expressed in the same price year. Confirm that the discount rate is real if the flows are real.

Next, separate the fiscal budget from social value. A project may impose costs on a ministry while reducing private health costs or travel time. A project can also be affordable this year but expensive to operate later. Ask for a yearly cash-flow table, not just one headline total.

Then inspect the treatment of risk and optimism bias. Are delays, cost overruns, demand uncertainty, and operating failures included? Does the analysis test a later start date? What happens if the main benefit is smaller than expected? Identify a switching value: the assumption that would reverse the preferred option.

Finally, inspect who receives the benefits. Ask whether local effects, vulnerable groups, environmental costs, and non-monetised outcomes are shown. After approval, establish a plan to compare actual outcomes with the forecast. The 2026 Green Book treats monitoring and evaluation as part of responsible public spending, not an optional final note. [1]

13. Evidence limits and transfer to India

The official rates discussed here come from the United Kingdom and United States. They should not be copied into Indian project appraisals without checking the relevant Indian ministry, scheme, financing conditions, and appraisal guidance. Exchange rates, real interest rates, income growth, public priorities, and project risk differ across countries and over time.

Even within one country, discounting cannot repair weak estimates of health benefits, land values, environmental damage, or behavioural change. A calculation can be exact while its inputs remain highly uncertain. Nor is the social discount rate a substitute for deciding whether a project meets legal duties or basic rights.

The Stern debate is important, but it does not provide a universal number for all climate or infrastructure projects. The UK Green Book is a current official framework; it is not proof that its assumptions are correct in every setting. The US policy history shows that official methods can change. Readers should verify the version and date of any policy document before using it.

14. Wider effects: public trust, finance, and future generations

Discount rates influence which projects pass a cost-benefit test. A higher rate generally favours projects with earlier benefits. A lower rate gives more weight to long-lived benefits and harms. This can affect decisions about preventive health, coastal protection, water networks, environmental cleanup, and research whose value appears later.

The rate can also shape the incentives of project sponsors. If only short-term outputs receive strong weight, agencies may favour visible projects with rapid returns over maintenance or prevention. If distant benefits are given too much weight without scrutiny, sponsors may justify costly projects using uncertain forecasts. Neither problem can be solved by changing the rate alone.

Public trust improves when analysts publish their assumptions, disclose uncertainty, and show how a decision changes under plausible alternatives. It also improves when the project is evaluated after delivery. The objective is to use public resources well while making the treatment of future people explicit.

15. Eight lessons to retain

  1. Discounting converts future costs and benefits into present values; it does not measure the moral worth of future people.
  2. Use real rates with real flows and nominal rates with nominal flows.
  3. Define the baseline before estimating benefits. Avoided damage is a change from that baseline.
  4. Different rates can reverse a project's measured net value, especially over long periods.
  5. The UK Green Book 2026 uses declining real social rates after year thirty; the US restored its 2003 Circular A-4 in 2025.
  6. Risk, income distribution, and unpriced effects need their own analysis.
  7. One benefit-cost ratio cannot replace a balanced public decision.
  8. Publish assumptions, test switching values, and evaluate actual outcomes after delivery.

Knowledge check: five questions and answers

1. Why must a real discount rate be paired with real values?

Because both must treat general inflation consistently. Using a real rate on nominal flows would overstate their present value relative to a consistent calculation.

2. What is the difference between a discount rate and a project financing rate?

The financing rate is the price of borrowed funds for a particular borrower. A social discount rate values future social costs and benefits. They may differ because public welfare includes effects beyond the borrower.

3. Why did the US 2023 discounting guidance not remain the current rule?

OMB memorandum M-25-15, dated 12 February 2025, revoked the November 2023 Circular A-4 and reinstated the September 2003 circular.

4. What does a positive net present social value fail to tell us?

It does not by itself show whether the project is affordable, fair, legally acceptable, robust to risk, or superior when important benefits cannot be priced.

5. Why might a lower rate matter more for a flood barrier than for a one-year service?

A flood barrier can avoid losses over many decades. Discounting compounds with time, so a change in rate has a larger effect on distant benefits than on near-term benefits.
