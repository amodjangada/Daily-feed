Executive summary

Many companies can measure fuel burned at their sites and electricity bought for their offices. Their larger climate impact may sit elsewhere: in materials, freight, suppliers, product use, and disposal. These indirect emissions are called Scope 3. Measuring them is difficult because one business depends on records from many others. The Greenhouse Gas Protocol's 2011 Scope 3 Standard provides fifteen categories for reporting these activities. It also warns that company inventories are not designed for simple comparisons across firms. A 2024 CDP and Boston Consulting Group analysis found that disclosed upstream supply-chain emissions were, on average, much larger than direct operational emissions among the companies studied. That finding does not apply equally to every sector or company. This guide explains how a firm maps its value chain, estimates emissions, improves data, and uses purchasing decisions to reduce physical emissions. It separates accounting changes from real changes, explains supplier incentives and double counting, and tests two illustrative calculations. The central lesson is that a credible inventory is useful only when it supports verified operational action.

Central question: what can a buyer actually change?

Central question: How can a company measure and reduce greenhouse gas emissions from suppliers, logistics, customers, and products when these activities occur outside its direct control?

The answer has two parts. First, accounting assigns emissions to a company's activities using consistent rules. Second, intervention changes how products are designed, made, moved, used, and recovered so that fewer gases enter the atmosphere. A company can improve its reported numbers without changing production. It can also help a supplier cut emissions before that reduction appears in the buyer's inventory.

A useful programme must identify important activities, choose suitable data, support suppliers, and test physical results. This report uses the GHG Protocol as the accounting reference. It does not assume that a reported reduction equals an independently verified reduction in global emissions.

1. Why corporate carbon accounting expanded

Early company environmental reports focused on fuel and energy at facilities the company controlled. These data were available from meters, bills, and fuel records. As supply chains spread across countries, many firms found that their direct operations represented only part of the emissions linked to their products.

In 2009, Hertwich and Peters published a trade-linked analysis of national consumption footprints. Their work showed why goods consumed in one place can cause emissions in another. It examined historical national data and should not be used as a current estimate for a particular company. [8]

In 2011, the World Resources Institute and World Business Council for Sustainable Development released the GHG Protocol Corporate Value Chain (Scope 3) Standard. It defined categories for upstream and downstream activities. Technical calculation guidance followed in 2013. The purpose was to make reporting more complete and to identify reduction opportunities, while recognising that data quality would improve over time. [1] [2]

Today, investors, customers, and target frameworks often ask firms to report more of their value chains. That pressure can support action, but it can also reward better reporting instead of better physical outcomes.

2. Terms and boundaries

Scope 1 covers direct emissions from sources a company owns or controls, such as fuel burned in a boiler. Scope 2 covers emissions from generating purchased electricity, steam, heat, or cooling consumed by the company. Scope 3 covers other indirect emissions linked to the company's value chain.

Upstream means activities related to what a company buys. Downstream means activities related to what it sells or distributes. Activity data are quantities such as tonnes of material, kilometres of freight, or units of electricity. An emission factor converts an activity into an estimated mass of greenhouse gases.

CO₂e is carbon dioxide equivalent, a common unit for combining different gases with specified warming factors. Primary data come from actual activities in a firm's value chain. Secondary data use averages, models, or proxies. Absolute emissions are total tonnes of CO₂e; intensity means emissions per unit of output or another measure.

A corporate Scope 3 inventory and a product life-cycle assessment have related purposes but different boundaries. An emissions factor, a product footprint, and a claim of avoided emissions should not be treated as the same thing.

3. The fifteen categories and why they matter

The GHG Protocol groups Scope 3 into eight upstream and seven downstream categories. Upstream activities include purchased goods and services, capital goods, fuel- and energy-related activities outside Scopes 1 and 2, upstream freight, waste, business travel, employee commuting, and upstream leased assets.

Downstream categories include downstream freight, processing of sold products, use of sold products, end-of-life treatment, downstream leased assets, franchises, and investments. The categories aim to organise a company's reporting without double counting the same activity within its Scope 3 inventory. [3]

The main categories vary by sector. Purchased materials can be large for a furniture or equipment manufacturer. Product use can matter more for a device that consumes electricity for many years. Investments can matter for financial institutions. A company should screen its activities before deciding which category deserves the most detailed data.

GHG Protocol states that its Scope 3 Standard supports comparisons of one company's emissions over time. It is not designed for simple comparisons between different companies. Product mix, boundaries, size, and methods can differ. [1]

4. Six steps from records to physical change

Step 1: Map the value chain

List major materials, suppliers, logistics, products, customer use, and disposal. Set a reporting year and company boundary. Use purchase orders, invoices, production records, and sales data to find activities. Record gaps rather than silently excluding them.

Step 2: Screen the largest sources

Estimate each relevant category with the data available. Broad spending factors can help find likely hotspots, but they do not reliably describe individual factories. Prioritise activities with large estimated emissions, poor data, and a realistic chance of reduction.

Step 3: Collect suitable data

For priority materials, obtain quantities, grades, locations, and supplier energy or product data. For smaller categories, documented secondary factors may be adequate. GHG Protocol notes that poor primary data can sometimes be less useful than high-quality secondary data. [4]

Step 4: Calculate and document

Multiply activities by appropriate factors and convert to CO₂e. Check system boundaries to avoid including the same stage twice within the inventory. Record the source, factor year, geography, and assumptions. Keep the underlying records for review.

Step 5: Change procurement and design

Use the inventory to select actions: reduce material, redesign a part, improve factory energy use, change freight operations, or extend product life where it reduces replacement demand. Agree on supplier data and commercial terms before claiming results.

Step 6: Verify and repeat

Compare physical activity and production conditions before and after each change. Recalculate the baseline when business boundaries or methods change materially. Explain whether a reported difference comes from operations, improved data, a different factor, or a change in product mix.

5. Diagram: two connected loops

Every stage has a different failure risk. Missing records produce incomplete coverage. Weak factors create false precision. Short contracts may prevent supplier investment. Higher production volume may cancel efficiency gains. A good system tests each point and reports what remains unknown.

6. What original evidence establishes

The 2011 GHG Protocol Standard defines the reporting categories and data principles. The 2013 Technical Guidance describes calculation methods. These sources support a consistent accounting process; they do not show that accounting alone reduces emissions. [1] [2]

CDP and Boston Consulting Group reported on 25 June 2024 that upstream Scope 3 emissions disclosed by firms in their analysis were, on average, twenty-six times direct operational emissions. They also reported that only 15% of disclosing corporates had a supply-chain Scope 3 target. This is evidence about a disclosure sample, with possible sector and reporting selection effects. It is not a universal multiplier for all companies. [5]

The IPCC's 2022 Working Group III assessment explains emissions embedded in international trade and consumption. It shows why the location of production can differ from the location of demand, and it discusses uncertainty in consumption-based estimates. [6]

Hertwich and Peters' peer-reviewed 2009 study estimated consumption-linked footprints across seventy-three nations and fourteen world regions. It supports the importance of indirect emissions. Its historical data and model boundaries do not verify a current company's inventory. [8]

7. Worked example one: selecting a lower-emission material

Illustrative data, not a supplier quote. A manufacturer purchases 100 tonnes of a material. Assume its current cradle-to-gate emission factor is 2.0 tonnes CO₂e per tonne. Purchased-material emissions are 100 × 2.0 = 200 tonnes CO₂e.

A qualified alternative supplier offers the same functional material with a factor of 1.2 tonnes CO₂e per tonne. If the company still needs 100 tonnes, the estimate becomes 100 × 1.2 = 120 tonnes CO₂e. The difference is 80 tonnes CO₂e, or 40% for this specific activity.

Before claiming a physical reduction, compare product performance, plant location, measurement year, energy use, recycled content rules, scrap, and transport. A factor from a different process or country may be unsuitable. GHG Protocol's technical guidance updated on 7 April 2026 identifies technology, time, geography, completeness, and reliability as key checks. [7]

This does not mean the company's total Scope 3 footprint fell by 40%. Nor does the difference between factors alone prove that global emissions fell by the same amount. Wider supply and demand effects may change the result.

8. Why data quality is often the central obstacle

A supplier may report total plant emissions while producing several materials. Assigning those emissions to a product requires an allocation rule. A retailer may know what it paid for a product but not its mass or manufacturing process. A freight operator may know distance but not vehicle load or fuel. These gaps can be large.

Primary data can reflect actual suppliers and track changes. But it can be incomplete, outdated, or hard to verify. Secondary data can cover more activities and help identify hotspots, but may hide differences between plants. The right choice depends on the decision. To compare two specific suppliers, generic spend factors are often too weak. To screen a large purchasing list, they may be useful initially. [4]

Check whether each factor matches the technology, time, geography, boundary, and reliability of the activity. The GHG Protocol's April 2026 assistance describes these criteria. [7]

Better records can increase a reported footprint because previously missed activities become visible. That should be explained as improved coverage. It is not evidence that the company's actual emissions rose by the same amount.

9. Double counting across companies

A material supplier burns fuel and reports it in Scope 1. A manufacturer buys that material and may report the related production emissions in Scope 3. A retailer farther along the chain may report emissions linked to the finished product. This overlap between different companies is expected.

The atmosphere received the physical emission once. Different corporate inventories show different relationships to the same activity. They therefore cannot simply be added to calculate global emissions. The fifteen GHG Protocol categories aim to avoid double counting within one company's Scope 3 categories, while overlaps between companies remain possible. [3]

Source-based inventories and consumption-linked inventories answer different questions. Source inventories help track physical emissions without global double counting. Value-chain inventories help buyers find the production processes they can influence. A company should state which accounting perspective it uses.

10. Worked example two: efficiency improves, total emissions rise

Illustrative values only. A company sells 1,000 units in its baseline year. Each unit causes 10 kg CO₂e in one defined product stage. The total is 10,000 kg CO₂e, or 10 tonnes.

In the next year, improved design reduces intensity to 8 kg per unit, a 20% reduction. But sales grow to 1,400 units. The total becomes 11,200 kg CO₂e, or 11.2 tonnes. Absolute emissions increase by 12%.

Both statements are true: the product is cleaner per unit, and total measured emissions rise. Reports should show both intensity and absolute emissions, with clear product and time boundaries. Whether the extra sales displaced more polluting products is a separate question that requires market evidence.

A target focused only on intensity can miss rising totals. A target focused only on absolute emissions can obscure useful efficiency gains during growth. Reporting both gives a clearer account of progress.

11. Supplier incentives and the cost of action

Buyers can request emissions data, but that request does not finance a new furnace, efficient motor, cleaner fuel, or factory redesign. Suppliers may have low margins, limited credit, or short contracts. If buyers demand lower emissions while selecting solely on immediate price, investment may not follow.

Longer purchase commitments, technical support, compatible product specifications, and fair payment terms can help. Contracts should link a defined operational change to measurable outcomes. They should state the product boundary, baseline, reporting period, and verification method.

There are risks. A buyer can move orders to a supplier with a lower reported factor without reducing total industry emissions. A supplier can shift high-emission work to another plant. A clean electricity claim may depend on an accounting instrument with limited local effect. These risks call for careful claims and physical evidence.

Small suppliers also face repeated requests from large buyers. Shared data formats and proportional reporting requirements can lower costs and improve accuracy.

12. Competing views: better data or faster action?

One view prioritises accurate accounting. Without credible baselines, firms can reward weak claims, miss major categories, or report reductions caused only by method changes. Investors and customers need a stable record over time. This position is especially strong for public claims and large procurement decisions.

Another view warns that firms can spend years gathering data while physical emissions continue. If an energy-intensive process has a clear lower-emission option, delaying action until every estimate is perfect may be costly. Engineering evidence can justify action even while the full corporate inventory improves.

A staged approach combines the useful parts of both positions. Screen broadly, begin credible physical improvements, and collect detailed primary data for high-priority activities. Report uncertainty openly. GHG Protocol supports phased improvement in data quality. [4]

Target frameworks also evolve. The Science Based Targets initiative updated its Corporate Net-Zero Standard to version 1.3 and near-term criteria to version 5.3, effective 15 September 2025. These are voluntary target-setting rules. A target's validation does not, by itself, verify that the claimed emissions reduction occurred. [9]

13. Disclosure, regulation, and wider effects

Disclosure requirements can make firms publish boundaries, methods, and missing data. Standard categories can help investors and customers interpret reports. Public procurement can create demand for products with credible life-cycle information. Common formats can help suppliers serve several customers.

Yet disclosure does not automatically make production cleaner. It can become a paperwork exercise if suppliers lack finance, technology, or commercial incentives. Different jurisdictions also have different reporting duties. A global firm should check the actual rules that apply to each entity and reporting year.

Governments can support common data infrastructure, product standards, testing, and clean production. Improvements in electricity, transport, and materials systems can help many firms at once. The IPCC's 2022 assessment explains why technology, energy systems, trade, and demand all affect emissions beyond one buyer's control. [6]

Reliable Scope 3 work can influence product design and supplier selection early, before equipment or contracts lock in a process. It can also create burdens for small firms. A useful system measures whether the environmental benefits justify the reporting effort.

14. A practical programme for a growing business

Appoint clear owners for procurement, operations, finance, and environmental data. Create a list of major inputs and suppliers. Record quantities and the year covered. Keep a versioned file of factors, units, source links, and calculation boundaries.

Estimate the material categories with available data. Rank them by size, uncertainty, and ability to act. Seek supplier-specific data for important materials and processes. Ask for energy use, production quantities, product allocation rules, and the reporting period. Avoid requesting costly detail for every minor purchase.

Select a few physical actions with responsible owners and milestones. A manufacturer may reduce material mass. A distributor may improve vehicle loading. A retailer may support lower-waste packaging or more efficient supplier production. Measure actual changes, not just revised spreadsheet factors.

Report absolute emissions, intensity where useful, category coverage, data quality, and method changes. Explain increases caused by better coverage. Use independent assurance for material claims where feasible. Make the inventory part of design and purchasing decisions rather than a separate annual document.

15. Evidence limits and uncertainty

Scope 3 estimates depend on incomplete records, models, allocation rules, and assumptions about future product use. A buyer may know a supplier's total emissions but not the portion attributable to its purchases. Product lifetimes and end-of-life outcomes can differ from forecasts.

Data can be outdated or mismatched by geography and technology. Acquisitions, outsourcing, and product mix changes can alter reported totals. Corporate reports can overlap. Some interventions move emissions to another location rather than reducing them.

The CDP 2024 disclosure sample is useful evidence of a measurement problem, but its twenty-six-times figure is not a standard ratio for every company. IPCC assessments and peer-reviewed studies establish the importance of supply chains, but do not verify a particular company's claimed reduction. Each important claim needs a traceable boundary and suitable evidence. [5] [6]

16. Nine lessons to retain

  1. Scope 3 covers indirect emissions across a company's value chain, outside Scope 2.
  2. GHG Protocol provides fifteen upstream and downstream categories.
  3. One company's inventory is best used to track its own progress, with consistent methods.
  4. Primary data are useful for specific suppliers; sound secondary data can be better than weak primary data.
  5. Check emission-factor technology, year, geography, completeness, and reliability.
  6. Overlap between corporate reports does not mean the atmosphere received duplicate emissions.
  7. Lower emissions per unit do not guarantee lower total emissions.
  8. Procurement, design, and supplier support must create physical change.
  9. Report uncertainty and method changes separately from reductions.

Knowledge check: five questions and answers

1. How do Scope 1 and Scope 3 differ?

Scope 1 covers direct emissions from sources the company owns or controls. Scope 3 covers other indirect emissions in its value chain, excluding Scope 2.

2. Why might a spend-based factor miss a supplier improvement?

It can change with price or industry averages instead of the supplier's actual energy and production. Supplier-specific activity data are better for tracking a particular intervention.

3. Can the same physical emission appear in several company reports?

Yes. It may appear as a supplier's Scope 1 and a buyer's Scope 3. Corporate totals should not simply be added to estimate global emissions.

4. Can intensity fall while absolute emissions rise?

Yes. Total emissions can increase if production grows faster than emissions per unit decline.

5. What should a company check before claiming a supplier switch reduced emissions?

Product equivalence, consistent boundaries, reliable facility and year data, actual quantities, and evidence of physical production changes.
