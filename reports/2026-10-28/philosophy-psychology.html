Why Feeling Certain Is Not the Same as Being Right

When should we trust our own confidence, and how can we tell whether it matches the evidence?

Research checked 28 October 2026 · Both worked examples use illustrative data · Source dates listed below

Executive summary

People often use confidence to decide whether to act, speak, invest or trust an expert. Confidence can contain useful information, but the strength of a feeling is not a direct measure of truth. Psychology distinguishes accuracy, confidence, calibration and the ability to recognize one's own errors. These are related skills, yet they can vary separately. Research shows that people may be overconfident about difficult tasks, underestimate their relative standing on some tasks, or give probability estimates that are too precise. The famous Dunning–Kruger finding also has a serious statistical debate: some patterns attributed to poor self-knowledge can arise from noisy measurement and regression toward the mean. Research on metacognition explains why confidence often relies on available clues rather than privileged access to correctness. This guide examines how certainty forms, what experiments show, how to read competing interpretations, and how to test confidence with repeated outcomes. It gives practical methods for making judgments more accountable without treating doubt as a virtue in itself. The goal is well-calibrated confidence: confidence that rises when evidence is strong and falls when it is weak.

1. The central question: what does confidence tell us?

A person may sound certain about a market forecast, a diagnosis, a memory or an answer on a test. That certainty can be useful. Experts often have learned to recognize reliable patterns. Yet people can also feel certain because an explanation is familiar, an answer comes to mind quickly or an audience expects decisiveness. None of those experiences alone proves that the claim is correct.

Confidence is a person's judgment about the chance that a belief or decision is right. Accuracy is whether the belief or decision matches a defined outcome. Calibration asks whether stated confidence corresponds to the rate of correct answers over many comparable cases. Someone who says “80% confident” on a large group of judgments is well calibrated at that level if roughly 80% are correct. A single correct answer cannot establish calibration.

These ideas connect to a philosophical question: how should the strength of belief track the strength of evidence? Psychology cannot settle every theory of knowledge. It can test whether confidence predicts accuracy, whether people detect their own errors, and how judgment changes with feedback. The useful distinction is between the experience of certainty and the evidence that would justify it. [1] [5]

2. Four different ways a judgment can go wrong

Researchers distinguish several forms of overconfidence. Overestimation means believing your absolute performance is better than it is. Overplacement means believing your performance ranks higher than other people's. Overprecision means giving a range of possible outcomes that is too narrow, or expressing excessive certainty about an estimate. Moore and Healy showed that these are different phenomena, and that they can move in different directions across easy and difficult tasks. [5]

Consider the logic without assuming a particular person is biased. A student can underestimate their raw test score yet still think they did better than most classmates. A forecaster can predict the correct direction of a change but be too certain about its exact size. A professional can be skilled at a task yet poor at telling when one of their own answers is wrong. These are different failures and require different measurements.

Metacognition means monitoring and evaluating one's own thinking. Metacognitive sensitivity asks whether a person gives higher confidence to their correct answers than to their errors. Metacognitive bias concerns their general tendency to give high or low confidence. A person can be confident on almost every trial while still distinguishing better from worse answers. Fleming and Lau explain why these dimensions should be measured separately. [7]

3. From judgment research to metacognitive measurement

Psychologists have long studied whether people know when they know. Early work on confidence judgments asked participants to choose answers to factual questions and state how certain they were. Fischhoff, Slovic and Lichtenstein reported in 1977 that participants were often too certain about answers, including on questions where they felt strongly confident. Their research made calibration a testable topic rather than a vague personality label. [1]

Koriat's 1993 work studied the feeling of knowing: the sense that a forgotten answer could be recognized later. His accessibility model proposed that this feeling can depend on related information that comes to mind during retrieval, including incorrect information. It challenged the idea that confidence comes from a direct internal signal of truth. [2]

Kruger and Dunning's 1999 studies then linked poor task performance with inflated self-assessment in particular tests. Subsequent work examined how task difficulty, comparison with others and statistical artifacts affect the pattern. Meanwhile, researchers developed more precise measures of metacognition in perception and memory tasks. The field moved from asking whether people are generally overconfident to asking which type of confidence, in which task, with which measure, and under which conditions. [3] [4] [7]

4. Stage one: the mind collects evidence

A judgment begins with information. Some information comes from direct observation; some comes from memory, testimony, statistics or prior expectations. The quality of the information matters, but the person rarely has a complete record of its reliability. People often need to make decisions with partial evidence. Confidence helps them act despite that uncertainty.

One problem is that the mind does not always distinguish the amount of accessible information from the amount of accurate information. If several related details come to mind quickly, an answer can feel supported. Yet those details may share one weak source, or they may be relevant without proving the specific claim. Koriat's research on feeling-of-knowing judgments shows how accessibility can contribute to subjective certainty. [2]

This is a general mechanism, not a claim that every fluent answer is wrong. Familiarity can be a useful signal when experience is broad and feedback has been reliable. The question is whether the cue was learned in an environment where it predicts success. A professional who has repeatedly tested a particular pattern may be justified in trusting it. A person who has heard the same unverified statement many times may feel the same familiarity without having comparable evidence. Separating these situations requires checking the source and the outcome.

5. Stage two: a confidence judgment is formed

After reaching an answer, a person estimates how likely it is to be correct. This estimate can draw on the strength of evidence, the speed of recall, the agreement of other people, the perceived difficulty of the question and past performance. Some cues are useful; others can mislead. A fast response may indicate practiced skill, or it may reflect a familiar but incorrect rule.

Confidence is also affected by how the question is asked. “How likely is your answer to be correct?” is different from “How much better are you than other people?” A task may be easy for everyone, hard for everyone or unevenly distributed across a group. Relative judgments require information about other people's performance, which a person may not have. Burson, Larrick and Klayman found that difficulty changed the direction and size of errors in relative comparisons. [4]

The key lesson is methodological. When a study reports overconfidence, ask what participants estimated: their own score, their percentile rank, or the probability that one answer was correct. Without this distinction, apparently contradictory findings may be about different judgments. Moore and Healy's 2008 analysis offers a framework for reading these results together rather than treating all certainty as one psychological trait. [5]

6. Stage three: confidence and accuracy can separate

Imagine two people who both answer 70% of questions correctly. The first person gives 70% confidence to almost every answer. The second gives 95% confidence when correct and 30% when wrong. Both have the same overall task accuracy. Yet the second person's confidence contains much more information about which answers are reliable. That difference is called metacognitive sensitivity.

Now imagine someone who gives 90% confidence on every answer, regardless of correctness. That person may have high confidence but little ability to distinguish good judgments from bad ones. The difference between confidence level and sensitivity matters because a single average can hide both. Fleming and Lau describe measures that compare confidence distributions for correct and incorrect trials, and discuss how task difficulty and response bias affect those measures. [7]

There is no universal confidence score that works equally well for all tasks. A measure developed for repeated visual decisions may need changes before it can assess business forecasts, open-ended writing or medical decisions. Feedback quality also matters. If the true answer is delayed or ambiguous, people cannot easily learn whether their confidence was justified. A sound assessment must define the outcome, collect enough judgments and avoid changing the task while comparing people.

7. Diagram: how confidence becomes reliable or misleading

The diagram separates three operations. First, a person reaches a judgment using evidence and cues. Second, they report confidence. Third, an independent process checks the outcome. The third stage is often missing from everyday debate. People may remember the occasions when certainty was rewarded and overlook cases where an equally confident claim failed.

Calibration requires repeated predictions and recorded outcomes. Feedback is most useful when it is timely, relevant and hard to reinterpret after the fact. A vague success criterion lets almost any result appear consistent with the original belief. The purpose of the loop is to improve the relation between stated confidence and real accuracy, rather than to force everyone to sound cautious.

8. What the original Dunning–Kruger studies found

Kruger and Dunning studied how people judged their performance on tests involving humor, grammar and logic. In their 1999 paper, participants in the lowest performance quartile substantially overestimated their percentile rank. The authors proposed that limited skill can also reduce the ability to recognize one's own errors. They reported evidence that training could improve participants' recognition of their limitations. [3]

The popular version of this finding is often too broad. It is sometimes used to claim that every inexperienced person is highly confident and every expert is modest. That is not what the original studies established. The researchers tested particular tasks, samples and ways of measuring self-assessment. Their result concerned average patterns within those conditions, not a diagnosis that can be made from one person's tone.

There is also a mathematical issue. If people are divided into groups based on noisy measured scores, the lowest observed group tends to include some people whose scores were unusually low that day. Their later or self-estimated performance can appear higher even without a special inability to know their own ability. This is regression toward the mean. It does not automatically disprove the original explanation, but it means that the shape of a quartile graph cannot by itself prove the psychological mechanism.

9. The serious statistical debate about low-skill overconfidence

Burson, Larrick and Klayman studied relative self-assessment across twelve tasks. They found that the relation between skill and error changed with task difficulty. On some difficult tasks, strong performers were less accurate in judging their relative position than weak performers. The authors argued that noisy judgments and general biases can produce much of the pattern. This provides an alternative explanation for some findings that appear to show a unique deficit among low performers. [4]

Gignac and Zajenkowski published a 2020 analysis arguing that conventional tests of the Dunning–Kruger effect can be distorted by regression toward the mean and better-than-average beliefs. In their study of self-assessed intelligence and an objective reasoning measure, they did not find the hypothesized nonlinear or variance pattern with the tests they considered more suitable. Their conclusion was limited to the measured domain and methods; it did not establish that all self-assessment is accurate. [8]

Two claims must therefore be kept separate. People can be badly calibrated about their ability; there is strong evidence for many forms of miscalibration. Whether the least skilled are uniquely poor at self-monitoring, beyond ordinary noise and measurement effects, is a more specific and contested hypothesis. A good reader examines task difficulty, how groups were formed, the measurement scale and the alternative statistical models before accepting a general explanation.

10. When can expert intuition deserve confidence?

Some people make accurate judgments rapidly. Others develop a strong sense of expertise in settings where outcomes are mostly unpredictable. Kahneman and Klein examined this difference in 2009. They argued that skilled intuition is most likely when the environment contains regular patterns that can be learned and when the person has enough opportunity to receive useful feedback. Subjective confidence by itself is a poor test of expertise. [6]

The idea can be applied without assuming that slow thought is always superior. A trained technician may recognize a recurring fault from a reliable signal. A forecaster may feel equally certain about a rare political event with no comparable past cases. The first judgment may rest on repeated, stable feedback. The second may depend on a persuasive story with little opportunity for correction. Both people can sound equally sure.

Good expertise includes knowing when the task has changed. A rule learned under one set of conditions may stop working after a new technology, regulation or incentive changes the environment. The expert must then reduce confidence or gather new evidence. This is a stronger standard than simply counting years of experience. The relevant questions are whether the world was predictable, whether the person received valid feedback and whether their skill transfers to the present problem.

11. Worked example one: test calibration with recorded outcomes

Illustrative data, not a published experiment. A team records twenty forecasts, each assigned an 80% chance of being correct. When the outcomes become known, twelve forecasts are correct. The observed success rate is 12 ÷ 20 = 60%. The team's stated confidence was 20 percentage points higher than its observed accuracy in this small group.

This result is evidence of overconfidence for these twenty forecasts, but it is not a precise estimate of the team's long-term calibration. With a small sample, chance variation can be large. The questions may also differ in difficulty, and the team might have selected only forecasts that were easy to resolve. A useful review would collect more predictions, group them by stated probability and check whether similar confidence levels have similar observed success rates.

The same log can test discrimination. If high-confidence predictions are more often correct than low-confidence predictions, confidence carries useful information even if the numerical levels are too high. Calibration and discrimination should be reported separately. This follows the distinction between bias and sensitivity in metacognition research. [7] The goal is to learn whether 80% means about eight successes out of ten across a suitable collection of decisions, not to punish a person for one incorrect forecast.

12. Worked example two: why a percentile chart can mislead

Illustrative data, not a published experiment. Suppose 100 students take a short test. Their observed scores include random noise from guessing, fatigue and question selection. Researchers sort them into four groups by that single test score. They then ask students to estimate their usual performance. Even if students have roughly similar self-assessment errors, the bottom observed group may appear to overestimate, because some members performed worse than their normal level on that particular test.

Consider a student whose usual score is 65 out of 100 but who gets 45 on the test after several unlucky errors. If the student estimates 65 as their typical score, the estimate exceeds the observed score by 20 points. That difference does not prove a failure to understand their normal ability. At the other extreme, a student whose usual score is 65 but who gets 85 may appear to underestimate when reporting the same usual level.

The example explains one source of regression toward the mean. It does not establish that the original Dunning–Kruger findings were entirely statistical artifacts. To test a stronger psychological claim, researchers need suitable measures of ability, repeated observations and analyses that separate noise from systematic self-assessment differences. Burson and colleagues, and Gignac and Zajenkowski, show why this distinction matters. [4] [8]

13. How researchers measure confidence properly

First, researchers define an outcome that can be checked. In a two-choice visual task, the outcome may be whether a participant correctly identified a stimulus. In a forecast, it may be whether an event occurred by a fixed date. Without a clear outcome, accuracy is difficult to calculate. Second, participants report confidence before learning the result. Recording the estimate afterward invites hindsight bias and selective memory.

Third, the study uses enough trials to compare confidence with outcomes. Calibration asks whether probabilities match observed frequencies. Discrimination asks whether confidence is higher on correct than incorrect trials. Fleming and Lau review measures based on signal detection theory and receiver operating characteristics, and explain why simple correlations can be affected by response bias and task performance. [7]

Fourth, researchers check whether the scale itself makes sense. Percentile rank, raw score and probability of correctness are not interchangeable. A person's relative rank also depends on the comparison group. Fifth, the analysis considers alternative explanations: task difficulty, noisy scores, selection effects and the consequences of choosing groups after seeing the outcomes. Good measurement cannot remove every source of uncertainty, but it can prevent a vivid chart from being mistaken for a complete theory.

14. Can confidence improve through practice and feedback?

People can learn to state probabilities more carefully, but training is not a guarantee. The important change is to make predictions explicit, record them before the outcome and review them with a consistent scoring rule. This process creates feedback that ordinary memory often fails to provide. It also encourages people to separate a confident explanation from evidence about how often similar explanations have worked.

Mellers and colleagues studied forecasting tournaments and reported that high-performing forecasters maintained strong accuracy across many questions. They described a combination of cognitive styles, task-specific skill, motivation and supportive environments. Their findings suggest that forecasting performance can involve both selection and development. The results do not imply that a short training exercise will make every person a skilled forecaster in every domain. [9]

Useful habits include defining the event, considering plausible alternatives, asking what evidence would change the estimate and updating when new information arrives. For repeated tasks, a prediction log is more reliable than remembering only notable successes. When a question has no measurable outcome, humility may be appropriate, but the absence of a test should be stated openly. The objective is not to reduce all confidence. It is to make confidence more responsive to evidence and to learn which environments support trustworthy judgment.

15. Competing explanations: deficient insight or ordinary noise?

One explanation emphasizes metacognitive skill. To judge performance well, a person must understand what a correct answer requires. Limited skill may therefore make both task performance and self-assessment worse. Kruger and Dunning's experiments provide evidence for this mechanism in their tested settings, including changes after skill training. This is a substantive psychological claim, and it deserves careful testing rather than casual dismissal. [3]

A second explanation emphasizes measurement and task structure. People may have noisy estimates of their ability, use shared assumptions about difficulty, and compare themselves with groups they do not know well. Dividing participants by observed performance can magnify apparent errors at the extremes. Burson and colleagues, and Gignac and Zajenkowski, provide evidence that such factors can explain important patterns without assuming a unique deficit among low scorers. [4] [8]

These explanations can both matter. Different tasks may create different mixtures of genuine skill deficits, response bias and measurement noise. The evidence does not justify declaring that one famous curve explains every overconfident person. It also does not justify treating confidence as meaningless. The better question is which mechanism is supported by the particular experiment, and whether the finding transfers beyond that task.

16. Wider effects: leadership, expertise and public debate

Confidence influences whose advice gets attention. A confident speaker may appear competent even when their evidence is weak. A cautious expert may sound less persuasive because they state uncertainty accurately. This creates a practical problem for organizations: communication style can be mistaken for predictive skill. Decisions should therefore consider records of outcomes, the quality of evidence and the conditions under which expertise was learned.

Teams can improve judgment by asking for estimates before discussion, recording reasons and checking later results. This reduces the chance that a strong voice will define the group's memory of what everyone believed. When several experts agree, it matters whether they used independent evidence or repeated the same source. Agreement is less informative when the underlying errors are shared.

In public policy, uncertainty should be reported in a form that can be evaluated. A range, scenario or probability can be useful when its meaning is clear. False precision is a problem, but excessive vagueness is also a problem because it prevents accountability. The relevant ethical standard is to communicate what is known, how it was tested and what remains uncertain. This is consistent with a philosophical view of intellectual responsibility: belief should be open to revision when evidence changes.

17. What well-calibrated confidence requires

Confidence is most useful when it is connected to outcomes. That connection is stronger when a person has repeated experience in a stable environment, receives accurate feedback and checks predictions rather than relying on memorable successes. It is weaker when tasks are rare, outcomes are ambiguous, or the person has incentives to sound certain. The same individual may be well calibrated in one domain and poorly calibrated in another.

The research also cautions against using popular psychological labels as insults. Calling someone a victim of the Dunning–Kruger effect after one confident statement is not a scientific assessment. A proper claim requires measured performance, a defined self-estimate, an appropriate comparison group and attention to statistical alternatives. The original findings, later criticisms and metacognition research should be read together.

The practical standard is simple to state but demanding to follow. Define the claim. Give a confidence estimate when the task permits it. Record the estimate before the outcome. Check the result independently. Revise the estimate when the evidence changes. Over time, the record reveals whether certainty is earned. The objective is neither permanent doubt nor forceful conviction. It is confidence that tracks the quality of the available evidence.

Key lessons

  1. Confidence is a judgment about correctness; accuracy is an observed outcome.
  2. Calibration asks whether stated probabilities match success rates across comparable cases.
  3. Overestimation, overplacement and overprecision are different errors.
  4. Metacognitive sensitivity measures whether confidence separates correct from incorrect judgments.
  5. Familiarity and ease of recall can influence confidence without proving truth.
  6. The original Dunning–Kruger studies do not justify judging an individual from their tone.
  7. Task difficulty, noisy scores and regression toward the mean can distort self-assessment charts.
  8. Expert intuition is more trustworthy in learnable environments with valid feedback.
  9. Recorded predictions and independent outcomes support better calibration than selective memory.

Five knowledge-check questions

1. How can a person be accurate but poorly calibrated?

They may answer many questions correctly while giving probabilities that are too high or too low. Accuracy and the numerical quality of confidence are separate properties.

2. What is the difference between metacognitive sensitivity and bias?

Sensitivity is the ability to give higher confidence to correct answers than to errors. Bias is the general tendency to report high or low confidence.

3. What did the original Dunning–Kruger studies show, and what remains contested?

They found inflated self-assessment among low performers in specific tasks. The degree to which a special metacognitive deficit, rather than noise and measurement effects, explains the pattern remains debated.

4. Why can years of experience fail to produce reliable intuition?

If the environment is unpredictable or feedback is weak, experience may strengthen familiar judgments without teaching which ones are correct.

5. What is a practical way to test whether 80% confidence is justified?

Record many comparable 80% predictions before outcomes are known. Later check whether roughly 80% were correct, while considering sample size and how cases were selected.
