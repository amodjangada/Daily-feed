Can Thermal Storage Make Industrial Electrification Affordable?

How can factories buy energy at one time, use heat at another, and test whether the change saves money and emissions?

Executive summary

Industrial heat is essential for making food, paper, chemicals, textiles and building materials. Much of it still comes from burning fuel when a factory needs heat. Thermal energy storage offers a different schedule. A plant can use electricity or recovered heat to charge a storage system, then draw useful heat hours later. This can help a factory buy electricity when it is cheaper, use more low-carbon power and avoid some peak demand. Storage does not create energy, and it does not automatically cut emissions. Heat leaks away, equipment has limits, and a low price at night does not always mean low carbon emissions. The business case depends on process temperature, daily operating hours, tariff structure, charging power, heat losses, backup equipment and the cost of capital. This report explains the main storage methods, how to size them, how to compare them with boilers and batteries, and what research shows. It also separates established industrial uses from emerging high-temperature systems. The main conclusion is conditional: storing heat can make electrification more useful when the factory can move electricity consumption without interrupting production.

1. Background: why the timing of industrial heat matters

A factory cannot usually stop a drying oven or sterilisation line whenever the electricity price rises. Production follows customer orders, equipment cycles, quality rules and worker shifts. Boilers have therefore been attractive. They turn fuel into heat when operators request it, and many sites already have steam pipes, burners and maintenance skills. The fuel can be stored on site or delivered through a pipeline. That makes heat supply predictable, though it also exposes the factory to fuel prices and emissions.

Electric heating can be efficient at the point of use, but electricity prices may change sharply by hour. A factory that replaces a gas boiler with an electric boiler and runs it at full power during expensive hours may face a larger energy bill. It can also increase peak demand and require a stronger grid connection. These costs explain why the ability to change when electricity is consumed can matter as much as the heater itself.

Thermal energy storage, abbreviated TES, stores energy as a temperature difference, a phase change, or a reversible chemical process. It can supply heat after the original source has stopped. People have stored heat in hot water and brick for a long time. Modern systems extend the idea to district heating, industrial steam, cooling and higher-temperature applications. The International Renewable Energy Agency reviewed these technology families in its November 2020 Innovation Outlook.

This report follows yesterday's study of industrial heat pumps. A heat pump moves heat from a cooler source to a warmer one using electricity. Thermal storage can complement it by allowing the heat pump to run at different hours from the process it serves. Storage can also pair with electric resistance heating, solar thermal systems or recovered waste heat. These combinations should be evaluated separately because their efficiency and temperature limits differ.

2. Essential terms and physical limits

Thermal power is the rate at which heat is delivered, often measured in kilowatts thermal (kWth) or megawatts thermal (MWth). Thermal energy is the total heat delivered over time, measured in kilowatt-hours thermal (kWhth) or megawatt-hours thermal (MWhth). A storage system rated at 4 MWth and 20 MWhth could, in a simplified case, deliver 4 MWth for five hours. Real output depends on temperature, losses and discharge limits.

Round-trip efficiency is the useful energy recovered divided by the energy put into storage over a full charge and discharge cycle. For a heat-only store, this can be measured as useful thermal energy out divided by thermal energy in. For an electrically charged heat store, another useful measure is useful heat out divided by electricity in. Do not compare these numbers without stating the boundary. A heat pump can deliver more heat than the electricity it uses because it draws additional heat from its environment; this is measured by its coefficient of performance, or COP.

Capacity factor describes how much equipment is used compared with continuous operation at full output. A tariff is the set of electricity charges, which may include an energy price, a demand charge for peak power, network charges and taxes. Demand response means changing electricity use in response to price or grid needs. A charge window is the period when energy is available for storage; a discharge window is when the process needs the stored heat.

Exergy is the part of energy that can perform useful work relative to the surroundings. It helps explain why temperature matters. One megawatt-hour of heat at 80°C cannot always replace one megawatt-hour of heat required at 500°C. Energy quantities alone do not describe whether a storage system can meet a factory's process conditions.

3. How different storage technologies work

The simplest method is sensible-heat storage. A material becomes hotter as energy enters it. Water tanks, rocks, concrete, ceramics and molten salts can store heat in this way. The approximate energy stored equals the material's mass multiplied by its specific heat capacity and the change in temperature. Water is well suited to many low-temperature systems because it is inexpensive and stores substantial heat per unit of mass. At high temperatures, material compatibility, pressure, insulation and thermal stress become more important.

A second method is latent-heat storage. A phase-change material absorbs or releases substantial energy while it melts or freezes. This can help hold a process near a desired temperature. However, heat must move through the material quickly enough. Some materials expand, separate or degrade over repeated cycles. A high laboratory energy density does not guarantee low installed cost or long life in a factory.

A third method is thermochemical storage. Energy drives a reversible chemical or physical reaction, and the reverse reaction releases heat. In some designs, components can be kept apart with low standing losses until needed. These systems can be attractive for longer storage periods, but materials, reaction speed, safety and system complexity remain major design questions. Their commercial maturity varies widely.

A fourth distinction concerns the heat source. An electrically charged thermal battery uses electrical resistance or another electrical conversion method to heat its storage medium. A solar-thermal system concentrates or collects sunlight as heat. A waste-heat store captures heat that would otherwise leave through exhaust or cooling streams. Each has different carbon accounting. Calling every heated brick or molten-salt tank a battery is convenient language, but it should not suggest the device can send electricity back to the grid as easily as an electrochemical battery.

4. Step-by-step: shifting electricity without stopping production

Stage one is mapping the heat demand. Engineers measure how many megawatt-hours of heat a process uses each day, the highest hourly rate, supply and return temperatures, pressure requirements and permitted interruptions. They also record whether demand is steady or comes in batches. Without these measurements, a storage size is mostly a guess.

Stage two is finding the available charging energy. The plant may have low-price grid hours, contracted wind or solar generation, waste heat from another process, or a heat pump that can run when conditions are favourable. Engineers must check whether enough charging power can reach the site. A factory with a small grid connection cannot fill a large store quickly simply because the tariff is cheap.

Stage three is charging the store. Electricity heats a medium, a heat pump transfers heat into it, or recovered process heat enters it through a heat exchanger. Temperature sensors and controls prevent overheating and track the stored energy. Charging takes time and incurs losses. Storage insulation reduces, but cannot remove, heat leakage.

Stage four is delivery. A heat exchanger, steam generator or direct air-flow path transfers heat from storage to the production process. The useful output must meet the required temperature and rate. A store that contains enough total energy can still fail if its heat-transfer equipment cannot deliver the required peak power.

Stage five is control and backup. A controller decides when to charge based on expected demand, electricity prices, carbon signals and safety constraints. A conventional boiler or another heater may remain as backup. The best design may be hybrid, with a smaller electric heater and storage covering predictable periods while a boiler handles rare peaks.

Stage six is measurement. Operators compare actual electricity purchased, heat delivered, standing losses, demand charges, downtime and emissions with a clear baseline. The baseline must represent the same production volume and quality. If output rises or the weather changes, a raw monthly bill comparison can give the wrong result.

5. Visual explanation: a heat-shifting system

The visual shows why a thermal store is a system, not merely a tank. An attractive storage material is insufficient if the charging heater is undersized, the discharge exchanger is slow, or the site cannot obtain low-cost electricity at the required hours. The controls must also respect product quality and worker safety.

The most valuable flexibility is often the ability to avoid a few expensive hours, rather than storing energy for many days. Longer storage can be useful, but it usually needs more insulation, capacity and capital. A plant should first identify the duration of its costly or high-emission periods before choosing a technology.

6. Worked example one: size a heat store

Illustrative engineering calculation. All quantities are invented to explain sizing. They are not performance claims for a commercial system.

Consider a food-processing plant that needs 2 MWth of useful process heat for four evening hours. It wants to avoid operating its electric heater during that window. The required useful heat is 2 MWth multiplied by four hours, or 8 MWhth. Assume the storage and discharge system delivers 90% of the energy charged during the cycle. To supply 8 MWhth, it must receive about 8.89 MWh of thermal input before the evening window.

If the available charge window is five hours, the average thermal charging rate must be at least 8.89 divided by five, or 1.78 MWth, before allowing for other losses and practical margins. If a resistance heater provides the charge and is assumed to convert electricity to heat with nearly 100% efficiency at the heater boundary, its electrical input is approximately 1.78 MW. The site must check whether its grid connection can support that extra load. If charging uses a heat pump, the electrical power could be lower, but the achievable storage temperature and COP must be checked.

Now consider discharge power. A store with 8 MWhth of usable capacity and a discharge limit of only 1 MWth cannot supply the plant's 2 MWth requirement on its own. It would need a larger exchanger, another heater or a changed process schedule. This is why energy capacity and power rating must be specified separately. Engineers also add a reserve margin for uncertain production, heat losses and equipment ageing.

A sensible-heat system could be sized from the usable temperature swing and material properties. But a simple mass calculation omits tank walls, heat exchangers, pumps, insulation, foundations and controls. The final installed cost can differ greatly from the cost of the storage material alone.

7. Business economics: the full cost of useful heat

The central economic question is whether the project lowers the present value of producing the required heat over its life. The relevant comparison is not the price of electricity alone. It includes capital cost, finance, maintenance, storage losses, demand charges, backup fuel, equipment replacement, process downtime and any carbon cost. The value of heat that would otherwise be wasted also matters.

A time-of-use tariff can create an opportunity when electricity is much cheaper during some hours. However, the tariff may charge for maximum monthly power. Fast charging could create a new demand peak and cancel much of the energy saving. Network fees may also differ between hours. A site with a fixed electricity price may have little incentive to shift consumption even if wholesale market prices vary.

Capital cost is often decisive. A storage system that saves operating expense but requires a large upfront payment may have a long payback period. A short simple payback can also be misleading if the project needs frequent repairs or if electricity-price spreads shrink. Discounted cash-flow analysis converts future costs and savings into today's value using a discount rate. The result should be tested under several price and production scenarios.

Another factor is the cost of the alternative. If the factory has an efficient gas boiler with many years of useful life, replacing it early can be expensive. If the boiler is near retirement, the decision changes. An electrified plant may also value lower local air pollution, reduced fuel-delivery risk, easier process control and the ability to meet customer emissions requirements. These benefits should be measured rather than assumed.

8. Worked example two: when price shifting pays

Illustrative financial calculation. Prices, efficiencies and operating days are invented. They are not a tariff quote or investment recommendation.

Assume a factory needs 8 MWhth of useful heat each working day for 300 days. Direct electric heating during peak hours would buy electricity at $110 per MWh. A thermal store can charge at $35 per MWh and deliver 90% of the charged energy as useful heat. Ignore the heater's small conversion losses in both cases. Direct heating costs $880 per day in energy. Stored heat requires 8 divided by 0.90, or about 8.89 MWh of charging electricity, costing about $311 per day.

The apparent energy saving is about $569 per day, or roughly $171,000 across 300 days. This figure excludes the capital cost of storage, interest, maintenance, insurance, standby losses beyond the assumed cycle efficiency, extra demand charges and backup heat. It also assumes that enough low-price hours exist on every operating day and that the site can charge without raising its tariff demand peak.

If the installed project cost were $900,000 and annual non-energy costs were $25,000, a simple annual net saving would be about $146,000 and simple payback roughly 6.2 years. A proper decision would discount future cash flows, include replacement and residual value, and test weaker electricity-price spreads. A 6.2-year simple payback alone does not establish that the project beats a boiler or another use of capital.

The calculation also does not show a carbon benefit. To estimate emissions, compare the emissions from the electricity actually used during charging with those from the displaced heat source. The marginal power plant supplying extra electricity may differ from the average grid mix. Hourly matching, on-site renewable supply and local dispatch conditions can materially change the result.

9. What independent research establishes

IRENA's November 2020 Innovation Outlook describes thermal energy storage as a way to separate heat demand from immediate energy supply. It reviews applications in industry, buildings, district heating and power systems. The report identifies several established technologies and emerging options. Its projections for future markets are scenarios, not observed deployment totals, and should not be treated as proof that any specific factory project will pay back.

The IEA Energy Storage Technology Collaboration Programme published a Flexible Sector Coupling report in November 2024. It examined how storage links electricity, heating, cooling and transport so that energy from one time or sector can meet demand at another time. This supports the system-level logic of flexibility, while leaving local cost and performance questions to project design.

A May 2025 IEA Energy Storage TCP report on large thermal storage for district heating examines planning and decision factors under different locations and system conditions. District heating is not identical to industrial steam. Its value for factory engineers lies in the need to account for heat networks, temperature levels, storage integration and operating constraints rather than looking only at the storage medium.

IRENA's September 2024 study of smart electrification models a distribution grid serving about 25,000 people. It finds that combining flexible heat pumps, electric vehicles and rooftop solar can reduce network stress and defer reinforcement in the modelled system. These are model results for a particular grid and assumptions. They do not establish that a factory installing a thermal battery will automatically obtain the same savings.

An August 2024 peer-reviewed study by Serena Patel and co-authors assessed repurposing Indian coal plants with molten-salt thermal energy storage. Its focus is electricity-system assets and techno-economic opportunities in India, not direct proof of industrial boiler economics. It is useful because it shows how plant-level cost, operating conditions and existing equipment affect the value of a thermal storage conversion.

A June 2025 preprint by Galteland and co-authors models a hybrid steam-electric system with a steam accumulator and battery storage using Norway and Germany electricity data. The authors report that steam accumulators were favoured under their assumed current investment costs, while battery economics varied with market conditions. As a preprint and model study, it should be read as evidence about design trade-offs, not a universal commercial result.

The International Energy Agency's April 2024 work on seasonal electricity variability also matters. It finds that flexibility needs rise as power systems add variable renewables and electrify demand. Industrial heat storage is one possible flexibility resource among many. Short-duration demand response, batteries, dispatchable generation and network upgrades all compete to meet system needs.

10. Climate accounting: why the charging hour matters

Electrification can cut direct combustion emissions at a factory. But the electricity used to charge a store must come from somewhere. A plant connected to a fossil-heavy grid may shift emissions from its boiler stack to power stations. Whether total emissions fall depends on the displaced fuel, heater efficiency, storage losses and the generation mix at the charging hours.

Annual average grid emission factors are useful for inventories but can conceal hourly effects. A solar-heavy system may have low-emission midday electricity and fossil generation at night. In another region, wind output may be stronger overnight. A project should examine local generation and the marginal response to extra demand where suitable data are available. The marginal effect means the emissions change caused by one more unit of electricity use, rather than the average emissions of all electricity produced.

Storage also has embodied emissions: materials, manufacture, transport, installation and eventual disposal. A well-used system can spread these impacts over many cycles, but poor utilisation increases their impact per unit of delivered heat. Life-cycle assessment counts impacts from production through use and end of life. The boundary must include both the new storage equipment and the equipment it replaces or avoids.

Claims of zero-carbon heat should therefore state whether they mean zero direct on-site emissions, low operational emissions under a stated electricity contract, or a measured life-cycle result. Those claims have different meanings. The strongest projects combine efficient heat use, low-emission charging electricity and reliable production performance.

11. Why promising projects still fail

One failure mode is temperature mismatch. A storage system can hold a large amount of heat but deliver it below the pressure or temperature needed for steam or a chemical process. A second is charging-power mismatch. The factory may have only a few cheap hours, but not enough electrical capacity to fill the store. A third is a weak price spread. Storage losses and extra fees can remove the expected saving.

Other problems are operational. Insulation can degrade. Heat exchangers foul. Thermal cycling can crack materials or seals. A control system may forecast production incorrectly. Maintenance teams may need new training. A process that requires high reliability will often retain backup capacity, which adds cost even when it is rarely used.

Financing is a further barrier. Energy-saving projects compete with equipment that directly increases output. Factory managers may use a short payback threshold and reject projects with uncertain energy-price savings. Suppliers may offer performance guarantees, but the contract must define heat quality, availability, measurement boundaries and who bears the risk of changing tariffs.

Some applications are inherently less attractive. A plant with steady heat demand, low-cost fuel and little variation in electricity prices may gain little from storage. In such a case, direct heat-pump use, waste-heat recovery, better insulation or process redesign may deliver greater value. The correct question is which intervention gives the most useful heat and emissions reduction per unit of capital.

12. Competing views: deploy now or wait?

The case for early deployment is strongest where thermal storage uses proven materials, the factory has predictable heat demand, electricity prices vary substantially and clean power is available during charging hours. Supporters point to the value of reducing fuel exposure, lowering peak electricity demand and preparing factories for stricter emissions rules. Water storage and some steam accumulators already have long operating histories. Delaying a sound project may lock in another long-lived fossil boiler.

The cautious case is strongest for high-temperature systems with limited operating records or complex integration. Critics ask whether advertised storage cost includes heaters, insulation, heat exchangers, electrical upgrades and backup. They also question whether future tariffs will retain the price spread that supports payback. These are serious objections because project economics can be sensitive to assumptions that suppliers do not control.

Both views can be reasonable in different plants. The evidence does not support treating all thermal storage as either mature and cheap or speculative and uneconomic. A low-temperature hot-water tank and a high-temperature industrial heat battery face very different engineering and commercial risks. Procurement should match the maturity of the exact application.

A practical middle course is staged deployment. Measure demand first, run a detailed hourly model, test the system at a smaller scale where possible, and expand only after verifying heat delivery and savings. The stage gates should be fixed before procurement, not changed after disappointing results.

13. A decision framework for a factory or investor

Begin with the process rather than a product brochure. Record the heat temperature, power profile, annual hours and required reliability. Identify waste-heat streams and efficiency improvements before sizing new supply. Avoid buying storage to cover heat demand that insulation or process redesign could remove at lower cost.

Next, model at least one full year of hourly electricity prices, demand charges, fuel costs and production schedules. Test whether low-price hours are long enough to charge the store. Check the site's transformer and connection limits. Compare direct electrification, heat pumps with storage, electric boilers with storage, and continued fuel use with efficiency upgrades.

Then obtain engineering guarantees. Ask suppliers to state usable thermal capacity at the required discharge temperature, maximum continuous output, expected cycle efficiency, standing losses, degradation, maintenance needs and safety provisions. The contract should specify what is measured at the factory process boundary. Energy stored inside a tank is less useful than verified heat delivered to the process.

Finally, test financial and climate performance under several conditions. Increase electricity prices, narrow price spreads, lower utilisation and include downtime. Apply a realistic discount rate. Use an emissions method suited to the decision and disclose uncertainty. The project is strongest when it still performs acceptably under plausible adverse conditions.

At the system level, policymakers can support useful flexibility with clear time-varying prices, fair grid access, transparent emissions data and standards for measuring delivered heat. But a tariff that rewards shifting load must also reflect real grid costs. Otherwise it may simply move costs to other users.

14. Key lessons

  1. Thermal storage moves heat use across time; it does not create energy.
  2. Specify thermal power and usable thermal energy separately.
  3. Temperature and pressure determine whether stored heat is useful to a process.
  4. Sensible, latent and thermochemical stores have different maturity and design limits.
  5. Storage value depends on charging windows, tariffs, grid capacity and heat demand.
  6. Cheap charging energy is not always low-carbon energy.
  7. Compare delivered heat costs, not just storage material prices.
  8. Use a clear baseline and measure actual heat at the process boundary.
  9. Proven low-temperature storage and emerging high-temperature systems require different risk judgments.
  10. Independent models support flexibility's potential but do not guarantee savings at a specific factory.

15. Knowledge check: five answered questions

1. What does thermal storage change in a factory's energy system?

It allows heat to be produced or collected at one time and used later. Production can continue while the plant changes when it consumes electricity, within storage power, temperature and capacity limits.

2. Why are a 10 MWhth rating and a 2 MWth rating different?

The first is energy capacity; the second is maximum heat delivery rate. At 2 MWth, 10 MWhth would last five hours in a simplified loss-free case.

3. Why can a low-price charging hour fail to save money?

Losses, new demand charges, insufficient charging power, extra maintenance or a small price spread can exceed the energy-price benefit.

4. Does a thermal battery automatically reduce emissions?

No. Emissions depend on the electricity source and charging time, displaced fuel, system efficiency, equipment manufacture and lifetime utilisation.

5. What evidence is needed before purchasing a system?

Measured process heat demand, hourly price and emissions scenarios, verified usable heat capacity and discharge temperature, installed cost, backup plan, maintenance needs and sensitivity analysis.
