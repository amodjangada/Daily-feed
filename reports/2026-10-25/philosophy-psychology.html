Why Remembering Can Change a Memory

When does recall reconstruct an event, and when can later information change what we remember?

Evidence-led learning report · 25 October 2026 · Witness example is illustrative

Executive summary

Memory lets people use past experience, but remembering is not the same as playing back a recording. A remembered event is reconstructed from stored information, present cues and knowledge acquired later. This can make recall useful and flexible. It can also produce mistakes. Laboratory studies show that the words used in a question can influence later reports of an event. People sometimes recall related words that were never presented. Researchers also study reconsolidation: under certain conditions, a reactivated memory may become open to change before it stabilizes again. This finding is strong in some animal experiments, while its scope and practical use in humans remain disputed. Not every act of recall changes a memory, and the original information is not necessarily erased when a person reports a false detail. This guide separates three processes that are often confused: reconstruction during recall, misinformation after an event, and reconsolidation after reactivation. It explains the experiments, the limits of each claim, and the practical steps that can protect accurate records and improve learning.

What a memory is—and is not

Memory is a set of processes that lets the nervous system retain information and use it later. Researchers often distinguish encoding, storage and retrieval. Encoding is the first processing of an experience. Storage refers to changes that allow information to persist. Retrieval is the use of that information after some time has passed. These are useful labels, but the processes overlap in the living brain.

Episodic memory concerns events experienced in a particular time and place. Semantic memory concerns general knowledge, such as the meaning of a word. Procedural memory supports skills, such as a familiar movement. A real event can involve all three. You may remember a conversation, know what the words meant and remember how to use the phone on which it happened.

An episodic memory is not a complete recording. Attention selects only part of an event. Later recall uses available details, related knowledge and cues from the present situation. Some parts may be accurate while other parts are inferred. A person can sincerely believe an inference was seen directly. This makes memory valuable for understanding experience but imperfect as evidence.

A useful distinction is between the event, the information stored about it and the story produced during recall. The event has already happened. The stored information may be incomplete. The recalled story is a present act of reconstruction. These layers help explain why two witnesses can give different accounts without either one intending to lie.

Researchers disagree about how particular memory traces change at the level of cells and networks. Yet there is broad evidence that reports of past events can be shaped by information encountered after those events [1][2].

How scientists discovered memory distortion

In a well-known 1974 study, Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed participants films of traffic accidents and varied a verb in a question about vehicle speed. The wording influenced reported speed estimates. In a later test, people exposed to certain wording were more likely to report broken glass that had not appeared in the film [1]. The experiment shows that post-event language can affect later reports. It does not show that every witness memory can be changed at will.

In 1995, Henry Roediger and Kathleen McDermott studied lists of related words. Participants heard words associated with an unpresented theme word. Many later recalled or recognized the theme word despite its absence. The authors reported false recall of the missing associate in 40 percent of trials in one experiment and 55 percent in another, under the conditions they tested [2]. These figures belong to a particular laboratory task, not to all human memories.

The 1993 source-monitoring framework developed by Marcia Johnson, Shahin Hashtroudi and D. Stephen Lindsay gave researchers a way to describe another source of error. People must judge whether remembered information came from seeing, hearing, imagining, reading or being told something. These source judgments depend on features of experience and can fail [3].

In 2000, Karim Nader and colleagues reported that a learned fear response in rats could become sensitive to a protein-synthesis blocker after the memory was reactivated [4]. This raised a different possibility: retrieval might sometimes alter the biological stability of an established memory. Later human studies tested related questions.

These findings concern connected but distinct phenomena. A misleading question can change a report. A false word can arise from normal meaning-based processing. A reactivated fear memory can show biological signs of renewed instability. It would be a mistake to treat them as identical mechanisms.

Stage 1: encoding is selective

People do not store every detail of a scene. They direct attention toward goals, threats, faces, sounds and unusual objects. Information outside attention may receive weak processing. Even information that reaches awareness may be stored in a form that emphasizes meaning over exact wording.

Suppose a witness watches a short event while also searching for a friend. The witness may later remember the main action but not the order of small movements. This is a normal limit of attention and encoding. A later question that assumes a detail was present can be difficult to evaluate because the witness has little stored information with which to check it.

Memory researchers often distinguish verbatim information, such as the exact words in a sentence, from gist, or the general meaning. Gist can be useful. It lets us understand ideas without preserving every sentence. But it can also support false recall when a strongly related detail fits the remembered meaning. The word-list findings of Roediger and McDermott illustrate this risk [2].

Encoding also depends on context. Sleep, stress, interest and prior knowledge can affect what is retained, though the size and direction of an effect depend on the task. No simple rule says that an emotional event must be remembered accurately in every detail.

The practical consequence is that later confidence cannot recover information that was never encoded. Asking more forcefully, repeating the question or encouraging vivid imagination does not guarantee that a missing detail will become available. A careful interviewer should allow uncertainty rather than pressure a person to fill gaps.

Stage 2: retrieval rebuilds an account

When a person remembers, the brain uses cues to recover and combine information. A cue might be a place, a smell, a question or a related thought. Some cues support accurate access to stored details. Others activate related knowledge that can be confused with the original experience.

The source-monitoring framework explains how people judge the origin of a mental image or statement [3]. A remembered detail may feel familiar, but familiarity alone does not establish where it came from. The person may have seen it directly, heard it later or imagined it while discussing the event. A source judgment can be quick and automatic or slow and deliberate.

Reconstruction is useful because it connects past information to present needs. It allows a person to infer what happened and plan what to do next. Yet the same ability creates a route for error. The mind can supply a detail that makes a story coherent even when the detail was not part of the original event.

This does not mean that every memory is false. Many everyday memories are sufficiently accurate for practical tasks. Accuracy depends on the event, the available cues, the delay, the question and the kind of detail being tested. Researchers test these factors rather than assuming that memory is either a perfect record or pure invention.

The key question is not simply whether someone remembers vividly. It is what evidence supports the detail and whether the source of the detail can be checked. Independent records, earlier notes and neutral questions can help separate a remembered observation from a later inference.

Stage 3: post-event information can enter the account

The misinformation effect describes changes in later memory reports after exposure to misleading information. Loftus reviewed decades of work in 2005, including studies of when misinformation has an effect and when warnings can reduce it [5]. Misleading information may come from an interview, a news report, a conversation or a repeated story.

One route is source confusion. A person learns a detail after the event and later attributes it to the event itself. Another possibility is that later information affects how the original information is accessed. Some theories propose changes to stored representations under certain conditions. Researchers continue to debate the relative contribution of these mechanisms.

The order of events matters. An initial neutral account made before discussion can provide a record against which later changes are compared. A report written only after repeated conversations is harder to interpret. Multiple people may share the same later story, so agreement between their final accounts does not always provide independent confirmation.

Warnings can sometimes help. A witness who knows that later information may be inaccurate can be more careful about source judgments. But warnings are not a universal repair. Once a false detail feels familiar, a person may struggle to distinguish it from an original observation.

This is why investigators, journalists and researchers should avoid questions that quietly insert an unverified fact. 'What color was the bag?' assumes that a bag existed. 'What objects, if any, did you notice?' allows the witness to report what was actually observed. The second question does not guarantee accuracy, but it reduces one source of suggestion.

Stage 4: consolidation and reconsolidation

Consolidation is the set of processes that help newly formed memories become more stable over time. It involves changes in neural systems and can continue after an event. Reconsolidation is a proposed process in which an established memory, after suitable reactivation, becomes temporarily open to change and must stabilize again.

The 2000 experiment by Nader, Schafe and LeDoux is central to this idea [4]. Rats learned an association between a cue and an unpleasant event. After the memory had formed, the researchers reactivated it and administered a drug that blocked protein synthesis in the amygdala, a brain region involved in fear learning. Later expression of the learned response was reduced. When the drug was given without memory reactivation, the same effect was not observed. Timing also mattered.

The study supports a biological process in this particular animal model. It does not show that recalling any human autobiographical memory always erases and rewrites it. Animal fear conditioning, human stories and learned skills differ in important ways. A drug-induced change in fear expression also does not by itself reveal every detail of the underlying memory representation.

Researchers now examine boundary conditions: the factors that determine whether reactivation makes a memory unstable. These may include memory age, strength, reminder duration, surprise and whether new information conflicts with expectation. A reminder can also start ordinary retrieval or new learning without necessarily producing reconsolidation.

The distinction matters because reconsolidation is sometimes described as a simple method for editing memories. Current evidence does not justify a general-purpose technique for deliberately rewriting personal history. Some controlled findings are promising, but translation to reliable clinical practice remains difficult.

Human studies: when new information joins an old memory

In 2007, Hupbach, Gomez, Hardt and Nadel studied how reminders affected memory for lists of objects [6]. Participants learned one list on the first day. On the next day, some received a reminder of the first learning session before learning a second list. At a later test, reminded participants were more likely to mix items from the second list into recall of the first. The timing of the test mattered.

The study is important because it concerns episodic memory in humans rather than only fear responses in animals. It suggests that a reminder can change how later information is linked to an earlier event representation. The authors interpreted the result in terms of reconsolidation. Other mechanisms, including source confusion and context-dependent retrieval, remain relevant to the broader debate.

A 2010 Nature paper by Schiller and colleagues studied a different form of learning: conditioned fear in humans [7]. The researchers reported that extinction training given after a brief reminder, within a proposed reconsolidation window, reduced later return of the conditioned fear response in their experimental conditions. The online paper first appeared in December 2009 and the journal issue appeared in January 2010.

The results stimulated interest in possible treatments for persistent fears. But a laboratory measure such as skin conductance is not the same as recovery from a complex clinical disorder. Later studies have reported mixed outcomes and explored when retrieval-extinction procedures work. This limits how strongly we can generalize from one protocol.

Together, these studies show that memory is responsive to new experience. They do not support the claim that a person can reliably choose a painful memory, recall it once and permanently replace it with a preferred story.

In the second, later information can affect a report or source judgment. In the third, a reactivated learned response may become open to modification under certain conditions. A changed answer does not by itself identify which route occurred.

Worked example: a witness and a later conversation

Illustrative example. Two people watch a bicycle collide with a parked scooter. The first person writes an account immediately and reports a red bicycle, a stationary scooter and no visible injury. The second person speaks with several bystanders before giving an account. One bystander confidently says the cyclist was using a phone.

A day later, the second person reports that the cyclist was looking at a phone. That detail may have been observed, inferred from the collision or learned from the bystander. The report alone cannot identify the source. If the second person feels certain, that confidence still does not prove direct observation.

The first person's early written account is useful because it was recorded before later discussion. It is not automatically correct. Both witnesses could have missed a detail. A camera recording, if available and authentic, would provide an additional independent source. The best conclusion combines sources while keeping uncertainty visible.

A neutral interviewer could ask each person to describe the event freely, then ask when and how each detail became known. The interviewer should record the first answer before supplying new facts. The goal is to preserve evidence, not to force two stories into agreement. This approach follows from source-monitoring and misinformation research [3][5].

The surprising benefit: retrieval can improve learning

Memory reconstruction has costs, but retrieval is also an effective way to learn. In 2006, Roediger and Karpicke compared studying material again with trying to recall it during learning [8]. In their experiments, repeated studying sometimes helped immediate performance, while prior retrieval practice produced better retention on delayed tests. This is often called the testing effect.

Retrieval practice makes a learner actively produce an answer rather than only reread it. The process can strengthen later access and reveal gaps in understanding. Feedback is valuable because it helps correct errors. A useful study session might involve reading a section, closing the material, explaining the main point from memory and then checking the explanation against the source.

This benefit should not be confused with the claim that every recall makes an episodic memory more accurate. Recalling a studied concept under controlled conditions differs from reconstructing a witnessed event after misleading discussion. Repeatedly rehearsing an error without correction can also make that error more familiar.

The practical lesson is to use retrieval differently for different goals. For learning, active recall followed by feedback can be powerful. For evidence gathering, preserve an early account, avoid suggestive prompts and distinguish observed details from later information. Both practices respect the constructive nature of memory.

The two uses share a basic principle: what happens around retrieval matters. Context, feedback and later information influence what a person can report or learn. Good methods make those influences visible rather than assuming that recall is a neutral readout.

Competing explanations and important limits

Researchers broadly agree that memory reports can be distorted. They disagree about what exactly changes in each experiment. One account emphasizes reconstruction and source-monitoring errors: people may retain information but misattribute its origin. Another emphasizes modification of stored representations after reactivation. Both processes may occur in different tasks, and their relative roles are not always easy to measure.

Reconsolidation researchers point to timing-sensitive animal experiments and selected human findings as evidence that established memories can enter a changeable state [4][6][7]. Critics of broad reconsolidation claims note that successful reactivation is hard to define, results vary across protocols, and a reduction in observed fear may reflect altered expression or new inhibitory learning rather than deletion of the original memory.

There is a second debate about practical intervention. Some researchers see a route to more effective treatment of persistent fear. Others emphasize that a conditioned response in a laboratory is much simpler than trauma-related symptoms in daily life. A clinical treatment must show safety and benefit in appropriate patient studies. The existence of a laboratory effect does not settle that question.

The phrase 'memory is rewritten every time it is recalled' is therefore too strong. Some recall leaves later performance largely unchanged. Some retrieval strengthens retention. Some reminders increase the effect of later information. Some specific reactivations permit reconsolidation-like changes. The conditions and mechanisms differ.

A careful explanation separates the observed behavior, the proposed mechanism and the application. The observed behavior may be a false report, a change in skin conductance or a later recall score. The mechanism is an interpretation supported to varying degrees by experiments. The application requires further testing.

Why it matters: justice, education and personal records

Eyewitness evidence can influence major decisions. Research on misinformation and source monitoring supports neutral interviews, careful recording of early accounts and limits on exposure to other witnesses' stories. These steps do not make eyewitness testimony useless. They help reduce avoidable sources of error and make the remaining uncertainty easier to assess.

Education offers a different application. Students often mistake familiar material for material they can explain from memory. Retrieval practice can reveal this gap. When combined with feedback and spaced study, it can support durable learning. The relevant outcome is later accurate recall, not how easy a passage feels during rereading.

In everyday life, the same principles suggest caution about highly detailed stories reconstructed long after an event. Photos, messages and contemporaneous notes can help, but they also need source checks. A photograph may show what happened at one moment without proving the whole sequence. A message can preserve an early interpretation rather than a complete fact.

There is an ethical issue in efforts to change distressing memories. Reducing harmful emotional responses may help some people, but claims about deleting or rewriting memories require strong evidence. People should not be encouraged to treat a research protocol as an established therapy without clinical support.

The larger lesson is that memory serves action, learning and meaning. It was not designed as a legal recording device. Good judgment uses memory where it is useful and adds independent evidence where precision matters.

Key lessons

  1. Encoding is selective. An event and the stored information about it are different.
  2. Retrieval reconstructs an account using stored details, cues and current knowledge.
  3. The source of a remembered detail can be confused with the detail itself.
  4. Suggestive questions can change later reports; they do not prove that the original trace was erased.
  5. False recall in word-list experiments shows that meaning can produce plausible errors.
  6. Reconsolidation is a conditional research mechanism, not a rule that every recall rewrites memory.
  7. Animal fear conditioning and human autobiographical memory require different levels of evidence.
  8. An early independent record can help protect evidence from later social influence.
  9. Retrieval practice with feedback can improve long-term learning.
  10. Confidence, vividness and repetition are insufficient substitutes for independent verification.

Knowledge check

1. Why is remembering different from playing a recording?

Recall uses incomplete stored information together with present cues and knowledge, so an account may contain both recovered details and inferences.

2. What is source monitoring?

It is the process of judging whether information came from seeing, hearing, reading, imagining or another source; mistakes can lead to false attributions.

3. What did Loftus and Palmer demonstrate?

In their traffic-film experiments, wording in questions affected speed estimates and later reports of broken glass. The result does not establish that all memories are equally suggestible.

4. Does reconsolidation mean every recalled memory is erased and rewritten?

No. Reconsolidation depends on conditions that vary by task, and changes in observed behavior do not always establish erasure of the original memory.

5. How should retrieval be used for studying and for witness evidence?

For studying, actively recall and check answers against reliable feedback. For evidence, record an early free account, avoid leading questions and preserve independent records.
