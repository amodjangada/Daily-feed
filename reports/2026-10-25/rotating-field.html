Why the World Is Retiring Leap Seconds

An international decision made ten days ago changes the way our clocks follow Earth. Understanding it requires physics, history, engineering and a careful definition of time.

Executive summary

The world has long used two different ideas of time. One comes from Earth's rotation: a day is linked to the planet turning beneath the Sun. The other comes from atomic physics: the duration of a second is defined by a repeatable transition in cesium atoms. Atomic time is stable. Earth's rotation changes slightly, so the two measurements slowly move apart.

Since 1972, Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) has used occasional leap seconds to keep clock time close to Earth's rotation. Inserting an extra second has helped astronomy and kept civil time connected to the sky. It has also caused difficult edge cases for computers, networks and satellite systems. A leap second can make an ordinary minute contain 61 seconds. Some software assumes that cannot happen.

On 15 October 2026, the 28th General Conference on Weights and Measures adopted Resolution 5. It states that continuous UTC becomes effective on 20 May 2027 and sets a much wider allowed difference between UTC and Earth-rotation time: 3,600 seconds. This is a real adopted decision, not an earlier proposal. It does not mean clocks suddenly jump by one hour in 2027. It allows the difference to change slowly without inserting leap seconds. The International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service (IERS) also announced on 6 July 2026 that there will be no leap second at the end of December 2026. [1] [2]

The deeper lesson is that a standard is a design choice with physical constraints. Accurate clocks require atoms, measurements, international coordination, communication networks and rules about exceptional cases. Changing one small rule affects many industries.

1. The central question: what should a clock follow?

Imagine that two laboratories own extremely good clocks. Their atomic seconds agree. But one laboratory also measures the position of stars to estimate how far Earth has turned. It finds that the planet did not rotate by precisely the same amount as yesterday. Which measurement should control the length of a second?

Modern standards answer in two parts. The SI second, the scientific unit of duration, comes from atomic physics. Civil time uses this stable second to run clocks, but for decades it also applied leap seconds to stay close to the turning Earth. This hybrid rule worked when accurate time was mostly a specialist service. It became harder as millions of servers, devices and transactions began relying on uninterrupted sequences of timestamps.

It helps to separate three questions. Duration asks how much time passes between events. Synchronization asks whether distant clocks agree about when an event happened. Calendar time asks which date and local hour society assigns to an event. A change to leap seconds concerns synchronization and the relationship to Earth rotation. It does not change the atomic definition of a second, or the choice of time zone in a country.

The distinction is practical. A navigation receiver must compare signal arrival times with very high precision. A calendar app must show a meeting at a familiar local hour. An astronomer may need Earth's actual orientation to point a telescope. All three need time, but they need different properties of it.

2. From the Sun to a global clock network

For much of history, communities defined noon by the Sun. Local noon depended on longitude. A clock set to solar noon in one town could disagree with a clock farther east. This was manageable when journeys were slow and communication was local. Railways, telegraphs and national administration increased the need for common schedules.

Standard time zones were a social solution: regions chose shared clock offsets rather than each town using its own solar time. This did not require the entire world to have the same local hour. It required a shared reference from which local hours could be calculated. Greenwich became an important reference through navigation and international agreements. Yet an accurate reference still needed an accurate second.

Early precision clocks relied on mechanical motion. Quartz oscillators later improved stability. But a pendulum responds to its environment, and a quartz crystal can change frequency with temperature, age and manufacture. Atomic physics offered a more reproducible basis. In 1967, the international second was defined using the cesium-133 atom. This separated the scientific unit of duration from small changes in Earth's spin. [3]

The next question was how to combine a stable atomic second with the astronomical day. The system introduced in 1972 used UTC with occasional leap seconds. The objective was to keep UTC near UT1, a measure of Earth's rotation angle. For decades, this was a compromise between physical regularity and astronomical meaning.

That compromise had a hidden assumption: a one-second adjustment would be easy for technology to accept. Many clocks could. Some databases, operating systems and distributed applications could not, or handled the event differently. The internet also increased the number of machines that needed to agree across borders and vendors.

3. How an atomic clock measures a second

A clock needs two parts: a process that repeats and a way to count its repetitions. A mechanical clock uses a moving object. An electronic clock often uses a quartz crystal. An atomic clock uses a frequency linked to a change between specific energy states of atoms.

Cesium-133 atoms can change between two closely spaced energy states when they absorb microwave radiation at the correct frequency. In a practical clock, electronics generate microwaves and adjust their frequency. The instrument detects how many atoms respond. When the response indicates resonance, a feedback system keeps the oscillator aligned with the atomic transition. Electronics count the cycles to measure time.

The SI second is defined through the frequency 9,192,631,770 hertz for the specified cesium transition. Hertz means cycles per second. The number is exact by definition. A real instrument still has uncertainty because fields, motion, temperature, electronics and measurement conditions can affect its output. The atomic transition provides a common target; engineering determines how closely a clock reaches it. [3] [4]

Advanced cesium fountain clocks cool atoms with lasers and let them move through a microwave measurement region for longer. This improves frequency measurement. Optical clocks use much higher frequencies from transitions in atoms such as strontium or ytterbium. They can achieve exceptional precision, and international bodies are studying a future redefinition of the second. That is a separate project from removing leap seconds. [4]

Accuracy means closeness to the intended standard. Stability means how consistently the clock runs over a chosen interval. A clock can be stable but offset from the correct time. It can also be accurate on average but noisy in the short term. Time laboratories compare many clocks to detect and reduce such weaknesses.

4. How UTC is made: no single master clock

UTC is an international calculation, not the reading on one machine in a secret room. The International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM) combines data from roughly 80 laboratories and 450 atomic clocks worldwide. Laboratories maintain local real-time time scales called UTC(k), where the letter identifies the laboratory. They compare clocks through calibrated links, including satellite-based methods. [5]

The BIPM computes International Atomic Time (TAI), a continuous atomic scale. UTC has the same rate as TAI but, under the historical leap-second system, differs by a whole number of seconds. BIPM publishes comparison results monthly in Circular T. It also produces a faster weekly estimate called UTCr. This explains a subtle point: the definitive international UTC calculation is retrospective. Real users receive a laboratory's best real-time approximation, not a live signal directly from a monthly calculation. [5] [6]

Redundancy matters. A single clock can fail or drift. A comparison network can detect disagreement, assign weights, calibrate links and identify abnormal behavior. Satellite signals can help laboratories compare clocks over long distances, but the satellite system itself also requires careful timing. BIPM documented the first official use of Galileo measurements in one UTC comparison in June 2023. [7]

5. Why Earth's rotation cannot supply a perfectly steady second

Earth's rotation is a physical process influenced by its atmosphere, oceans, interior and the gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun. Some effects change the length of a day over short periods. Others operate over centuries. Earth therefore cannot provide the same repeatable interval as an atomic transition.

UT1 is a time scale connected to the actual rotation angle of Earth. Observers use radio astronomy, satellite techniques and geodesy to estimate it. The difference UT1 − UTC tells us how far the civil reference has moved from rotation-based time. This difference is separate from TAI − UTC, which records the accumulated whole-second offset from leap seconds.

These two offsets are easy to confuse. In October 2026, TAI is 37 seconds ahead of UTC according to IERS Bulletin C 72. That does not mean the Sun is 37 seconds away from the clock. Under the existing leap-second rule, the difference between UT1 and UTC is controlled near one second. The two comparisons answer different questions. [2]

Solar noon is also affected by geography and Earth's orbital motion. A country can set a time zone far from its local apparent solar time. Daylight-saving rules can move civil clocks by an hour in a single night. The new UTC policy considers that social clock time already differs from solar time for many reasons. This does not remove the value of UT1 for scientific work. It changes whether the global reference itself must make abrupt adjustments to stay close to UT1.

6. What a leap second does to a computer

A positive leap second adds one labeled second at the end of a UTC day. A sequence can read 23:59:59 → 23:59:60 → 00:00:00. A negative leap second would skip a label, but none has been introduced so far. The last positive leap second occurred at the end of December 2016. IERS Bulletin C 72 says no leap second will be inserted at the end of December 2026. [2] [8]

Why is one second difficult? Software often treats a timestamp as a fixed-format value with seconds numbered 00 to 59. Some programming libraries do not represent 60. Some devices step backward or repeat a label when they convert the leap to their internal representation. In a distributed system, two servers can then disagree about event order. This is especially troublesome when logs, payments or safety systems assume time always moves forward.

Engineers developed different responses. Some apply the extra second as an abrupt adjustment. Others slow the clock slightly over a period called a leap smear. A smear avoids a sudden step, but during the smear its clock is intentionally offset from strict UTC. Two systems using different smear methods can disagree even when both claim to be synchronized. Google documents a 24-hour smear used by its public time service and warns against mixing smeared and unsmeared sources. [9]

The problem is therefore more precise than saying leap seconds always break computers. Correctly designed systems can handle them. The operational risk comes from inconsistent implementations, mixed time scales and the need to maintain rare exceptional behavior across a very large installed base.

7. What the world decided on 15 October 2026

The 2022 General Conference on Weights and Measures had already agreed to widen the allowed separation between UTC and UT1 in or before 2035. It asked for technical work on a continuous scale. The October 2026 conference made the timetable specific. Its Resolution 5 sets 20 May 2027 as the effective date for continuous UTC and 3,600 seconds as the maximum permitted absolute difference between UT1 and UTC. [1] [10]

This is a change to the policy for UTC adjustments. It is not a redefinition of the SI second. It is not a demand that everyone change their local time zone. It does not add or subtract 3,600 seconds in May 2027. The larger bound permits a slow difference to accumulate while keeping UTC continuous. The resolution says the new arrangement should maintain continuity for several centuries, while organizations continue to monitor Earth rotation.

The resolution identifies several reasons for the earlier start: infrastructure needs continuous timestamps; distributed sensors and smart grids increase the cost of a rare clock step; a future negative leap second could be especially difficult; and different providers already use inconsistent ways to soften leap seconds. The document also recognizes that astronomers and other users can obtain UT1 − UTC information separately from specialized services. [1]

There is an important distinction between a legal or scientific decision and a completed technical migration. The decision has been adopted. Operators still need to check the way their time servers, software libraries, data formats and external partners interpret leap-second notices. Specific products may update on different schedules. The primary resolution establishes the rule; it does not certify every installed system.

8. Two worked examples

Example A: what a 24-hour smear changes

Assume, for illustration, a positive leap second must be handled by a system that spreads the adjustment uniformly over 24 hours. A day has 24 × 60 × 60 = 86,400 ordinary seconds. Spreading one extra second across that period changes the clock rate by roughly 1 / 86,400 = 0.00001157, or 11.57 parts per million. That is about 11.57 microseconds per indicated second. A clock can then avoid a sudden step, but it differs from strict UTC during the adjustment. A smear centered on the leap can have an offset near half a second around the event. Google's published explanation gives the details. [9]

This calculation does not mean an extra leap second is scheduled. It shows why different software designs can produce different readings during a leap event. The engineering requirement is agreement on the time scale and on the conversion method.

Example B: why a 37-second offset is not an error

Assume an event is labeled 12:00:00 UTC on a date in October 2026. Under the currently published offset, the same instant is 12:00:37 TAI. Both labels can be correct. The clocks count seconds at the same rate but use different origins because UTC has included leap-second adjustments. The 37 seconds are a difference between two defined time scales. They are not the uncertainty of the clock, and they are not the difference between civil noon and the Sun. [2]

In a system that records TAI internally but displays UTC, the software must apply the correct offset for the date. Applying today's offset to historical data can produce mistakes because the TAI–UTC difference changed when earlier leap seconds were introduced. This is a data-model problem as much as a clock problem.

9. How accurate time reaches a phone, server or satellite receiver

A laboratory's reference time does not travel directly into every device. Time distribution uses several layers. Satellite navigation systems transmit precisely timed radio signals. Receivers compare the arrival times to estimate position and can also produce a timing output. Network Time Protocol (NTP) lets computers adjust their clocks using time servers. More specialized networks use methods such as Precision Time Protocol when tighter local synchronization is required.

Each layer introduces uncertainty. Signals take time to travel. Network paths may have different delays in each direction. A receiver's local oscillator can drift between updates. Engineers estimate delays, monitor offsets and apply corrections rather than assuming a received timestamp is perfect. NIST notes that GPS signals derive from onboard atomic frequency standards and are widely used for time and frequency comparison. [11]

Time is also affected by relativity. Clocks moving at different speeds or in different gravitational conditions do not always accumulate time at the same rate. Satellite systems account for such effects. The important operational lesson is that a timestamp is the result of a physical clock, a reference scale, a transmission path and software that interprets the data. A highly accurate clock alone cannot guarantee a reliable end-to-end system.

For an application designer, a robust approach is to separate elapsed duration from wall-clock labels. Measure intervals with a monotonic clock, which is designed not to jump backward. Store event times with an explicit time scale and offset when appropriate. Use UTC for cross-system civil timestamps, and use UT1 only when actual Earth orientation is required. Test conversion code at unusual boundaries, even if those boundaries are becoming less common.

10. The strongest cases on both sides

Case for continuous UTC: make the reference predictable

Critical infrastructure benefits when a second always follows the previous second without an inserted or skipped label. An uninterrupted reference reduces the number of rare states that software must support. It also makes time distribution more consistent across services. The case is strongest for networks that coordinate many machines and for systems where ordering errors can have large consequences. The 2026 resolution explicitly identifies transport, power, telecom, space systems, sensors and virtual services as reasons to act. [1]

Supporters also argue that civil time already includes large policy choices. Time zones and daylight-saving rules can separate local clock time from the Sun by much more than a second. A slowly changing difference between UTC and UT1 is manageable if scientific users can obtain accurate UT1 corrections separately. This separates two requirements that do not need the same technical solution.

Case for close Earth alignment: preserve a useful common meaning

The historical UTC design had a real benefit. A civil timestamp was also a close approximation to Earth's rotational time. That made some astronomical, navigation and geophysical tasks simpler, especially where equipment assumed a small UT1 − UTC difference. Removing the strict one-second bound can require those users to fetch and apply a correction explicitly. Some legacy instruments and formats may need updates. NIST's leap-second guidance describes this trade-off. [8]

A second concern is institutional: once a reference standard changes, all participants need a common interpretation. Poor coordination could create inconsistent conversion rules during the transition. A continuous UTC standard reduces future leap-second problems, but it does not automatically repair past data or remove the need to track multiple time scales. This is a serious implementation challenge, not an argument that atomic time is inaccurate.

The balance of evidence is practical rather than ideological. The 2026 international decision favors continuous UTC because broad infrastructure costs now outweigh the benefit of forcing the global civil reference to remain within one second of Earth rotation. Specialist Earth-rotation measurements remain available.

11. What changes next—and what remains open

Most people will notice nothing on 20 May 2027. Their phone will still show local time, and a second will still be a second. The immediate work is behind the screen. Time-service operators must confirm how their software interprets leap indicators, historical offset tables and time-scale conversions. Organizations that use leap smears must understand what happens when no new leap second is scheduled.

Engineers should avoid a false assumption: eliminating future UTC leap-second steps does not mean every time source is identical. GPS-related time scales, TAI, UTC, UT1 and local civil time serve different purposes. Time-zone rules can change through legislation. Old records can contain leap seconds. Network delays and clock drift still exist. The new rule removes one category of discontinuity from the international reference; it does not remove every source of timing error.

For scientific work, the critical task is to preserve easy access to accurate UT1 − UTC data. The 2026 resolution asks the BIPM and partner organizations to keep monitoring the difference and to support users. A larger allowed difference creates more room before an adjustment would be required, but it also increases the importance of recording which time scale a measurement uses.

Several questions remain. How will each operating system and network-time implementation announce and adopt the 2027 change? Will legacy software continue to expect a leap second every time an IERS bulletin arrives? How will long-lived scientific archives record historical UTC and UT1 conversions? How will standards groups document future behavior if Earth's rotation changes in unexpected ways? The resolution sets the direction; technical coordination will determine the quality of the transition.

There is a larger lesson about standards. A standard can be physically exact and still depend on social agreement. The atomic definition of the second rests on measurable physics. The choice to keep civil time close to Earth's rotation is a policy rule. Both are legitimate parts of timekeeping, but they should be explained separately.

12. Key lessons

  1. The SI second measures duration using a specified cesium atomic transition.
  2. Earth rotation varies, so a solar day is not an ideal fixed unit of duration.
  3. TAI is continuous atomic time; UTC has historically used leap seconds to stay close to UT1.
  4. UTC is computed from an international network of laboratories and clocks, then realized locally for real-time users.
  5. A leap second can introduce an unusual timestamp that some software cannot handle consistently.
  6. A leap smear avoids an abrupt clock step but creates a temporary difference from strict UTC.
  7. On 15 October 2026, the CGPM adopted continuous UTC effective 20 May 2027.
  8. The new allowed |UT1 − UTC| difference is 3,600 seconds; this is a limit, not a scheduled one-hour clock jump.
  9. Scientific users can still obtain UT1 corrections, while most civil systems benefit from a continuous reference.
  10. Good system design records time scales explicitly and separates elapsed duration from wall-clock time.

13. Knowledge check

1. Why is a cesium atom a better reference for the SI second than Earth's rotation?

Answer: The specified atomic transition has a reproducible frequency. Earth's rotation changes slightly because of physical processes, so it cannot define an equally stable duration.

2. What is the difference between TAI − UTC and UT1 − UTC?

Answer: TAI − UTC is the whole-second offset between continuous atomic time and UTC. UT1 − UTC measures how UTC differs from time based on Earth's rotation. The 37-second TAI offset is not a 37-second solar-time error.

3. Why can two servers disagree during a leap smear?

Answer: A smeared clock changes rate temporarily. A server using strict UTC or a different smear period can show a different timestamp during the adjustment.

4. What exactly becomes effective on 20 May 2027?

Answer: Continuous UTC under CGPM Resolution 5, with an allowed absolute UT1 − UTC difference of up to 3,600 seconds. It does not mean a one-hour jump occurs on that date.

5. Why does a telescope operator still need UT1?

Answer: Pointing and Earth-orientation calculations depend on Earth's actual rotation. A continuous civil time scale alone does not encode that orientation; the operator must use the measured UT1 correction.
