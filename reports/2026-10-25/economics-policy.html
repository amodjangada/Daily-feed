Why Central Banks Pay Interest on Reserves

How can a tool for controlling inflation create losses on a central bank’s balance sheet—and what does that mean for taxpayers?

Executive summary

Central banks set interest rates to influence inflation and economic activity. In modern systems they often pay interest on commercial banks’ reserve balances. A reserve is a deposit that a bank holds at the central bank to settle payments and meet liquidity needs. Paying interest helps the central bank keep short-term market rates near its chosen policy rate, even when the banking system holds large reserves. But this method has a fiscal consequence. When policy rates rise, interest paid on reserves can increase quickly while income from older, fixed-rate bonds changes slowly. Central-bank profits can fall, and some banks report losses. This does not mean the central bank has run out of money or must stop fighting inflation. It does mean that public finances, bank earnings and the distribution of monetary-policy costs deserve close attention. This guide explains how reserves arise, why interest is paid, what bond purchases change, how losses reach government budgets, and which policy alternatives are credible. It separates accounting losses from economic costs and uses clearly illustrative numbers to show why the same balance sheet can produce profits in one interest-rate environment and losses in another.

1. Background: from scarce reserves to abundant reserves

A central bank issues the settlement asset used by commercial banks. In the United States this asset is a reserve balance at the Federal Reserve. If a customer of Bank A pays a customer of Bank B, Bank A normally transfers reserves to Bank B. Households do not hold reserve accounts directly. A commercial-bank deposit is a claim on a commercial bank; a reserve balance is a claim on the central bank. This distinction matters because the public cannot simply transfer its ordinary savings into a reserve account.

Before the global financial crisis, several central banks kept reserve balances relatively scarce. They could influence the overnight interbank interest rate by making small changes in reserve supply. When banks needed a scarce settlement asset, the price of borrowing it responded strongly to supply. That arrangement required close daily management of market conditions. It also depended on relatively predictable demand for reserves.

During the 2008 crisis and later the pandemic, central banks bought large amounts of government and other eligible securities. These purchases were part of quantitative easing, or QE: a policy intended to ease financial conditions when conventional interest-rate cuts had limited room. Bond purchases created reserve balances as a payment mechanism. Banks then held far more reserves than in the old scarce-reserves system. Central banks needed a reliable way to raise market interest rates without first selling all those securities.

Paying interest on reserves provided that method. In the United States, Congress authorised reserve remuneration in legislation that took effect in 2008. The Federal Reserve now describes its ample-reserves framework as one in which the rate paid on reserves is a main instrument for controlling short-term interest rates. The Bank of England and European Central Bank use related, though institutionally different, systems. There is no single universal operating framework, and the exact interest-bearing liabilities differ across jurisdictions.

2. Terms that make the accounting clear

A policy rate is the short-term interest rate the central bank seeks to influence. Reserve remuneration means interest paid by a central bank on qualifying reserve balances. The interest-on-reserve-balances rate in the United States is often called IORB. The overnight rate is the price of borrowing funds for one day. The spread is the difference between two rates, such as the interest earned on a bond and the interest paid on reserves.

A central-bank balance sheet records assets and liabilities. Government bonds held by the central bank are assets because they promise future payments. Reserve balances are liabilities because they are amounts owed to commercial banks. Banknotes are also central-bank liabilities, but they usually pay no interest. Equity is the residual difference between recorded assets and liabilities. Its accounting meaning does not map perfectly onto the solvency of a commercial business.

Quantitative tightening, or QT, reduces a central bank's securities holdings, commonly by allowing bonds to mature without full reinvestment or by selling them. A remittance is a transfer of a central bank's surplus earnings to its government under the applicable law. A deferred asset in Federal Reserve accounting records earnings that must be recovered before normal remittances resume. These terms describe distinct flows; they should not be treated as interchangeable.

3. Mechanism: why interest on reserves guides market rates

Stage one is the settlement need. Banks need reserves to make final payments and satisfy liquidity demands. A bank that has more reserves than it wants can try to lend or deploy its surplus, while a bank with fewer reserves may borrow. In an ample-reserves system, the banking sector as a whole holds enough reserves that small changes in supply do not greatly move the overnight rate.

Stage two is the outside option. If an eligible bank can earn interest at the central bank, it will normally resist lending comparable funds at a materially lower rate, subject to market frictions, regulation and access differences. The reserve rate therefore anchors many short-term rates. It is not a perfect floor for every borrower or lender. Some money-market institutions cannot earn IORB directly, and balance-sheet costs can cause market rates to trade below it.

Stage three is transmission. When the central bank raises reserve remuneration, the opportunity cost of lending money cheaply rises for banks. Money-market rates tend to follow. Banks and investors then reassess lending, bond prices, exchange rates and spending plans. These channels influence demand and inflation with uncertain delays. The central bank can tighten monetary policy while maintaining a large quantity of reserves.

Stage four is the accounting effect. A central bank may own a bond that pays a fixed coupon for many years, while its reserve liability reprices almost immediately when policy changes. If the interest rate on reserves rises above the return on much of its securities portfolio, net interest income falls. This is a maturity mismatch: the timing of changes in asset income differs from that of liability costs.

Stage five is the public-finance link. When earnings are positive, some central banks remit profits to government. Lower earnings reduce those remittances. Some jurisdictions have explicit government guarantees that require cash transfers when a purchase programme loses money. Others record losses for later recovery. The fiscal effect depends on local law and accounting; a reported loss is not automatically an immediate tax bill.

4. Visual model: the flow of reserves and income

The phrase 'money printing' can hide the important details. A purchase changes who holds the government bond and what form of central-bank liability finances it. It does not by itself cancel the bond, erase government debt, or guarantee that consumer spending rises in proportion to reserves. Banks cannot collectively dispose of reserves merely by lending to households; loans create deposits, while reserves shift among banks unless the central bank or other balance-sheet operations remove them.

Interest on reserves is therefore a policy implementation tool, not a reward paid simply because banks choose to save rather than lend. Its public cost still deserves examination. The key question is whether the monetary system can maintain rate control and financial stability at lower overall cost, considering transition risks.

5. Worked example: why the same portfolio can switch from profit to loss

Illustrative example only. These figures are invented for teaching. They are not an estimate of any central bank's actual portfolio or future losses.

Assume a central bank holds 100 billion units of fixed-rate bonds yielding 2% a year. For simplicity, it funds the entire portfolio with 100 billion units of interest-bearing reserves. Ignore operating costs, capital gains, banknotes and other assets. The bonds generate 2 billion units of annual interest income. At a reserve rate of 0.5%, interest expense is 0.5 billion, so net interest income is plus 1.5 billion.

Now suppose the central bank raises the reserve rate to 4% while the bonds still pay 2%. Annual reserve expense becomes 4 billion. The same portfolio now produces a net interest loss of 2 billion. The bonds have not necessarily defaulted. Their fixed income has simply become lower than the floating cost of the liabilities used to finance them. If reserves decline or older bonds mature and are replaced by higher-yielding bonds, the gap may narrow. The path depends on policy rates, reinvestment and the balance-sheet mix.

Notice a second effect. A rise in market yields also lowers the market value of existing fixed-rate bonds. Whether this appears immediately in reported earnings depends on accounting and whether the bonds are sold. A realised loss on a sale and an unrealised change in market value are different events. For sound analysis, track interest flows, realised gains or losses, and valuation changes separately.

6. Evidence from the United States and United Kingdom

The Federal Reserve's September 2025 IORB FAQ states that interest on reserve balances is central to rate control in its ample-reserves framework. It also explains why a rapid removal of reserve interest could cause major market disruption if the Fed tried to restore scarce reserves through rapid securities sales. This is an institutional assessment by the central bank itself; it explains the operating design but should not be mistaken for independent proof that no alternative could ever work.

The Federal Reserve's 2024 annual financial information describes deferred assets arising when earnings are insufficient for expenses and statutory distributions. Its statistical tables show a consolidated deferred asset associated with Treasury remittances of about $216 billion at the end of 2024, compared with about $133 billion a year earlier. A deferred asset is not an ordinary asset that a private buyer can readily purchase. It represents future earnings that will be retained before normal transfers to the Treasury resume. The exact accounting treatment is specific to the Federal Reserve.

The United Kingdom offers a different fiscal arrangement. The Bank of England's Asset Purchase Facility, or APF, bought bonds as part of QE. The UK Treasury indemnified the APF, which means the Treasury agreed to cover specified losses and receive specified gains. A written answer in the UK Parliament on 14 April 2025 reported £124 billion transferred from the APF to the Treasury between 2012 and 2022, and £85.9 billion transferred in the opposite direction since October 2022 through the date described in that answer. These are cash-flow figures for specified periods, not a complete measure of the macroeconomic benefits or lifetime net costs of QE.

The UK's Office for Budget Responsibility has explained that higher reserve interest and lower returns on existing bonds can reverse the earlier income gains from QE. HM Treasury's 2024–25 annual accounts explain why the indemnity can require cash payments when bond sale or redemption proceeds fall below acquisition funding or when the policy rate exceeds portfolio coupon income. These official sources help distinguish an accounting result from the timing of public cash flows.

A Bank for International Settlements bulletin published in February 2023 finds that central-bank losses and even negative accounting equity do not directly prevent central banks from operating. Its authors stress that policy institutions should be judged by their ability to meet mandates. This conclusion does not make fiscal costs irrelevant; it says that the operating capacity of a currency-issuing central bank differs from that of a normal firm.

7. Why losses matter even when the bank can keep operating

The first concern is the government budget. If a central bank sends less income to the Treasury, the Treasury has less revenue than it would otherwise have received. Where a formal indemnity exists, the government may make cash payments. Both can change fiscal forecasts, borrowing needs and political debate. The net effect must be evaluated together with any lower borrowing costs, crisis prevention or economic support that the earlier policy provided.

The second concern is political legitimacy. Interest paid to commercial banks can be large and visible during a period of high policy rates. It may look unfair when households face expensive mortgages or public budgets are constrained. Yet bank shareholders do not automatically receive every unit of reserve interest as profit. Deposit pricing, bank funding costs, regulation, competition and taxes affect the distribution. A serious distributional analysis must measure those channels rather than equating gross reserve interest with net bank profit.

The third concern is independence. Central banks sometimes need to tighten policy even when doing so reduces their own earnings. If political authorities pressure them to avoid losses, inflation control could suffer. Conversely, independence does not remove the duty to explain public costs and evaluate the design of policy tools. Transparent accounting and credible institutional boundaries support both goals.

The fourth concern is risk management. A central bank with large fixed-rate assets and floating-rate liabilities faces duration risk. The risk can be understood in advance, even if the exact future path of interest rates cannot. Central-bank balance sheets also contain currency, credit and liquidity risks, depending on jurisdiction. Comparing one year's reported profit across countries without adjusting for these differences is misleading.

8. Competing policy choices

One position is to keep full remuneration of reserves and focus on a clear operating framework. Supporters argue that it maintains predictable control of short-term rates, allows banks to hold ample liquidity, and avoids forcing abrupt bond sales. The Fed's published explanation strongly supports this position for the United States' current framework. It is especially relevant when the reserve system has already become large and a sudden change could unsettle money markets.

Another position is to use a tiered system. Under tiering, a central bank pays different rates on different amounts of reserves. For example, it might remunerate a core balance at one rate and additional balances at another. Tiering can reduce the central bank's direct interest bill, but it also changes banks' incentives. If the lower-paying tier is large, banks may try to shift balances or adjust money-market activity. The central bank must still keep the overnight rate near its target. Distributional effects across banks with different reserve holdings can be substantial.

A third position is to shrink the balance sheet more quickly and return toward scarce reserves. This could reduce interest-bearing liabilities over time. The costs are transition risks, market pressure from bond sales, possible realised losses and the need to rebuild the old fine-tuning system. The best pace depends on market functioning and demand for reserves. A rapid move that undermines rate control could be more costly than the interest savings.

A fourth position emphasises the fiscal perimeter. Some analysts argue that policymakers should evaluate the central bank and Treasury together, since the public sector ultimately bears much of the interest-rate risk. That view helps identify the true economic exposure. However, legal cash flows, budget rules and institutional independence still matter. Consolidation is a useful analytical method, not permission to ignore who must make a payment and when.

No credible option removes every cost. A good comparison asks what happens to monetary control, bank liquidity, market volatility, fiscal flows, distribution and public trust under the same economic conditions.

9. Worked example: a tiered-reserve proposal

Illustrative policy comparison. The rates and amounts below are invented. The example ignores market adjustments and should not be read as a forecast of fiscal savings.

Suppose total reserve balances equal 80 billion units and the policy-linked remuneration rate is 4%. Paying 4% on all reserves costs 3.2 billion per year. Now imagine that 20 billion units receive 4% and the remaining 60 billion receive 1%. The direct arithmetic cost falls to 1.4 billion: 0.8 billion on the first tier plus 0.6 billion on the second. The simple annual difference is 1.8 billion.

That arithmetic is only the beginning. Banks may change their demand for reserves, seek other short-term assets, alter deposit rates or trade with institutions facing different access rules. If the lower rate weakens the policy-rate floor, the central bank may need other tools or pay higher rates elsewhere. A government might also face higher bond yields if market conditions deteriorate. The direct saving therefore cannot be equated with a net public benefit without a model of the full financial system.

Tiering also raises a fairness question: which banks receive the high-paying allocation? A rule based on size, historical reserves or payment needs will create different winners. Policymakers must consider competition and incentives as well as the aggregate interest bill.

10. What this changes in public-policy debates

The issue connects monetary policy to fiscal policy more closely than simple textbook accounts suggest. A government bond can be held by a household, pension fund, bank or central bank. Each holder faces different risks and receives different payments. When a central bank finances bond purchases with remunerated reserves, part of the public sector's financing cost becomes more sensitive to short-term policy rates. This does not mean every unit of government debt becomes overnight debt, because currency and other liabilities remain important.

For citizens, the useful question is not whether a central bank made a profit last year. It is whether its policy helped deliver stable prices, resilient payments and sustainable economic activity at reasonable long-run cost. The same action can produce a near-term accounting loss while reducing the damage from a crisis. The opposite is also possible: positive income does not prove a policy was wise.

For investors and businesses, reserve remuneration helps explain why short-term market rates can rise quickly when policy changes. It also clarifies why banks may change deposit and loan pricing at different speeds. For legislators, it suggests three demands: disclose interest-rate risk before a policy is adopted, publish understandable cash-flow and accounting measures, and compare alternatives against credible counterfactuals.

The biggest unresolved question is how large a central-bank balance sheet should be in normal times. More reserves can support liquidity and robust payments; fewer reserves can reduce certain rate-sensitive liabilities. The right answer depends on the financial system's structure, the desired degree of resilience and the tools available to control rates.

11. Key lessons

  1. Reserves are commercial banks' settlement balances at the central bank; households cannot hold them directly.
  2. Interest on reserves is a key tool for controlling short-term rates in an ample-reserves system.
  3. QE purchases add securities to central-bank assets and can add reserves to liabilities.
  4. Fixed-rate assets funded by floating-rate liabilities create interest-rate risk.
  5. Central-bank losses can reduce Treasury remittances or trigger indemnity payments, depending on the country.
  6. An accounting loss does not by itself stop a currency-issuing central bank from operating.
  7. Gross reserve interest is different from commercial banks' net profits.
  8. Tiering and faster balance-sheet reduction may save direct interest expense but create other risks.
  9. Policy evaluation should include inflation, market stability, distribution and long-run fiscal effects.

12. Knowledge check: five questions and answers

1. Why does paying interest on reserves help a central bank raise market rates?

It raises the return eligible banks can earn without lending to another bank, increasing their minimum acceptable rate in many short-term markets. Frictions and access limits mean it is an anchor, not an absolute floor for all rates.

2. Why can a bond portfolio become loss-making without a bond default?

Its fixed coupon may remain low while the interest rate paid on the reserve liabilities financing it rises. The difference reduces net interest income.

3. Is a Federal Reserve deferred asset the same as a Treasury cash payment?

No. It records earnings to be recovered before normal remittances resume. Cash payments under a government indemnity, such as the UK's APF arrangement, are a different mechanism.

4. Does a central-bank accounting loss prove QE was a mistake?

No. Evaluation also requires the economic benefits, crisis conditions, alternatives and total public cost. Losses are evidence of financial exposure, not a complete welfare assessment.

5. What is the main risk of reducing reserve remuneration through tiering?

Lower remuneration can change banks' incentives and weaken rate control unless the system is carefully designed. Savings must be compared with financial-market and distributional effects.
