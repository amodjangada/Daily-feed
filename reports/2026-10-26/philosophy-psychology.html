How Social Norms Change Behavior—and When They Fail

Why do people follow group expectations, and why do some social messages make behavior worse?

Research checked 26 October 2026 · Worked examples are illustrative

Executive summary

People often change what they do after learning what others do or approve of. These shared expectations are called social norms. They help groups coordinate, but they can also preserve harmful practices or spread mistakes. Researchers distinguish descriptive norms, which concern typical behavior, from injunctive norms, which concern approval and disapproval. A message about what most people do can change behavior, yet it may also push people who already behave well toward a worse average. Norms depend on who counts as the relevant group, which signals people notice, and whether they trust the information. Field experiments on household energy use, hotel towel reuse, social attitudes, and workplace choices show both promise and limits. A replication of a famous littering result also found that one influential detail did not hold up as expected. This guide explains how people learn norms, why private beliefs and public behavior can differ, and how norms sometimes shift quickly. It also shows how to test a norm-based intervention without confusing persuasion, social pressure, and lasting change.

1. What is a social norm?

A social norm is a shared expectation about behavior in a group. It can describe what people usually do, what they think others should do, or both. Norms help people coordinate when formal rules are incomplete. They influence how people greet one another, share space, discuss disagreement and decide whether to help someone.

A descriptive norm is a belief about what most people do. An injunctive norm is a belief about what others approve or disapprove of. These can point in different directions. A person may believe that many people litter but also that most people disapprove of littering. A message that highlights common littering can unintentionally make littering seem normal.

A norm is different from a personal value, a habit or a law. A personal value is what an individual considers important. A habit is a learned response that can start automatically in a familiar situation. A law is a formal rule backed by institutions. Norms can support laws or conflict with them. A behavior may be legal but socially disapproved, or illegal yet common in a particular setting.

The group matters. A person may follow one norm among close friends and another at work. People do not react equally to every group. They pay more attention to groups they identify with, depend on, or expect to meet again. This helps explain why a message about distant strangers may have little effect even when its statistics are accurate.

2. How researchers began to study norms

Early social psychologists studied how people adjust judgments and actions in groups. Later work separated different kinds of social influence. One reason for following others is informational: their behavior may suggest what is correct in an uncertain situation. Another is normative: agreement may avoid rejection or signal membership. These reasons can operate together.

Cialdini, Reno and Kallgren's 1990 research developed a focus theory of normative conduct. The authors argued that a norm has greater influence when attention is directed toward it. Their field studies of littering distinguished messages about what people do from messages about what they approve [1]. The framework helped explain why a crowded, littered space can send a different signal from a clean one.

Later field studies moved beyond laboratory judgments. Researchers tested norm messages in hotels, homes and communities. Goldstein, Cialdini and Griskevicius reported that hotel towel-reuse messages describing the behavior of other guests performed better than a standard environmental appeal in their tested settings [3]. Schultz and colleagues found both beneficial and harmful effects of information about neighborhood electricity use [2].

The field has also become more careful about generalization. A 2021 replication of parts of the original littering research reproduced the broad finding that clean settings discouraged littering, but failed to reproduce the predicted effect of a single piece of litter [8]. This is an important lesson: a useful theory can survive while one specific experimental claim needs revision.

3. Stage one: people observe signals

People rarely count the actions of everyone around them. Instead, they infer what is normal from a small set of visible signals. These can include a friend's action, a public statement, a sign, a news story, or an institutional decision. The signals are uneven. Loud or frequent behavior can look more common than it is. Quiet approval may remain invisible.

Tankard and Paluck's 2016 review identifies individual behavior, summary information about groups, and institutional signals as important sources of perceived norms [7]. A workplace rule, for example, can communicate what the organization considers acceptable even before employees change their own views. But a rule that is rarely followed can also expose a gap between stated values and actual practice.

Attention is a key part of the process. The same person can see a behavior many times without thinking about its social meaning. A well-timed message can make the behavior relevant to a current choice. That is why signs placed at the moment of action may work differently from broad campaigns shown days earlier.

Signals can be misleading. A social media feed may overrepresent highly active users. A meeting may appear unanimous because dissenters stay silent. A business may display positive reviews while dissatisfied customers do not post. When people infer a norm from such samples, they may believe that a view is more common than it is. Correcting the information can change behavior, but only if people trust the correction and consider the reference group relevant.

4. Stage two: expectations become pressure

After noticing a signal, people form expectations. They may ask, often without stating it aloud: What will others do? What do they want me to do? What will happen if I act differently? These are separate questions. A person can believe that an action is common without thinking it is good. They can also believe that an action is approved even when few people perform it.

Social rewards and costs help sustain a norm. Approval, trust and belonging can reward conformity. Criticism, exclusion and embarrassment can discourage deviation. In many cases, no one needs to impose a formal penalty. The expectation of a response can affect the choice. But the strength of this pressure varies with the group, the visibility of the action and the person's dependence on others.

Norms also carry information. If most people choose one exit in an unfamiliar building, following them may seem sensible. The same tendency can spread an error if the crowd is mistaken. This means conformity is neither always irrational nor always wise. Its value depends on whether other people's actions provide reliable information.

A reference group is the group whose behavior or approval matters for a particular decision. A person may care about the practices of colleagues more than a national average. This helps explain why specific, local messages sometimes work better than broad statistics. Yet local messages can be misleading if they use a small or biased sample. A credible intervention must report true information and define the group clearly.

5. Stage three: behavior reinforces the expectation

Norms can become self-reinforcing. When people expect others to behave in a certain way, they may choose that behavior themselves. Their actions then provide more visible evidence that the behavior is normal. This feedback can maintain a convention even when many individuals privately prefer something else.

Researchers call one form of this problem pluralistic ignorance. People privately reject a practice but incorrectly assume that others support it. Because they fear standing out, they continue to conform. Their public behavior makes the mistaken belief look true. A correction that reveals wider private support for change can weaken the cycle.

Bursztyn, González and Yanagizawa-Drott studied perceived attitudes toward women working outside the home among young married men in Saudi Arabia. They found that men underestimated support among comparable men. In their experiments, correcting beliefs increased men's willingness to help their wives search for jobs, and later job-search outcomes changed [5]. The result is specific to the studied population and setting. It shows that inaccurate expectations can have real effects beyond survey responses.

Feedback can also preserve useful norms. When people see others keeping shared spaces clean, they may be more likely to do the same. The difficulty is that visible behavior can be both an outcome and a cause. A researcher must separate these roles before claiming that a message changed the norm rather than simply describing a trend already under way.

6. Diagram: the norm feedback loop

The loop helps explain why a campaign can have effects beyond the first people who see it. If some people change and others observe them, the new behavior may become more credible. But the same loop can spread a harmful practice. The direction depends on what the group notices and what people think others will reward.

Not every action feeds back equally. A private choice may have little effect on a visible norm. A prominent person's public behavior may affect many observers. A formal institution can change perceived approval without immediately changing actual conduct. These differences matter when designing and evaluating interventions.

7. Why a true norm message can backfire

Suppose a household uses less electricity than most neighbors. A letter says that the neighborhood average is higher than the household's own use. The letter is factually correct. Yet it may suggest that higher consumption is normal and acceptable. The household might then increase use. This is a boomerang effect: a message intended to improve behavior causes movement in the wrong direction for some people.

Schultz and colleagues tested descriptive norm feedback about residential electricity consumption. Higher-consuming households reduced use after seeing the neighborhood average, while lower-consuming households could move upward. Adding an injunctive signal of approval for low use removed the observed boomerang effect in their experiment [2]. The result illustrates why “what most people do” and “what people approve” should not be treated as the same message.

The practical lesson is to check where the target person starts. A statement such as “many people ignore this rule” may normalize the very conduct a campaign wants to reduce. A message about an improving trend can sometimes avoid this problem, but only when the trend is real and meaningful to the audience.

The broader evidence is mixed across settings. Some norm messages work well; others have small effects, no effect, or unintended results. The intervention's wording, the target group and the outcome measure all matter. It is unsafe to assume that social proof always persuades or that a single experiment supplies a universal script.

8. What field experiments reveal

Laboratory studies can isolate a mechanism, but field experiments show whether it survives ordinary distractions and real choices. The hotel towel study compared different signs in actual guest rooms. Messages describing reuse by other guests performed better than a general environmental appeal, with especially strong results for a closely matched reference group in the tested setting [3]. This does not prove that the same message works for every hotel or behavior.

Paluck's field experiment in Rwanda studied a radio drama that presented ideas about prejudice, conflict and social behavior. Compared with a control program, the treatment changed perceptions of social norms and several reported behaviors, while showing limited change in private beliefs [4]. The finding is important because public behavior can change without a matching shift in every private attitude.

These studies have different strengths. A field setting improves practical relevance, but it can be harder to control every influence. A survey of perceived approval may capture beliefs without proving later action. A direct measure of electricity use or towel reuse captures behavior more closely, but it may say little about long-term motives.

Research should therefore report what was measured and for how long. Did people say they would change? Did they perform a one-time action? Did a repeated practice change? Did the effect persist after the message disappeared? Each answer supports a different claim. A short-term response is useful evidence, but it should not be described as permanent cultural change.

9. Worked example: a neighborhood energy message

Illustrative numbers, not a new experiment. Imagine a building with 100 apartments. The monthly average electricity use is 250 units. Apartment A uses 340 units. Apartment B uses 170 units. A message that says “your neighbors use 250 units on average” tells A that it is above the norm and B that it is below the norm.

If A wants to conform, it may reduce consumption. If B wants to conform, it may increase consumption. The same accurate statistic can move the two households in opposite directions. A more careful message could pair the comparison with truthful approval of low use and practical conservation steps. The actual effect would need to be tested; the example only shows the mechanism.

For evaluation, compare households randomly assigned to the message with similar households that do not receive it. Measure consumption before and after. Report results separately for initially high- and low-use households. Also check weather, occupancy and other factors that change electricity demand. Without a comparison group, a fall in use during cooler weather could be wrongly credited to the message.

Notice that a lower bill is not the same as lower energy use if prices change. Choose the measure that matches the claim. A study about energy conservation should track energy units, while a study about household cost should track spending and tariffs.

10. Worked example: changing a team norm

Illustrative workplace case. A company wants staff to report near-miss safety incidents. Managers privately support reporting, but workers believe colleagues will see it as disloyal. The company announces that reporting is encouraged. Few reports appear. Management concludes that employees do not care about safety.

There are several possible causes. Workers may not trust confidentiality. Reporting may take too long. Supervisors may punish people informally. Or employees may wrongly believe that peers disapprove. A norm-based intervention should first measure these beliefs and barriers separately. Asking only “Do you support safety?” would miss the difference between personal approval and perceived group approval.

Suppose an anonymous survey finds that most employees support reporting, while many underestimate their colleagues' support. Management can share the true aggregate result, show leaders responding fairly to reports, and make reporting simple. It should not invent a favorable statistic or identify individual respondents. A pilot can then compare teams or time periods while tracking reporting quality and actual safety outcomes.

More reports could mean better trust, more incidents, or both. The number of reports alone does not prove improved safety. The organization must also inspect the severity of incidents, corrective action and whether people feel safe raising concerns. Norm messages can support change, but they cannot replace fair systems and real incentives.

11. Can a small group change a large norm?

Norms sometimes seem stable until many people change quickly. One explanation is that individuals wait for enough others to support a new convention. Once a critical number acts, the perceived risk of switching falls. The change can then spread through a network. Researchers call the point where adoption begins to accelerate a tipping point.

Centola and colleagues studied an artificial social-convention task in which people received incentives to coordinate on a shared convention. They introduced committed minorities seeking to change it. The experiment found a tipping pattern, with a threshold near 25% in the studied conditions [6]. The authors also noted that the required threshold depends on features of the setting.

The 25% figure is often repeated as if it were a law of social change. It is not. Real groups differ in network structure, power, enforcement, identity, history and personal costs. A minority may need far more support to change a deeply enforced practice. In other settings, a small change by a respected institution may alter expectations quickly.

The useful insight is about coordination rather than a fixed percentage. People may support change privately but wait until they believe enough others will join. Visible, credible signals can help them update that belief. However, leaders should not treat an artificial coordination experiment as a direct forecast for political, cultural or workplace change.

12. Evidence, replication and uncertainty

The research supports several distinct claims. People notice information about what others do and approve. Some field experiments show that norm messages can change measurable behavior. Misperceived group support can prevent people from acting on private preferences. Institutional and public signals can change expectations [2] [5] [7].

The limits are just as important. A norm message may work only for a particular group, outcome or time period. Some studies measure short-term actions; others measure beliefs. These results should not be combined as if they measure the same thing. Researchers also face selection effects, spillovers between groups, and the possibility that a message changes how people answer surveys rather than how they act.

The 2021 littering replication is especially instructive. Bergquist and colleagues conducted two preregistered field experiments and an online study, with 1,798 participants in total. They replicated lower littering in clean settings compared with littered ones. But the original prediction about a single piece of litter did not replicate; adding one piece increased rather than decreased littering in their tests [8].

This does not erase the wider literature on norms. It shows that the details of attention, context and interpretation matter. A strong practical approach treats a norm message as a testable intervention. It measures actual behavior, reports failures, and updates the design when the evidence disagrees with the theory.

13. Competing views: information, pressure or institutions?

One view emphasizes information. People follow others because other people's behavior helps them decide what is sensible. This view predicts stronger influence when the situation is uncertain and the reference group appears informed. Another view emphasizes social pressure. People conform to avoid disapproval or maintain membership, even when they privately disagree. Both mechanisms can operate in the same setting.

A third view places more weight on institutions and incentives. A workplace may announce a new norm, but employees will judge whether managers actually reward or punish the behavior. A campaign that asks people to conserve energy may have limited effect when prices, equipment or housing conditions prevent change. This view does not deny social influence; it argues that material conditions can dominate it.

There is also disagreement about the durability of norm change. Some interventions shift an immediate choice but fade later. Others may alter expectations in a network and produce lasting effects. Evidence from one domain cannot settle the issue for every domain. The appropriate test follows the outcome over time and checks for unwanted effects.

Ethical concerns matter too. A true statement about peers can inform people. Selective statistics can manipulate them. Publishing a minority's behavior as if it were the majority's is deceptive. Norm campaigns should use accurate data, protect privacy and avoid shaming people who face constraints that the message ignores.

14. How to design a responsible norm intervention

First, define the exact behavior. “Improve culture” is too vague. “Increase accurate near-miss reporting” is measurable. Next, identify the relevant group and ask what people believe others do and approve. Measure private attitudes separately from perceived group attitudes. The difference may reveal a mistaken norm, but a survey must protect respondents from pressure to give acceptable answers.

Second, identify the barrier. If people already know the norm but lack time, money or permission, another message may not help. If the barrier is a false belief about group approval, truthful feedback may be useful. Choose the intervention that matches the mechanism. Avoid repeating how common the unwanted behavior is unless there is a clear reason.

Third, test before scaling. Random assignment can help isolate the message's effect. Measure actual behavior, not only intentions. Check whether people who already behave well move backward. Examine whether the effect differs across groups. Follow up after the message stops to see whether the change persists.

Finally, communicate results with limits. Report the comparison group, sample size, time period and outcomes. Do not describe a temporary increase in compliance as a permanent norm shift. If the intervention causes harm or unfair pressure, stop or revise it. The best use of social norms is to make genuine expectations visible while respecting individual choice.

15. Why social norms matter in daily life

Norms affect more than advertising or public campaigns. They influence whether people speak during meetings, ask for help, report mistakes, conserve shared resources and accept unfamiliar practices. A norm can reduce the need for constant enforcement when a group already agrees on fair behavior. It can also make change difficult when public conduct hides private disagreement.

For organizations, the key question is whether the stated norm matches what people observe. A policy that praises openness will not create openness if criticism is punished. For public institutions, accurate information about group behavior can support cooperation, but it should be paired with services and incentives that make the desired action possible.

For individuals, the useful lesson is to distinguish observation from approval. Seeing many people do something does not establish that it is good or that everyone supports it. Silence does not establish agreement. A person can seek better information about what others actually believe before deciding how much weight to give a social signal.

Social norms are powerful because people make decisions in groups. They are limited because people also respond to personal goals, resources, identity and institutions. Understanding both sides makes norm research useful without turning it into a simple formula for persuasion.

Key lessons

  1. A descriptive norm concerns what people do; an injunctive norm concerns what they approve.
  2. People infer norms from visible behavior, statistics and institutional signals.
  3. The relevant reference group matters more than an abstract population average.
  4. Public behavior can differ from private belief because of social pressure.
  5. Pluralistic ignorance can keep an unpopular practice in place.
  6. Norm messages can backfire when they normalize unwanted behavior.
  7. Field experiments should measure actions, not only stated intentions.
  8. A replication can support a broad theory while rejecting a specific result.
  9. There is no universal 25% threshold for social change.
  10. Accurate information and fair institutions matter more than clever wording alone.

Knowledge check: five questions with answers

1. How do descriptive and injunctive norms differ?

Descriptive norms concern what people commonly do. Injunctive norms concern what others approve or disapprove of.

2. What is pluralistic ignorance?

It occurs when people privately reject a practice but wrongly believe that others support it, so they continue to conform.

3. Why can a neighborhood-average message increase consumption?

A household already below the average may move upward toward what it sees as normal unless the message also supports conservation.

4. Does the 2018 tipping-point study prove that 25% can change any society?

No. It studied an artificial coordination setting. The required share varies with the social context and incentives.

5. What is a strong test of a norm campaign?

Use a credible comparison group, measure real behavior over time, examine unintended effects, and report limits and group differences.
