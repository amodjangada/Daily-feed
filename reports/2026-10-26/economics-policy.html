Who Really Pays a Tax? The Economics of Tax Incidence

If the law taxes a company, why might workers, buyers, and landowners carry part of the cost?

Executive summary

A tax law tells us who must send money to the government. It does not, by itself, tell us who loses purchasing power. Economists call the final distribution of that burden tax incidence. When a government taxes a product, buyers may pay a higher price, sellers may receive a lower price, and some trades may stop. How the burden is shared depends on how strongly buyers and sellers respond to price changes. The same principle applies to payroll taxes, business taxes, and property markets, but the details differ. Firms can adjust wages, profits, investment, and location. Research also shows that attention matters: a tax displayed in a posted price can change behavior differently from a tax added at checkout. Evidence from corporate tax studies remains contested because firms and workers move, and models must account for those changes. This guide explains the basic mechanics, the main evidence, and the limits of simple claims about who pays. It also shows why good tax policy must consider both revenue and household welfare.

1. Why the name on the tax bill is not the whole story

Every government needs revenue to provide services and pay obligations. A tax is one way to transfer purchasing power from private users to the public budget. Law specifies the taxpayer, the base being taxed, the rate, and how payment is collected. Economics asks a different question: after people change their choices, whose real income or consumption falls? This second question matters when evaluating fairness, business costs, inflation, and incentives. A shop may send sales tax to the treasury, but the shop can raise prices. An employer may remit payroll tax, but workers may accept lower wages over time. A company may pay corporate income tax, but investors, workers, and customers can all be affected.

Economists developed tax-incidence analysis from the study of supply and demand. Early public-finance work examined how prices adjust when taxes are placed on goods and factors of production. Modern research adds market power, search costs, mobility, incomplete information, and behavioral responses. It also studies whether the benefits of public spending compensate the people who pay. This report focuses on the cost side. The question of how governments use revenue is equally important for judging the whole policy, but it is a separate calculation.

The prior Daily Feed editions on government debt and interest on central-bank reserves explained how public obligations are financed and how money moves between the state and the financial system. This chapter follows the other side of public finance: how a given amount of tax revenue changes decisions and divides costs across people. It is relevant even when the budget is balanced. Two taxes that raise the same revenue can have very different effects on wages, prices, investment, and the distribution of living standards.

2. Essential terms before the calculation

Statutory incidence means the legal obligation to pay or remit a tax. Economic incidence means who ultimately bears its economic burden after prices, wages, profits, and choices adjust. The distinction is the foundation of the subject. Tax base means the activity or value to which the tax applies, such as sales, income, land value, or payroll. Tax wedge is the difference a tax creates between what a buyer pays and what a seller receives. For work, it can be the difference between an employer's total labor cost and the worker's take-home pay.

Elasticity measures how much quantity changes when price changes, usually as a percentage response to a one-percent price change. Demand is relatively inelastic when buyers have few substitutes or cannot quickly reduce use. Supply is relatively inelastic when sellers cannot easily change output or move resources. Pass-through is the share of a tax-related cost change that appears in the selling price. Pass-through is not necessarily the same as incidence, because quantity changes and other costs matter.

Deadweight loss is the value of mutually beneficial trades that no longer occur because of the tax, beyond the revenue transferred to government. Salience means how visible or noticeable a tax is to the person making a decision. Capitalization means that expected future costs or benefits are reflected in the present price of an asset, such as land. These terms help explain why a tax may be recorded in one place while its effect appears elsewhere.

3. The mechanism: how a tax changes a market

Start with a competitive market without tax. At a certain price, the amount consumers wish to buy equals the amount firms wish to sell. Now add a fixed tax per unit. A buyer must pay a price that is higher than the amount the seller keeps by exactly the tax. If the seller must remit it, the seller still asks whether producing one more unit is worth the net amount received. If the buyer remits it, the buyer still asks whether buying one more unit is worth the total cost. Under the standard model, these are the same two decisions.

The new equilibrium is found where the quantity buyers want at their total price equals the quantity sellers supply at their net price. In most ordinary cases, the buyer's price rises by less than the full tax, the seller's net price falls, and the quantity traded decreases. This adjustment is why the legal payer alone does not identify the final burden. The economic burden is measured relative to the prices and opportunities people would have had without the tax.

If buyers cannot easily reduce purchases, they accept more of the price increase. This tends to place a larger burden on buyers. If sellers cannot easily reduce production or move their resources elsewhere, they accept a lower net price, so more of the burden falls on sellers. The relevant elasticities are relative. Saying that demand is inelastic is incomplete without asking how elastic supply is. Time also matters: drivers may have few options next week but can buy a different vehicle or move closer to work over several years.

The result depends on assumptions. The familiar diagram treats products as similar, firms as price takers, and participants as aware of all prices. In real markets, large firms can set prices strategically. Contracts may prevent quick wage changes. Prices may be regulated. Consumers may overlook taxes added late in a purchase. A supply chain may cross borders. In these settings, we need richer models and observed data rather than a universal pass-through percentage.

4. Visual model: where the burden goes

The diagram follows the tax through prices and behavior. It is a conceptual map, not a measured allocation. The statutory payer appears at the top, but the burden is determined farther down. Note that a single tax can change more than one price and can reduce activity at the same time.

5. Worked example: a ten-rupee unit tax

Illustrative calculation, not market data. Suppose buyers demand Q = 120 − 2P, where P is their price, and sellers supply Q = 20 + 3P, where P is their received price. Without tax, setting demand equal to supply gives 120 − 2P = 20 + 3P. Therefore P = ₹20 and Q = 80 units. This simple model uses a single market and ignores external effects, transport costs, and income changes.

Introduce a ₹10 tax per unit. Let Pc be the buyer price and Pp the seller's net price. The tax creates Pc = Pp + 10. The market-clearing condition is 120 − 2(Pp + 10) = 20 + 3Pp. Solving gives Pp = ₹16, Pc = ₹26, and Q = 68. The buyer pays ₹6 more per unit. The seller receives ₹4 less per unit. Buyers bear 60% of the per-unit price wedge, and sellers bear 40%, in this model.

Government revenue equals ₹10 × 68 = ₹680. Twelve units that would have traded without tax no longer trade. With linear curves, the deadweight-loss triangle is ½ × ₹10 × 12 = ₹60. This ₹60 is not the same as the ₹680 transferred to government. Revenue can finance valuable public services; the lost trades are an additional efficiency cost in the model. The result is sensitive to the assumed slopes. Changing the responsiveness of buyers or sellers changes the burden split.

Now imagine that the law says buyers must send the ₹10 to government rather than sellers. If buyers and sellers see all prices and can adjust freely, the same equilibrium appears. The shop may post a lower pretax price, but buyers still compare the total ₹26 cost. This is the classic statutory-incidence invariance result. Later sections explain why it can fail when taxes are hard to see or institutions constrain adjustment.

6. When visibility changes the result

In a field experiment reported by Raj Chetty, Adam Looney, and Kory Kroft in the American Economic Review in September 2009, adding tax-inclusive price tags in a grocery store reduced demand for affected goods by about 8%. The researchers also compared changes in alcohol taxes that appeared in shelf prices with taxes added at the register. Consumers responded more strongly to taxes that were included in posted prices. This is evidence that some buyers do not fully account for taxes they see only later. It does not mean all shoppers behave the same way or that the exact effect applies to every country or product. [1]

Amy Finkelstein's August 2009 study of electronic toll collection found that tolls rose after payment became less noticeable. Her estimates imply that steady-state toll rates were roughly 20–40% higher than they would have been without electronic collection, under the study's model and comparison strategy. The finding supports a link between attention and political price setting, but it does not show that electronic payment itself is harmful. Electronic tolls can also save time and reduce congestion at booths. The policy question is whether the convenience is accompanied by clear price information and accountable rate setting. [2]

These studies change a familiar textbook lesson. When people do not notice all parts of the price, shifting the legal point of collection can change perceived prices and behavior. Chetty's 2009 theoretical work shows how welfare calculations must adapt when people make attention errors. A tax can impose an efficiency cost even when measured demand barely changes, because observed choices may not reveal informed preferences. Policymakers therefore need to test how taxes are displayed and understood, as well as how much revenue they raise. [3]

7. Corporate taxes: shareholders, workers, and landowners

Corporate income tax is often described as a tax on companies. A company is a legal entity, but its cash flows belong to people. Lower after-tax profit can reduce payments to owners. Firms may also cut investment, change wages, increase prices, or move activity. Property owners may receive lower rents when a location becomes less attractive. These responses happen over different periods, so the incidence of a corporate tax can change over time. It is misleading to claim that workers always pay all of it or that shareholders always pay all of it.

Juan Carlos Suárez Serrato and Owen Zidar studied changes in US state corporate taxes and the responses of local labor markets. Their 2016 American Economic Review paper used a model with firms and workers that could move imperfectly across locations. Their original estimates divided the burden among firm owners, workers, and landowners rather than placing it entirely on one group. This work is valuable because it connects a tax to wages, rents, firm location, and profits, not only to the amount reported on a company's tax return. [4]

The research also offers an important lesson about uncertainty. In 2023, Clément Malgouyres, Thierry Mayer, and Clément Mazet-Sonilhac published a formal comment questioning part of the model's identification. The original authors published a reply and corrected their structural estimates. In that reply, they reported shares of 38.1% for firm owners, 35.0% for workers, and 26.8% for landowners, with wider confidence intervals for some estimates. These are estimates for a particular US setting and model. They are not universal rules for corporate tax incidence. [5][6]

The dispute is substantive, not a reason to ignore the field. It shows why researchers must test assumptions about firm composition, labor mobility, local productivity, and the choice of comparison areas. A tax change can attract different types of firms. Wages can change because the mix of jobs changes, not only because each employer changes its pay. If the analysis does not separate these channels, it may assign too much or too little of the burden to workers. The right conclusion is a range of possible effects supported by context-specific evidence.

8. Payroll taxes and the meaning of a labor-cost wedge

Payroll taxes may be legally split between employees and employers. For example, one contribution may appear as a deduction from pay, while another is paid directly by the employer. The OECD defines the labor tax wedge as the difference between an employer's total labor cost and a worker's take-home pay, taking account of specified taxes, social contributions, and benefits. This is a useful accounting measure. It does not by itself tell us how much of the economic burden employers or employees ultimately bear. [7]

Over time, employers care about the full cost of hiring and workers care about the pay and benefits they receive. If wages can adjust, a tax formally charged to employers may reduce wage growth. If minimum-wage rules bind, workers have bargaining power, or labor supply is limited, the adjustment may instead appear in employment, employer profits, or product prices. Some social contributions finance benefits that workers value, which further complicates a simple burden calculation. The practical task is to examine total compensation, employment, and benefit value together.

For India or any other country, it would be a mistake to transfer an OECD average directly into local policy. Formal employment, labor-market enforcement, contract work, payroll reporting, and access to public benefits differ widely. A useful country study must first identify the actual law, who is covered, which firms comply, and how workers move between formal and informal jobs. Only then can we estimate the burden and the welfare effects of changing contributions.

9. Consumption taxes: efficiency and fairness

Value-added tax, or VAT, is collected through a chain of firms and generally taxes final consumption. India's goods and services tax, or GST, has a related value-added structure, with important country-specific rules and exemptions. Retailers remit tax, but buyers may pay higher prices and producers may receive less. The burden can differ across goods and income groups because households buy different baskets and have different ability to change purchases.

A 2014 OECD and Korea Institute of Public Finance study examined consumption taxes across 20 OECD countries. It found that consumption taxes often appear regressive when measured against current annual income. Measured against expenditure, they are often roughly proportional or slightly progressive. This does not mean low-income households face no hardship. It means the denominator matters: some high-income households save a large share of income, while retired or temporarily low-income households may spend more than current income. [8]

Alastair Thomas revisited this issue using models for 27 OECD countries in a 2020 OECD working paper. The paper argued that expenditure-based measures often give a more meaningful view of lifetime consumption-tax burdens than a single year's income. Both approaches answer legitimate but different questions. Current income is relevant for immediate affordability; expenditure can better reflect consumption over time. A good policy review should show both measures, explain household types, and avoid calling one figure the whole distributional story. [9]

Reduced rates for essential goods can help poor households as a share of their spending, yet richer households may gain more money in total because they buy more. Direct cash support may target need better if government can identify recipients accurately and pay them reliably. But targeted transfers can miss eligible people, create administrative costs, and raise privacy concerns. The strongest choice depends on tax administration, available data, and the social goal. The OECD study cautions that reduced VAT rates often provide a weak way to direct aggregate benefits toward low-income groups. [8]

10. Worked example: a reduced tax rate and household support

Illustrative calculation, not observed household spending. Suppose a government reduces a tax on a set of basic goods from 10% to 5%, with the entire five-percentage-point reduction passed through to consumers. A lower-spending household buys ₹2,000 of these goods at pretax prices each month. Its saving is ₹100. A higher-spending household buys ₹6,000 and saves ₹300. The lower-spending household may gain more relative to its income, but the higher-spending household receives three times as much cash benefit. If pass-through is incomplete, both savings are smaller; sellers may keep some of the benefit.

Suppose there are 1,000 households of each type. The monthly tax revenue lost is ₹100,000 for the first group and ₹300,000 for the second, or ₹400,000 in total. A perfectly targeted transfer of ₹400 to each of the 1,000 lower-spending households would direct all ₹400,000 to that group. But this comparison assumes perfect eligibility data, zero administrative costs, no behavioral response, and full delivery. Real programs must account for errors, costs, price changes, and political feasibility. The example shows the distributional logic, not a universal case against reduced tax rates.

11. Competing views and policy trade-offs

One policy view favors broad tax bases with relatively uniform rates. Its strongest argument is that fewer exemptions can reduce compliance costs, tax avoidance, and distortions between similar goods. Governments can then use cash transfers or public services to support households with greater need. This approach works best when records are accurate, payments are reliable, and beneficiaries can use them. It can perform poorly when eligible people lack access or when transfers are delayed. Broad taxes can also make the cost of essentials more visible immediately.

Another view favors differentiated rates, exemptions, and special taxes to address distributional or social goals directly at the point of sale. Lower rates on necessities may be easier to use than a complex transfer application. Higher taxes on harmful activities can reflect costs imposed on others, called external costs. But exemptions create boundary disputes and lobbying opportunities. Higher-income consumers may receive large absolute benefits from reduced rates. A rate intended to help one group can change supply decisions and end up helping a different group.

Both views require evidence about pass-through, household budgets, administration, and political capacity. A tax designed to discourage pollution should be judged partly by how much pollution it reduces. A tax designed to fund services should be judged by reliable revenue and its broader welfare effects. A tax designed to redistribute should be judged by who gains and loses after both taxes and spending. Comparing only the statutory rates cannot answer these questions.

There is also a tension between short-run and long-run results. A tax increase can be passed through quickly when inventories are low and demand is strong. Over several years, businesses may invest in different equipment or consumers may find substitutes. A tax cut may increase profits before competition lowers prices. Researchers should specify the time horizon and measure the outcome that matches the policy objective. Otherwise, apparently conflicting studies may be measuring different stages of adjustment.

12. How economists estimate incidence in practice

A credible incidence study begins with a clear counterfactual: what would prices, wages, and quantities have been without the tax change? Researchers may compare areas with different reforms, examine changes around policy dates, use randomized information displays, or estimate supply and demand from observed behavior. Each method has risks. Regions may differ for reasons unrelated to tax. Firms may anticipate reforms. A tax may be introduced during a recession. Price data may omit discounts, quality changes, or informal transactions.

A useful study checks whether treated and comparison groups were moving similarly before a reform. It tests for responses before the official date, since anticipation can matter. It measures the price consumers pay and the amount suppliers keep, when possible. For labor taxes, it checks wages, employment, hours, and benefits. For corporate taxes, it examines profits, capital investment, rents, and firm composition. The choice of data determines which channels can be seen.

Researchers must also separate incidence from distribution. Incidence concerns the causal burden of a specific tax. Distribution asks how the combined system affects groups across income, wealth, region, or age. A household can pay more in sales taxes and still gain overall if it receives valuable transfers or public services. The Congressional Budget Office's household-income distribution work illustrates why analysts often examine income before and after transfers and federal taxes. Its figures apply to US definitions and periods, not to other countries automatically. [10]

Finally, uncertainty should be reported in a useful form. A single percentage can hide wide confidence intervals, untested assumptions, or changes over time. Sensitivity analysis asks how results change when we vary elasticities, market structure, compliance, and behavior. An evidence-based policy brief should state the central estimate, plausible range, and which assumptions would reverse the conclusion. A strong result can survive several methods; a fragile result may depend on one model choice.

13. Why the distinction matters

For a household, incidence helps explain why a tax that is described as a business levy can affect wages or prices. It also explains why a lower tax rate does not always produce an equal reduction in retail prices. For a founder or business manager, it clarifies that the effect of a tax depends on customer alternatives, supplier capacity, competition, and contract terms. A firm with loyal buyers may pass on more of a cost change than a firm in a market with close substitutes.

For public policy, incidence is essential to deciding whether a measure is fair and effective. Governments can collect the same amount through different bases, but the burden can fall on different people and can change how much work, investment, or consumption occurs. Tax design therefore needs three linked assessments: who bears the cost, what behavior changes, and what public value the revenue creates. Ignoring any one of these can produce a misleading conclusion.

The durable lesson is to follow the economic response rather than the tax form. Ask who can change behavior, who has alternatives, what prices can move, and over what time period. Then test those predictions against evidence. This approach will not settle every political disagreement, because societies value fairness and efficiency differently. It will make the disagreement clearer and prevent false certainty about who pays.

14. Key lessons worth remembering

  1. The legal remitter and the economic bearer of a tax may differ.
  2. Relative demand and supply elasticities determine the burden in the basic competitive model.
  3. A tax creates a wedge between the buyer's price and the seller's net receipt.
  4. Tax revenue is a transfer to government; deadweight loss is a separate efficiency cost.
  5. When taxes are not visible, legal collection rules can affect behavior.
  6. Corporate taxes can affect owners, workers, and property owners, and estimates are contested.
  7. Payroll tax labels do not by themselves determine changes in total compensation.
  8. VAT fairness looks different when measured against current income versus expenditure.
  9. Reduced tax rates can give large total benefits to households that spend more.
  10. Good analysis combines prices, quantities, distribution, time horizons, and the value of public spending.

15. Knowledge check: five questions with answers

1. If a shop sends sales tax to government, does the shop necessarily bear the full cost?

No. Its selling price and net receipt can change. Buyers may pay more, sellers may receive less, and fewer units may be sold. The final split depends on market responses.

2. In the illustrative ₹10 tax model, why did buyers bear ₹6 and sellers ₹4?

The assumed demand and supply slopes gave a new equilibrium with a ₹26 buyer price and a ₹16 seller receipt, compared with ₹20 before tax. Other elasticities would give a different split.

3. Why can the legal payer matter when a tax is hidden until checkout?

Some consumers do not fully account for a tax that is less visible. Changing how it is displayed or collected can alter perceived prices, demand, and welfare.

4. Why is a single corporate-tax incidence percentage unsafe to use everywhere?

Workers and firms have different mobility, markets and bargaining conditions. Research models also differ and can be contested. Evidence from one region and period is not a universal constant.

5. Why can a reduced VAT rate help poorer households proportionally yet give richer households more cash?

Poorer households may spend a larger share of income on the item, while richer households may spend more money in total. A rate cut applies to each unit purchased, so total savings can be larger for higher spending.
