Can Sewage Warn Us About an Outbreak?

How scientists measure pathogens in wastewater, what the signal reveals, and why good public-health decisions require more than a laboratory result.

Executive summary

Wastewater surveillance measures traces of pathogens in sewage to learn whether infections are circulating in a community. It can detect change even when people do not seek a test, have mild symptoms, or have no symptoms. A sewer system combines waste from many households. A carefully chosen sample can therefore act as a pooled public-health observation. Scientists measure genetic fragments of a virus or other target, compare results across dates, and ask whether the trend is rising, falling, or uncertain.

But the measurement is indirect. It is affected by who uses the sewer, how much pathogen each person sheds, rainwater, laboratory recovery, and how samples are collected. A positive sample does not tell us how many people are ill. It also does not show that infectious virus is present. The strongest use is to add another signal to clinical testing, hospital data, and local health reports. This chapter explains the history, chemistry, statistics, decisions, costs, privacy questions, and limits of that system, including studies from India. Sources include the WHO's 2024 multi-pathogen guidance, CDC methods updated in 2026, and peer-reviewed studies from Yale and Ahmedabad.

1. The central question

Can a city see an outbreak before hospitals do?

A hospital counts people who arrive for care. A testing service counts people who request and receive tests. Both depend on choices, access, symptoms, reporting, and time. Wastewater collects material from people who never enter those systems. Some infected people shed pathogen material in stool or other waste. When their waste reaches a treatment plant, it joins material from many other people. A laboratory can detect selected biological markers in this mixture.

That gives an unusual kind of information: a population-level signal with limited information about individuals. It is particularly useful when clinical tests become less common or when the health system has a reporting delay. Yet it does not replace a clinical diagnosis. We must separate three questions: Is a pathogen present? Is its signal changing? What action, if any, should follow? Each question needs different evidence. A detection may justify closer observation; a sustained rise may justify communication or extra clinical sampling; a policy decision needs context about severity, local risks, and resources.

The main scientific problem is an inverse problem. We observe material at the end of a pipe and try to infer what happened upstream. Several different causes can produce the same reading. A reliable programme is designed around this uncertainty instead of hiding it.

2. From polio to pandemic surveillance

Environmental surveillance did not begin with COVID-19. Public-health teams have tested sewage for poliovirus for decades. Poliovirus can spread without a visible case of paralysis. Monitoring sewage therefore helps reveal circulation that case reports alone might miss. The WHO Global Polio Surveillance Action Plan 2025–2026 includes environmental surveillance alongside monitoring of acute flaccid paralysis, laboratory sequencing, and data systems. The methods complement one another because each sees a different part of the problem.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, researchers adapted this idea to SARS-CoV-2. In 2020, a team led by Jordan Peccia measured viral RNA in primary sewage sludge around New Haven, Connecticut. Their results tracked the first local wave and were compared with case counts and hospital admissions. This was evidence that wastewater could follow infection dynamics; it was not proof that every sewer signal would lead every hospital measure by the same number of days. Study design, reporting delays, and the disease itself affect the timing. See the September 2020 paper.

India contributed early evidence. Manish Kumar and colleagues detected three SARS-CoV-2 gene targets in untreated wastewater at Ahmedabad's Old Pirana treatment plant in samples collected in May 2020. Their July 2020 study found a higher genetic signal in the later sample, consistent with a rise in active reported cases. Two dates alone could not establish a reliable forecasting system. Later work in Ahmedabad used longer sampling series and reported a lead of roughly one to two weeks relative to reported cases in that setting. The 2023 longitudinal study examined 287 samples; its reported lead should not be treated as a universal rule.

In September 2020, the United States launched its National Wastewater Surveillance System. The programme later expanded beyond SARS-CoV-2. CDC's programme overview describes monitoring of influenza A, respiratory syncytial virus, measles, avian influenza A(H5), and mpox as well as COVID-19. Different pathogens need different assays, sampling choices, and interpretation rules.

3. Five concepts needed to read the data

Pathogen: an agent such as a virus or bacterium that can cause disease. Not every organism detected in sewage causes disease, and not every detected genetic fragment comes from an infectious particle.

Shedding: release of biological material from an infected person into waste. Shedding can begin before symptoms, continue after symptoms, or vary widely between people. It depends on the pathogen and stage of infection.

Sewershed: the land area and population whose wastewater flows to a given sampling point. It is defined by pipes and drainage, not necessarily by municipal or district boundaries. Visitors, commuters, hospitals, and industrial users may alter what the sample represents.

RNA and PCR: RNA is genetic material used by many viruses. Reverse-transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction, or RT-qPCR, converts target RNA to DNA and amplifies selected sequences so that they can be measured. A positive result usually indicates that a target genetic sequence was present above the assay's detection limit. It does not by itself establish that live infectious virus remains.

Concentration versus load: concentration measures copies per unit of sewage, such as copies per litre. Load combines concentration with wastewater flow to estimate copies moving past a site per day. A programme may use either or both. They answer related but different questions. Laboratory recovery, sampling error, and flow measurements can make both uncertain. In particular, CDC's 2025 wastewater viral activity level methodology switched to a site-standardized metric using non-normalized data, so it would be wrong to say every public dashboard always normalizes by flow.

4. How the measurement works

The process begins with a question. A health team chooses a target, such as influenza A, and a population it wants to monitor. It maps the sewer network, selects a safe sampling point, and agrees on collection frequency with the utility. A sample taken after wastewater has been chemically treated may lose the target signal, so untreated influent or suitable sludge is often preferred.

Step 1 — Collect. A single grab sample is quick but can reflect one unusual moment. A composite sample combines many small portions over a period, often 24 hours. This reduces some daily variation. Automated samplers may collect by time or flow. Neither approach fixes a poor sampling location. The CDC sampling guidance describes these trade-offs.

Step 2 — Preserve. The sample must be stored and moved under a validated protocol. Heat, time, and handling can degrade RNA or change the measured amount. Teams use controls to identify sample-processing failures.

Step 3 — Extract. A laboratory concentrates target material if needed and extracts nucleic acids from a difficult mixture of organic matter, chemicals, and microorganisms. Recovery is imperfect. A recovery control helps reveal how much material may have been lost.

Step 4 — Measure. An assay looks for selected genetic sequences. Replicate tests, negative controls, positive controls, and limits of detection matter. A weak signal near the detection limit is less certain than a clear repeated signal. Sequencing can sometimes identify patterns of mutations linked to variants, but mixed fragments make exact interpretation difficult.

Step 5 — Analyse. The team checks laboratory quality, notes rainfall and wastewater flow, compares repeated samples, and estimates whether the trend changed beyond expected noise. A graph should show dates, uncertainty, and missing samples. It should not present a single number as a direct count of infected people.

Step 6 — Act and learn. Public-health officials compare wastewater with test positivity, emergency visits, hospital admissions, and local reports. They may increase testing, investigate a new signal, communicate risk, or decide that no immediate action is justified. Later, they assess whether the response improved decisions. The WHO 2024 framework stresses integration with other surveillance, not isolated use.

5. Why the signal is hard to interpret

Imagine that two towns each report the same concentration of viral RNA. They need not have the same number of infections. One town may have a smaller sewer population, different rainfall, or a few people who shed unusually large amounts. Even within one town, shedding changes with disease stage and variant, while the sewer network changes with weather and industrial activity.

Wastewater can contain inhibitors: substances that interfere with the laboratory assay. Recovery efficiency may vary between samples. Some targets break down faster than others. A plant may receive wastewater from a hospital or a large event venue, which changes the population contributing to the sample. Communities using septic tanks or informal sanitation may be absent entirely. A city-wide claim based only on a few large sewer networks can exclude rural or unsewered populations.

Teams address these problems with repeat samples, consistent methods, controls, site metadata, and carefully chosen comparison periods. Some methods use a human faecal marker to estimate dilution or human contribution; others use flow data or standardized site-level distributions. There is no single adjustment that removes every source of uncertainty. CDC's August 2026 data-methods page explains its quality checks and site coverage, while its viral activity methodology shows why the choice of metric can change.

A strong upward trend across several samples is usually more informative than a one-day spike. However, a rising genetic signal does not automatically predict rising severe illness. Prior immunity, age distribution, vaccination, and changes in the pathogen may alter the relation between infections and hospital admissions. The public-health meaning must be recalibrated over time.

6. Worked example: rain changes concentration

Illustrative calculation; these are not observed data. A treatment plant receives 50 million litres of wastewater in one day. A laboratory measures 100,000 target RNA copies per litre. Estimated daily load is:

50,000,000 L/day × 100,000 copies/L = 5 × 1012 copies/day.

On a rainy day, water flow doubles to 100 million litres per day. Suppose the same total amount of target material enters the sewer, but the additional water dilutes it. The concentration would fall to 50,000 copies per litre. The calculated daily load stays at 5 × 1012 copies. Looking only at concentration would suggest a 50% decline. Looking at estimated load would suggest no change.

Real systems are more complex. Rain can also wash new material into pipes, cause overflow, or alter travel time. Flow meters have error. This example only demonstrates why dilution and metric choice matter. It does not show that flow adjustment is always the correct method. A standardized public dashboard may deliberately use a different validated approach.

7. Worked example: a sustained rise

Illustrative calculation; these are not observed data. Suppose three comparable samples from one site in the first period measure 100, 120, and 90 units of a validated site metric. Three later samples measure 210, 240, and 220. Their geometric means are approximately 103 and 223 units. The ratio is about 2.17. This suggests that the later period has a little more than twice the measured signal of the earlier period.

That ratio is a description of measurements, not a statement that the number of infected people doubled. To decide whether the change is meaningful, an analyst checks the dates, uncertainty, rainfall, assay performance, site coverage, and whether the rise persists. It is also important to check whether both periods used the same laboratory method. If the method changed, the apparent jump could partly be technical.

A public-health team might use the sustained rise to ask local clinics whether they see more respiratory illness, to increase sequencing, or to prepare communications for high-risk groups. If the signal falls in the next samples and other indicators remain stable, a large response may be unnecessary. The value lies in earlier, better-informed choices rather than perfect prediction.

8. What the evidence shows

The early Yale study established that viral RNA in sludge could track community infection dynamics in a defined population. Its strength was repeated sampling during a known wave. Its limit was that correlation at one place and time does not establish a universal conversion from copies to cases. The published research should be read for the sampling design and comparison with clinical data, not just its headline.

Ahmedabad's early 2020 study showed that SARS-CoV-2 genetic targets could be detected in Indian wastewater. Later, a longer Ahmedabad series reported signals that preceded reported case counts by one to two weeks in that context. These are important observations for cities with uneven access to clinical testing. However, reported-case lead time depends on when people seek tests and when cases are entered into official data. A programme should test its own local performance instead of importing the reported lead from another city. See Kumar and colleagues, 2020, and the 2023 study.

At a larger scale, CDC reports that its programme receives data from more than 1,000 US wastewater sites in a typical week and recommends testing twice per week. Those figures describe programme coverage, not universal representativeness. Coverage is shaped by which treatment plants participate. The 2026 methodology document also describes policies to protect small populations from public identification.

The strongest general finding is that wastewater provides a useful independent trend indicator for selected pathogens under suitable conditions. The weaker claim is that it always gives a fixed early-warning window or exact infection count. WHO's December 2024 guidance recommends prioritizing targets based on public-health value, feasibility, ethics, and how the data will change a real decision.

9. Beyond COVID-19

Polio illustrates why environmental surveillance matters even when clinical cases are rare. A person can shed poliovirus without a diagnosis of paralytic disease. A sewage detection can therefore prompt genetic investigation and review of vaccination coverage. But one positive sample does not by itself establish the number of infected people or prove widespread transmission. The WHO's 2025–2026 surveillance plan combines sewage data with case surveillance, laboratory networks, and reporting systems.

Respiratory viruses create another use case. Influenza A and RSV circulate seasonally, and the proportion of infected people who obtain clinical tests changes. Wastewater may help describe broad changes in circulation. Different pathogens shed differently, however, so a testing method validated for SARS-CoV-2 cannot simply be assumed to work for influenza or RSV. A programme needs target-specific assays, sample handling, and validation.

Emerging pathogens raise harder questions. If a new marker appears in wastewater, it may be a genuine warning, contamination, or a signal from an unusual contributor to the sewer. Confirmatory testing and clinical investigation are needed. Sequencing mixed wastewater can sometimes identify characteristic mutations, but it may not reconstruct complete genomes or assign them to individual patients. A strong programme states both what its method can detect and what it cannot infer.

10. What public-health teams can do with it

The most useful output is often a decision rule, agreed before the next alert. A city might define what counts as a sustained rise, how quickly a result must be reviewed, and who receives the information. It should also specify the response: additional clinical testing, communication to hospitals, targeted laboratory sequencing, or closer monitoring. A signal with no planned response may add cost and confusion without improving health.

Public dashboards need careful language. A chart can show low, moderate, or high activity relative to the site's history, but those categories should not be presented as exact individual risk or exact case numbers. Reports should display data gaps and explain method changes. A rising trend can justify awareness without justifying emergency restrictions. Conversely, a negative sample does not guarantee that nobody in the community is infected: the target may be below the detection limit or absent from that day's sample.

Wastewater is particularly valuable when clinical reporting is delayed or testing access is uneven. It is less valuable when sample coverage is poor, laboratory turnaround is slow, or no action follows the result. The correct measure of success is whether the system improves decisions at a reasonable cost, not simply how many samples it processes.

11. The strongest competing cases

Case for investing

Supporters argue that a single pooled sample can monitor trends across a large sewer-connected population without asking each person to visit a clinic. The method can include asymptomatic and untested infections, can continue when testing behaviour changes, and can support more than one pathogen using shared collection infrastructure. Its history in polio and its performance during COVID-19 show that it can add evidence unavailable from case counts alone. A programme that uses existing treatment plants and validated laboratories may deliver useful surveillance at a lower marginal collection burden than mass individual testing.

Case for restraint

Critics point to uneven sewer access, unstable relations between shedding and disease burden, assay costs, missing samples, and privacy concerns at small sampling scales. A system can produce sophisticated charts that are difficult to act on. Money spent on laboratory testing could sometimes do more good if spent on primary care, vaccination, clinical diagnostics, sanitation, or reliable hospital reporting. These are genuine competing priorities, especially where resources are scarce.

How to decide

Both arguments can be true. A sound investment case names the pathogen, population, sampling frequency, laboratory cost, turnaround time, likely decision, and alternative use of funds. It also includes a review date and criteria for reducing or ending monitoring if the information does not change action. WHO's multi-pathogen prioritization framework is designed for this type of choice.

12. Privacy, representation and governance

Community sewage is less personally identifying than a medical record, but it is not automatically free of ethical risk. Sampling a small institution, school, workplace, or residential block can stigmatize a group, especially if results are public and the target is sensitive. The smaller and more identifiable the population, the more care is required. Public health benefit must be weighed against consent, transparency, proportionality, and possible misuse.

CDC's 2026 public display rules say it does not normally publish certain small-sewershed or facility-specific results without local approval; the page names a 3,000-person threshold for sewersheds, along with additional safeguards. This is a policy of that programme, not a universal ethical standard for every country.

Representation is also an ethical issue. People outside municipal sewer networks are not automatically counted. Rural households, informal settlements, and users of septic systems can be absent from a city dashboard. WHO's guidance includes other human-impacted environmental waters where appropriate, but methods must be adapted to local conditions. A dashboard should state whom it covers. It should never label a whole district safe because one connected part has a low signal.

13. Open technical and policy questions

How many people are infected? Direct conversion remains difficult because shedding varies and the observed signal is a mixture. Models can estimate infections under assumptions, but the uncertainty can be wide. An estimate should state those assumptions.

How early is early? The advantage changes with the pathogen, the sampling schedule, clinical testing delays, and laboratory turnaround. It is an empirical question for each programme.

How should sites be compared? Different sewer systems, lab methods, and statistical baselines can make direct ranking misleading. Standardization helps but cannot make populations identical.

What should be measured next? Multi-pathogen panels can spread collection costs, but each added target requires validation and a decision use. More targets do not automatically mean more useful information.

Who should own and explain the data? Utilities, laboratories, health departments, and local communities all have roles. Public trust depends on clear governance, timely correction of errors, and transparent reporting of what a detection means.

14. Ten lessons

  1. Wastewater surveillance measures pooled biological signals, not individual diagnoses.
  2. A positive genetic test does not establish that live infectious pathogen is present.
  3. Repeated trends usually carry more meaning than a single result.
  4. Sewershed boundaries differ from administrative boundaries.
  5. Rain, flow, shedding, sampling, and laboratory recovery can change readings.
  6. Different dashboards may use different validated normalization methods.
  7. Local evidence is needed before claiming a fixed lead over clinical cases.
  8. Polio surveillance shows that the idea predates COVID-19.
  9. Privacy and coverage matter most where sampling populations are small or unevenly served.
  10. The final test is whether information improves public-health decisions.

15. Knowledge check: five answered questions

1. Why can a wastewater sample detect infections missed by clinical testing?
It receives biological material from people connected to the sewer whether or not they seek a clinical test. The result is still a pooled and incomplete population measure.
2. Does twice the measured viral concentration mean twice as many infections?
No. Flow, shedding, population, sampling, and lab recovery can all change the concentration. A rise is a signal that requires context.
3. Why might a 24-hour composite sample be better than one grab sample?
It combines material collected across the day and can reduce the effect of short-term changes in sewer flow and waste composition. It cannot correct every sampling bias.
4. Why should wastewater data be compared with hospital and clinical data?
Wastewater describes circulation of target material; clinical data help describe illness, severity, and affected groups. Together they support stronger decisions.
5. What is one reason to stop a wastewater programme?
If reliable testing is too slow, unrepresentative, or costly to change any useful public-health decision, resources may be better used elsewhere. WHO recommends periodic reassessment.
