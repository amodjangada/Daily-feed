When Does Green Hydrogen Make Business Sense?

Where does renewable hydrogen create enough value to justify its electricity use, cost, and infrastructure?

Executive summary

Green hydrogen is made by splitting water with electricity from renewable sources. It can replace hydrogen produced from fossil fuels and may help industries that need hydrogen as a chemical input. But its value depends on the whole system. Electricity cost, electrolyser use, financing, water, transport, storage, and the buyer's willingness to pay all affect the final price. It also matters what hydrogen replaces. Using clean electricity directly is often more efficient when a practical electric alternative exists. The International Energy Agency reported in June 2026 that global hydrogen demand exceeded 100 million tonnes in 2025, while low-emissions production remained close to one million tonnes. Most hydrogen still came from fossil fuels. Many proposed clean projects have not reached investment decisions because customers lack firm purchase contracts. This report explains production, costs, emissions, and market risks in stages. It compares strong arguments for targeted hydrogen investment with reasons to avoid using hydrogen everywhere. The central lesson is to evaluate a specific customer, supply chain, and carbon outcome before building capacity.

1. Background: an old industrial gas with a new climate role

Hydrogen has been used in industry for many decades. Refineries use it to remove sulphur and process oil. Fertiliser plants combine hydrogen with nitrogen to make ammonia. Chemical plants use it to produce methanol and other products. These are established markets with continuous demand, trained operators, and safety rules. The new climate question is how to make the same hydrogen with much lower greenhouse gas emissions, and where else it may be useful.

Most conventional hydrogen comes from natural gas or coal. Steam methane reforming uses high-temperature steam to react with methane and produce hydrogen and carbon dioxide. Coal gasification produces a gas mixture from coal and then separates hydrogen. Both routes release greenhouse gases unless emissions are captured and upstream leaks are controlled. The IEA's 2024 review estimated that global hydrogen production emitted 920 million tonnes of carbon dioxide in 2023, using its defined accounting boundary. This historical figure should not be treated as a 2026 emissions estimate. [3]

The 2019 IEA report The Future of Hydrogen helped frame hydrogen as a possible tool for industrial decarbonisation and energy security. Governments and firms then announced large clean-hydrogen plans. The industry developed electrolysers, proposed new pipelines, and explored hydrogen-derived fuels. Yet announced capacity is not operating capacity. By June 2026, the IEA described a sector with real growth, but persistent cost gaps, weak purchase commitments, and project delays. The commercial lesson is that an attractive long-term market does not guarantee near-term cash flow. [1][8]

This study continues the previous Daily Feed lessons on industrial heat pumps and thermal energy storage. Those technologies often use electricity directly to deliver heat. Hydrogen offers a different route: convert electricity into a molecule that can serve as a chemical input, fuel, or storage medium. Understanding when that extra conversion is valuable is the central business question.

2. Essential terms and clear boundaries

Electrolysis is a process that uses electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen. An electrolyser is the equipment that performs this process. Green hydrogen usually means hydrogen made through electrolysis using renewable electricity, but the exact definition depends on the certification scheme. Low-emissions hydrogen is a wider term that can include other production methods if their full emissions meet a defined threshold. Color labels such as green, blue, and grey are informal shortcuts. They do not replace a measured carbon-intensity value.

Carbon intensity is the greenhouse gas emissions per kilogram of hydrogen, usually stated in kilograms of carbon dioxide equivalent per kilogram of hydrogen. Capacity factor describes how much a plant operates relative to full output over a period. Levelised cost of hydrogen estimates average production cost per kilogram across the equipment's life, including capital, energy, operation, and sometimes financing. It is a comparison tool, not a guaranteed sale price.

An offtake agreement is a contract in which a buyer agrees to purchase output under defined conditions. A final investment decision, or FID, is a formal commitment to proceed with a project, normally after technical, commercial, and financing reviews. A derivative is a product made from hydrogen, such as ammonia or synthetic fuel. A feedstock is a material used to make another product, rather than merely a fuel burned for heat.

Additional renewable electricity means new or otherwise additional clean supply that can be linked to hydrogen production under a specified rule. Temporal matching means comparing clean electricity generation and electrolyser consumption over a defined time period. These concepts matter because an electrolyser connected to a fossil-heavy grid may raise total emissions even if its operator purchases annual renewable-energy certificates. The exact result depends on the power system and the accounting method.

3. How green hydrogen is produced, one stage at a time

Stage one is to secure electricity. A project can use power from a dedicated solar or wind plant, a clean grid, or a combination of sources. The price paid per kilowatt-hour is critical because electrolysis uses substantial electricity for each kilogram produced. A plant that uses only cheap midday solar may have low energy cost but operate fewer hours. A plant that runs continuously may use more expensive power or need storage. The business must balance electricity price against the cost of idle equipment.

Stage two is to prepare water. Electrolysers require water of suitable quality, often treated to remove impurities. Water supply may be a modest share of total production cost in some places, yet it can be a serious local constraint. Desalination, wastewater treatment, and cooling arrangements have their own energy use, infrastructure, and environmental impacts. The IEA's 2024 review noted that a large share of announced low-emissions projects were in water-stressed regions. That finding describes project geography, not proof that all those projects will create harmful water shortages. [4]

Stage three is electrolysis. Alkaline electrolysers use an alkaline electrolyte and are a mature option. Proton exchange membrane, or PEM, electrolysers use a polymer membrane and can respond quickly to changing power, but they have different material and cost needs. Solid oxide electrolysers use high-temperature steam and can achieve useful efficiencies where suitable heat is available, but they face their own technical and operating challenges. No technology is best for every site. The relevant choice depends on operating hours, temperature, equipment cost, durability, and maintenance.

Stage four is gas treatment and delivery. Hydrogen may need drying, purification, compression, storage, and transport before the buyer can use it. Compressing gas requires energy. Liquefying hydrogen requires cooling to very low temperatures and additional energy. Converting hydrogen into ammonia makes some transport and storage tasks easier, but conversion and later recovery can create substantial losses. A project selling hydrogen at the electrolyser gate has a different cost structure from one delivering fuel across an ocean.

Stage five is customer integration. An ammonia plant may be able to substitute clean hydrogen into an existing chemical process, but its pressure, purity, flow, and reliability requirements must be met. A steel plant using hydrogen-based direct reduction needs suitable ore, equipment, and reliable heat and gas supply. A shipping-fuel project needs port facilities, vessel compatibility, and fuel standards. The full system succeeds only when production, logistics, and the customer's process work together.

Every additional stage adds equipment, cost, and potential energy loss. A business plan must identify the actual delivery point at which it quotes its price.

5. The five costs that determine commercial viability

The first cost is electricity. For a given electrolyser, electricity consumption per kilogram multiplied by the electricity tariff provides a useful starting estimate. If power costs twice as much, the energy portion of hydrogen cost roughly doubles, other things equal. But a low advertised renewable tariff is not the same as the price of power delivered whenever the plant needs it. Grid charges, backup supply, curtailment, balancing, and taxes can change the effective price.

The second cost is the electrolyser and its supporting equipment. A larger plant may reduce some unit costs, but it also requires more capital before any product is sold. A stack may need replacement during the project's life. Pumps, transformers, cooling, purification, and control systems must be included. The IRENA report Green Hydrogen Cost Reduction, published in December 2020, explained how larger plants, manufacturing scale, design, and improved operation could reduce costs. These were technology pathways and scenarios, not promises that every future project would reach a specific cost. [5]

The third cost is utilisation. Imagine an expensive machine that runs for only a few hours each day. Its fixed annual cost is divided across fewer kilograms. Increasing operating hours spreads that cost over more output, but may require buying more expensive electricity. This creates a trade-off between the price of energy and the number of hours the plant operates. A project that claims both very cheap power and very high utilisation must explain how it obtains them together.

The fourth cost is capital. Hydrogen plants are capital-intensive and often depend on long-term contracts. Lenders want to know whether the customer will buy enough hydrogen at a price that covers operating costs and debt payments. High interest rates, uncertain policy, and construction risk raise the required selling price. Public guarantees or low-cost finance can improve the project, but the risk then moves partly to taxpayers or another guarantor. A low headline production cost is incomplete without its financing assumptions.

The fifth cost is delivery and market access. A hydrogen user beside the plant may need only a short pipe. A distant buyer may require compression, storage tanks, trucks, new pipelines, or conversion into another chemical. The product may also need certified proof of its emissions intensity. Contracts must allocate losses, downtime, quality failures, and changing electricity prices. The customer compares delivered hydrogen with the incumbent fuel or feedstock, not with the producer's best-case plant-gate estimate.

A robust business case therefore needs at least two price numbers: a cost to produce at the plant and a cost to deliver hydrogen meeting the buyer's specification. It also needs a revenue model with contract volume, price adjustment rules, credit risk, and a realistic operating profile. The difference between a technical demonstration and a bankable project is often the strength of this commercial structure.

6. Worked example: what a kilogram of hydrogen might cost

Illustrative calculation; these are assumed values, not a quote or forecast. Suppose an electrolyser uses 52 kilowatt-hours of electricity for one kilogram of hydrogen, measured at the defined production boundary. Assume delivered electricity costs US$0.04 per kilowatt-hour. Electricity cost is then 52 × 0.04 = US$2.08 per kilogram. Add US$1.20 per kilogram for annualised equipment and financing, and US$0.40 for water, maintenance, and other operations. The estimated production cost is US$3.68 per kilogram before delivery, storage, taxes, and any margin.

Suppose the customer's conventional hydrogen alternative costs US$1.60 per kilogram at a comparable boundary. The illustrative clean-hydrogen premium is US$2.08 per kilogram. At 10,000 tonnes per year, that is 10 million kilograms, so the annual premium is about US$20.8 million before changes in volume or price. A policy subsidy, carbon price, premium product contract, or higher fossil-gas price might close part of this gap. None should be assumed without a binding rule or commercial agreement.

Now change only the power cost from US$0.04 to US$0.06 per kilowatt-hour. Electricity cost becomes US$3.12 per kilogram, and total illustrative production cost rises to US$4.72. The difference is US$1.04 per kilogram. This simple sensitivity shows why a small change in electricity price can matter more than a promotional claim about the electrolyser. In a real financial model, operating hours, efficiency, capital costs, degradation, and taxes must also vary together.

The example is deliberately narrow. The cost of hydrogen from natural gas changes with gas prices and carbon costs. A plant that runs more hours may lower capital cost per kilogram. A renewable plant may sell surplus electricity instead of using it for hydrogen. These opportunity costs can alter the best choice. The purpose of the calculation is to show which inputs deserve careful verification before investment.

7. When does hydrogen actually cut emissions?

An electrolyser releases no carbon dioxide from the water-splitting reaction. This does not make every kilogram it produces climate-neutral. Electricity generation may emit carbon dioxide. Manufacturing equipment and renewable assets also creates emissions. Water treatment, compression, transport, and hydrogen leakage can add further impacts. A proper life-cycle assessment defines which stages are counted and reports a consistent emissions boundary.

The IEA's 2024 analysis estimated that conventional natural-gas hydrogen without carbon capture typically has an emissions intensity of about 10–12 kilograms of carbon dioxide equivalent per kilogram of hydrogen. It also found that electricity for electrolysis must be sufficiently low-carbon to outperform that route. In its analysis, a threshold around 200–240 grams of carbon dioxide per kilowatt-hour was relevant, depending on the comparison and system boundary. This is a guide from a defined model, not a universal certification rule. [3]

Illustrative calculation, not an actual grid emissions measurement. Using the same assumed 52 kilowatt-hours per kilogram, electricity with an emissions intensity of 0.60 kilograms of carbon dioxide per kilowatt-hour causes 31.2 kilograms of electricity-related emissions per kilogram of hydrogen. At 0.03 kilograms per kilowatt-hour, it causes 1.56 kilograms. These figures omit manufacturing, water, transport, and other stages. They show why the electricity source can change the climate result more than the label attached to the electrolyser.

The comparison must also ask what clean electricity would otherwise have done. If renewable generation is scarce, using it for hydrogen may leave more fossil generation on the grid. If the project builds additional clean supply that matches its operating hours, that risk can be reduced. Rules about additionality, temporal matching, and location aim to address this problem, but they vary across jurisdictions. An annual certificate alone does not prove that every hour of hydrogen production was supplied by zero-emissions power.

Hydrogen from fossil fuels with carbon capture can also reduce emissions relative to unabated production, but its outcome depends on capture performance, methane leakage, energy use, and the full supply chain. The strongest analysis compares measured emissions intensities under the same boundary rather than assuming that all blue or green projects perform alike. It also examines how the hydrogen is used: avoiding one kilogram of fossil-based industrial hydrogen is a different climate benefit from using hydrogen to replace direct electricity.

8. Where hydrogen has a strong case—and where it does not

The clearest near-term case is to replace fossil-based hydrogen in existing chemical and refining processes. These users already need hydrogen molecules, so the project can focus on changing how they are made. Ammonia and methanol are especially important because hydrogen is a feedstock in their production. The IEA's 2026 review reports that almost all global hydrogen demand in 2025 remained in established industrial and refining uses. That is why replacing current high-emissions supply can be more practical than building entirely new consumer markets. [1]

Steelmaking is another possible use. Hydrogen can help remove oxygen from iron ore in direct-reduced-iron processes, potentially reducing dependence on coal-based routes when suitable electricity, ore, and equipment are available. But a steel project must finance much more than an electrolyser. It needs a suitable reduction plant, electricity infrastructure, ore handling, and a buyer for low-emissions steel. The economics depend on the whole steel value chain and on how much of the cost premium customers will accept.

Long-distance shipping and aviation may need fuels with high energy content that can be stored and handled within tight space and weight limits. Hydrogen-derived ammonia and synthetic fuels are candidates, but they require conversion steps and dedicated safety systems. Their emissions depend on how the hydrogen and other inputs are produced. The 2026 IEA report sees potential for hydrogen-based products to diversify fuel supply over time, while noting that they are not ready at a scale that can quickly solve an immediate energy crisis. [1]

For passenger cars, many building-heating applications, and low-temperature industrial heat, direct electricity often has a strong efficiency advantage. Batteries deliver electricity to a motor without first converting it to hydrogen and back. Heat pumps can deliver several units of heat for each unit of electricity under suitable conditions. Hydrogen production, compression, transport, and use add conversion losses. This does not ban hydrogen in every such case. It means a project must show a clear reason why a hydrogen route is better than a practical direct-electric option.

The comparison should use the same service. For transport, compare cost and emissions per kilometre under realistic duty cycles. For heating, compare delivered useful heat at the required temperature and reliability. For steel, compare the cost and emissions of a tonne of finished steel. Comparing only energy content at the factory gate misses much of the decision.

9. What the latest evidence says about scale

The IEA's Global Hydrogen Review 2026, published on 18 June 2026, reported that world hydrogen demand exceeded 100 million tonnes in 2025. Low-emissions production grew by about 20% to almost one million tonnes, still a very small part of the market. Global installed electrolysis capacity doubled in 2025 to more than four gigawatts, with China accounting for much of the new deployment. These are historical observations reported in June 2026, not estimates of today's operating capacity. [1][2]

The same review reduced the pipeline of announced low-emissions hydrogen projects that might produce by 2030 to around 27 million tonnes a year. Many announced projects had not taken final investment decisions. The IEA reported that projects already committed or assessed as having strong potential had declined compared with its 2025 assessment. The gap between announcements and investment decisions matters because a proposal can be cancelled, delayed, or redesigned before construction. Treating every announcement as future production would overstate the market. [1]

Demand is a major constraint. The IEA reported that new offtake agreements in 2025 were about 1.7 million tonnes a year, and only around one-fifth of the newly signed volume had firm commitments. A producer cannot safely borrow against a non-binding statement of interest as though it were guaranteed revenue. Firm agreements still contain risks, but they provide stronger evidence of customer demand. The sector needs buyers who can pay for the premium and have reasons to keep buying through changes in energy markets. [1][6]

The IEA's 2025 review had already warned that the announced project pipeline was shrinking and that lower natural-gas prices, equipment costs, and slow policy implementation were widening the near-term commercial gap. The 2026 update reinforces the need to separate operating projects, committed projects, and speculative plans. This is a reason to use milestones in investment decisions, rather than a reason to assume hydrogen will never scale. [7]

There is also positive evidence. More electrolysers are operating, some industrial users have signed purchase contracts, and governments have created certification and procurement schemes. Learning can lower equipment costs as production increases. But a business model cannot rely on future learning alone. Investors should ask what happens if costs fall more slowly than expected, if the customer delays a plant, or if the policy that supports the premium changes.

10. Competing views: invest early or wait for stronger demand?

The case for early investment is strongest where a buyer already uses hydrogen and can sign a long-term agreement. An industrial cluster can share infrastructure, pool demand, and lower transport costs. Early projects can teach engineers how equipment performs, create skilled jobs, and develop standards. Governments may also value domestic fertiliser production or reduced dependence on imported gas. The 2026 IEA report discusses energy-security benefits of diversifying supply chains, while warning that those benefits take time to develop. [1]

The case for caution is also substantial. Clean hydrogen remains more expensive than conventional production in most regions in the near term. Large announced projects often depend on uncertain subsidies, optimistic electricity prices, and buyers who have not committed to purchase. Building production capacity without a market can leave idle equipment and public liabilities. Some proposed uses have direct-electric alternatives that waste less energy. A government can support the wrong route if it rewards hydrogen volume without checking emissions avoided or the value of the final service.

These views are compatible with selective deployment. The key disagreement is about which projects deserve scarce capital and public support now. Supporters place more weight on learning, industrial strategy, and future markets. Critics place more weight on near-term cost, alternative technologies, and policy risk. A credible decision should compare options at the same service level and disclose assumptions about electricity, carbon, financing, and customer contracts.

A good policy can be technology-neutral where practical: reward verified emissions reductions, reliable energy supply, or industrial output rather than a fuel label alone. In sectors where infrastructure needs coordinated investment, targeted support may be justified. Yet subsidies should have transparent eligibility, review dates, and clear measures of results. Without those safeguards, public funds can support plants that do not deliver the promised climate or economic value.

11. Water, land, safety, and local effects

Hydrogen production has local impacts that a global carbon calculation may miss. Electrolysis needs purified water and often cooling. Renewable electricity may require land, transmission lines, and materials. Large plants can change local power demand. Where water is scarce, a project should show its source, treatment process, discharge plan, and effect on other users. The relevant measure is local water availability and competing demand, not simply litres per kilogram in isolation.

Hydrogen is a small molecule and can leak through unsuitable seals. It is flammable and requires appropriate detection, ventilation, materials, pressure control, and trained staff. These are manageable engineering challenges, but they are real costs. Safety standards and emergency planning are part of project viability. Ammonia can be easier to move in some supply chains but introduces toxicity risks that need different controls. The best carrier depends on destination, end use, and local infrastructure.

A sustainable project also needs social acceptance. Communities may ask whether new power lines and water infrastructure will serve them or mainly support exports. Local jobs and tax revenue can create benefits, but the distribution of those benefits matters. In its 2026 Africa chapter, the IEA argues that hydrogen plans should be connected to broader electricity access, industrial development, and financing needs. The same principle applies elsewhere: project benefits should be assessed in the place where resources are used. [1]

12. A practical decision process for a business

First identify the buyer's exact need. Is hydrogen required as a chemical input, a reducing agent, a high-temperature fuel, or a means of long-duration storage? Specify purity, pressure, delivery hours, volume, and reliability. If a direct-electric route can provide the same service, compare it before selecting hydrogen. This first step prevents investment in equipment that does not solve the customer's main problem.

Second establish the reference case. What does the customer pay today for fossil-based hydrogen or another technology? What emissions are caused by that reference case, using a clear boundary? Include any existing carbon price, product standard, or import risk. The reference price must be comparable to the delivered price of the proposed hydrogen. An artificially cheap baseline or an incomplete clean-hydrogen price will distort the investment decision.

Third build an hourly energy and operations model. Use real or contracted power prices, expected renewable output, equipment efficiency, planned downtime, storage needs, and delivery schedules. Estimate production cost across a range of utilisation rates and electricity prices. Then add water, equipment replacement, financing, transport, certification, and taxes. Stress-test delays and low demand. A single annual average price cannot show whether the plant can meet the buyer's schedule.

Fourth secure the commercial agreement. The strongest projects have a creditworthy customer, a clear quantity, an acceptable price formula, and terms for delivery failure or changes in policy. If the customer will pay a premium because its own buyers want lower-emissions products, verify that demand. If the project depends on public incentives, check whether the incentive is enacted, funded, and available for the full relevant period.

Fifth measure climate performance using the actual electricity supply and supply-chain emissions. Use a transparent certification method and check how results change under different grid conditions. A low-emissions claim should survive independent review. The final investment decision should compare the hydrogen project with other ways to reduce the same emissions. The best choice is the option that provides the needed service with an acceptable cost, risk, and verified environmental result.

13. Why this matters beyond hydrogen

Hydrogen illustrates a wider rule of sustainable business: a low-carbon input has value only when it solves a real customer problem at a viable cost. Technology can work in a demonstration and still fail commercially if the supply chain is incomplete. Policy can reduce risk, but it cannot create permanent demand merely by announcing a target. Investors need credible buyers and a route to stable cash flow.

The sector also shows why climate accounting must follow the whole process. A plant can be clean at its own fence while relying on high-emissions electricity or energy-intensive transport. Conversely, a higher-cost input may be valuable when it cuts large emissions in a process that has few alternatives. Comparing entire systems is more useful than comparing equipment labels or isolated efficiency figures.

For industrial managers, the immediate task is to map current hydrogen demand and examine where low-emissions supply can be introduced with the least disruption. For policymakers, it is to create clear standards, dependable demand signals, and public support tied to measured outcomes. For investors, it is to separate operational evidence from forecasts. Those lessons apply to many other clean industrial technologies.

14. Key lessons worth remembering

  1. Hydrogen is already an industrial feedstock; its climate challenge is mainly how it is produced.
  2. Green and blue labels do not replace a measured life-cycle emissions intensity.
  3. Electricity cost and electrolyser utilisation strongly affect hydrogen cost.
  4. Plant-gate production cost is lower than the full cost of delivery in many projects.
  5. Using low-carbon electricity is essential to making electrolysis climate-beneficial.
  6. Replacing existing fossil-based industrial hydrogen is often a clearer first market than inventing new uses.
  7. Direct electrification is often more efficient when it can deliver the same service.
  8. Firm purchase contracts matter more to financing than project announcements.
  9. Water, safety, infrastructure, and local impacts belong in the business case.
  10. Investment decisions should compare whole systems and test cost, demand, and policy risks.

15. Knowledge check: five questions with answers

1. Why is an electrolyser using ordinary grid electricity not automatically green?

The electricity may be generated from fossil fuels. Its emissions and other supply-chain impacts must be included. An electrolyser's water-splitting reaction has no direct CO₂ output, but the whole process may still emit substantial greenhouse gases.

2. What two factors can make cheap renewable electricity insufficient for low-cost hydrogen?

Low operating hours spread fixed equipment and financing costs across fewer kilograms, while delivery, storage, and reliability requirements add costs beyond the advertised electricity tariff.

3. Why is replacing existing fossil-based hydrogen often a strong first market?

The buyer already needs hydrogen as a chemical input and may have existing handling systems. That removes some demand uncertainty, although clean supply must still meet price, quality, and reliability requirements.

4. In the illustrative emissions calculation, what changes when electricity falls from 0.60 to 0.03 kg CO₂/kWh?

For 52 kWh per kilogram of hydrogen, electricity-related emissions fall from 31.2 to 1.56 kg CO₂ per kilogram of hydrogen. Other life-cycle emissions still need to be added.

5. What does a final investment decision show that an announced project does not?

It shows that sponsors have formally committed to proceed after relevant reviews. It does not remove all risk, but it is stronger evidence of likely delivery than a proposal without financing or firm customers.
