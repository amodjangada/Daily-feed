Why Retrieval-Augmented AI Still Gets Facts Wrong

Why can an AI search the right documents and still produce an unsupported answer?

Research checked 26 October 2026 · Numerical examples are illustrative

Executive summary

Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, connects a language model to documents outside its training data. Instead of relying only on patterns stored in model weights, the system searches a collection, selects passages, and gives them to the model with the question. This can improve answers about changing facts and private records. It can also show readers where an answer came from. Yet retrieval does not guarantee truth. A search can miss the right document, select an old version, split a key sentence across passages, or return text that looks relevant but answers a different question. The model may ignore useful evidence, combine incompatible claims, or cite a passage that does not support its statement. Research on dense retrieval, long-context models, irrelevant evidence, and adaptive retrieval shows why these errors occur. This guide follows a question through the full RAG pipeline. It explains each stage, how to measure failure, and how to design a system that can decline to answer when evidence is weak. The central lesson is that finding text and proving a claim are different tasks.

1. Why language models need external evidence

A language model learns from examples by adjusting numerical parameters, also called weights. The weights help it predict and generate text. They are not a searchable list of facts with dates and source addresses. A model can produce a useful answer without knowing whether the underlying information has changed. It may also produce a confident sentence for which it has no reliable evidence.

Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, adds an external source. A program searches a document collection and passes selected text to the model with the user's question. The model then uses that text while writing its answer. Researchers call the model's learned weights parametric memory and the external collection non-parametric memory. The second store can be updated without retraining the model.

Search systems existed long before modern text generation. The important change was to combine retrieval with a generator that could summarize and join evidence from several passages. Lewis and colleagues presented a neural RAG method in 2020 and reported improvements on knowledge-intensive tasks [1]. Their results established a useful approach, but they did not prove that every later RAG product is reliable.

Consider the distinction between a model that remembers a typical returns policy and a system that can read the exact policy effective today. The second system has a better route to the right answer. It still needs to retrieve the correct version and apply every condition. The rest of this guide explains that chain of work.

2. Terms that describe the system

A corpus is the set of documents available for search. Indexing prepares those documents so they can be found quickly. A chunk is a smaller passage taken from a document. A query is the text used for a search. A retriever ranks passages that might answer the question. A reranker examines a short list more closely and changes the order.

An embedding is a list of numbers representing aspects of a text. A dense retriever compares embeddings of questions and passages. A vector index stores these representations and helps find nearby matches. Keyword search instead relies strongly on matching terms and their distribution. Hybrid search combines candidates from both methods.

Three measures should remain separate. Retrieval recall asks whether the search found the evidence needed for an answer. Correctness asks whether the final claim is factually right. Faithfulness asks whether the displayed evidence actually supports the claim. A response can be correct but cite the wrong source. It can also faithfully repeat a source that is obsolete.

The context window is the text a model can process in one request. Grounding connects an answer to evidence. Abstention means choosing not to state a claim when evidence is insufficient. These words matter because a high score at one stage does not imply success at the others.

3. How search and generation came together

Traditional search systems often use lexical matching. BM25, for example, ranks text using information about the words in a query and the documents that contain them. Such methods remain effective for part numbers, exact names, legal phrases and uncommon terms. They can struggle when the question and the relevant passage use different words.

Karpukhin and colleagues studied dense passage retrieval, which learns numerical representations of questions and passages. Their EMNLP 2020 paper reported top-20 passage retrieval accuracy improvements of 9 to 19 percentage points over a strong BM25 comparison on the studied open-domain question-answering datasets [2]. Those figures describe the tested systems, not a universal advantage of dense search.

The original RAG work combined a retriever with a sequence-to-sequence generator. The model could condition its answer on text fetched from an external collection [1]. Izacard and Grave later studied a generator that reads several retrieved passages and combines information from them. Their 2021 experiments reported gains on open-domain question-answering benchmarks as the number of retrieved passages increased [3].

These advances made a practical architecture possible: store documents, search for evidence, and write a natural-language answer. They also created a new dependency chain. A generator may be excellent while the search returns the wrong page. A retriever may find the right page while the generator overlooks an exception. The quality of the final answer depends on both stages and on the data preparation between them.

4. Stage one: collect and prepare reliable documents

The first decision is what counts as an authoritative source. A collection can contain approved policies, old drafts, meeting notes, web pages, and user comments. Search relevance does not determine authority. Each record should have a stable identifier, an owner, a version or effective date where needed, and an access rule. A newer document is not automatically the correct one for a question about a past transaction.

Next, the system extracts text. PDFs may contain tables, columns and footnotes. Extraction can reverse a table's rows or omit a negative word. A search index built from damaged text can return a passage that appears credible but has lost its meaning. Good ingestion keeps section headings, page references and table relationships. It should report extraction failures instead of hiding them.

Long documents are usually split into chunks. Very short chunks can separate a rule from its exception. Very long chunks can mix unrelated topics and consume the context window. Overlap between adjacent chunks can preserve text across boundaries, but it creates duplicates. Chunk size and overlap should be tested against real questions, not chosen from a universal rule.

Document permissions must also survive indexing. A passage from one customer's account should never be retrieved for another customer merely because the wording matches. Filtering by access rights should happen before text is sent to the model. This is a data security requirement, not something a prompt can reliably repair later.

5. Stage two: find the right passages

When a user asks a question, the system may search using the original wording or a rewritten query. Rewriting can add synonyms, but it can also remove important conditions. A question about orders placed before June must retain that date constraint. A question about a specific product must retain its exact identifier.

Keyword retrieval works well for exact terms. Dense retrieval may find a passage with similar meaning but different wording. Hybrid retrieval combines their strengths. Some systems rerank the candidate passages by reading each one with the question. Reranking can improve the order of results, but it cannot recover a relevant document that the earlier search missed.

Choosing how many passages to return creates a trade-off. Too few can omit the required exception. Too many can include conflicting versions and irrelevant material. The 2021 work by Izacard and Grave found gains from more passages in its studied setting [3]. Other work found that irrelevant retrieved context can reduce answer accuracy [5]. Both results can be true because the effect depends on the passages and the model.

A useful retrieval review asks four questions. Did the right document enter the index? Did the query find it? Does the selected chunk contain the full rule, including conditions? Is the passage current and authorized? These checks locate the failure stage more precisely than a single search score.

6. Stage three: assemble a usable context

The application builds a model input from the user's question, selected passages, source labels and instructions. The prompt can ask for evidence-based answers, source references and clear limits. It should keep each passage's date and document identifier close to the text so the model can distinguish versions.

Order matters. Liu and colleagues tested models on tasks that required locating information in long inputs. Performance was often strongest when the relevant material appeared near the beginning or end, and weaker when it appeared in the middle [4]. This finding shows why accepting a long context is different from reliably using every part of it. The effect must still be tested on the actual models and tasks in use.

Duplicates also matter. Six near-identical excerpts from one page may appear to provide strong support while hiding a conflicting update elsewhere. Deduplication and source diversity can help, provided they do not remove necessary context. Clear section boundaries and a consistent citation format reduce confusion.

Retrieved text is data, not a source of new instructions. A web page may contain words that tell the model to ignore the user or reveal private information. The application must keep trusted instructions separate from untrusted document content. It should test malicious passages and limit any tool actions the model can take. A source's relevance does not give it authority to control the assistant.

7. Stage four: answer, verify or abstain

The model now uses the question and passages to generate an answer. It can summarize, compare and combine information. The risk is that it can also add details that are plausible but absent from the evidence. A hallucination in this setting is a generated claim that is false or unsupported for the intended task.

RAG can reduce this risk, but it does not remove it. The model may favor a remembered figure over the retrieved one. It may combine rules from different products or infer a legal exception that no source states. When a question requires several steps, an early unsupported assumption can affect the whole answer.

A practical answer policy has three outcomes. Answer when the evidence directly supports the requested fact. Answer with a clearly marked limitation when only part of the question is supported. Abstain when the needed evidence is missing, contradictory or too weak for the risk. Abstention is particularly important when a wrong answer could affect money, health, safety or legal rights.

Research on Self-RAG explored a model that decides when retrieval is useful and critiques its use of evidence. Its ICLR 2024 results reported gains on several tested tasks [6]. Adaptive retrieval is promising, but self-checking by the same model is not a substitute for independent source review and evaluation.

8. Visual explanation: a chain with separate failure points

These failures need different repairs. If the correct document was never indexed, change ingestion. If it was indexed but never returned, improve retrieval. If it was returned but ignored, inspect the prompt and generator. If the answer cites an obsolete policy, fix version selection and source authority.

For debugging, save the document version, candidate passages, selected context and final response, subject to privacy rules. Stage-level records show which component failed. Without them, teams may keep changing the model even when the actual problem is a missing document.

9. Worked example: a policy exception

Illustrative policy, not a real retailer's terms. A store states: “Unopened standard items may be returned within 30 days. Custom-made items cannot be returned unless damaged on delivery.” A user asks whether a custom-made desk can be returned after 12 days because they changed their mind.

If search returns only the first sentence, a model may answer yes because 12 is less than 30. The calculation is correct, but the conclusion is wrong. The search omitted the decisive exception. If both sentences were retrieved and the model still answered yes, the failure would be in evidence use or generation.

Under this illustrative policy, changing one's mind is not enough to return a custom-made item. A grounded answer should identify the custom-made exception and explain that the damage condition does not apply to the stated reason. It should not invent consumer-law rights or assume facts about the item.

A useful test asks three related questions: one about an unopened standard item, one about an undamaged custom item, and one about a custom item damaged on delivery. This contrast set tests whether the system follows the exact conditions rather than repeating the most common rule. The test is more informative than asking the same simple question many times.

10. Worked example: three different accuracy measures

Illustrative numbers. Suppose reviewers prepare 100 questions and identify a required evidence passage for each. The retriever returns that passage among its top five results for 88 questions. Its retrieval recall at five is 88/100, or 88%. For those 88 questions, the generator answers 75 correctly. For the other 12 questions, it happens to answer two correctly from general knowledge. Overall answer accuracy is (75 + 2)/100 = 77%.

Now reviewers inspect the citations. Only 68 of the 77 correct answers are supported by the passages cited beside their claims. The system therefore produces correct, visibly supported answers for 68% of the full test set. The remaining nine correct answers may still be useful, but the citations do not establish their evidence basis.

These numbers must be measured separately. Retrieval and generation errors are not independent, and a model can sometimes answer correctly without the nominated passage. Multiplying a retrieval score by a generation score would hide those relationships.

If the system can decline to answer, measure coverage, the share of questions it attempts, as well as accuracy on answered questions. Answering only easy questions can raise conditional accuracy while reducing usefulness. Answering everything can raise coverage but create unacceptable errors. The right balance depends on the task's risk.

11. What the studies establish

Lewis and colleagues showed that retrieval and generation can be combined for knowledge-intensive tasks [1]. Karpukhin and colleagues showed that learned dense retrieval can improve passage recall on their tested datasets [2]. Izacard and Grave found that a generator could use multiple retrieved passages effectively on the benchmarks they studied [3]. These are experimental findings with specific models, data and measures.

Other research reveals limits. “Lost in the Middle” found that the position of relevant information in long inputs affected model performance [4]. Yoran and colleagues examined how irrelevant context can reduce answer quality and tested ways to improve robustness [5]. Self-RAG explored adaptive retrieval and model self-critique [6].

RAGAS proposed automated measures for retrieval-and-generation systems, including evidence relevance and faithfulness [7]. Automated evaluation can make repeated testing faster, but the evaluator can also make mistakes. Important claims and disputed cases still need human review or checks against reliable reference data.

The strong conclusion is conditional: retrieval can help when the right sources are available, the search finds them, and the model uses them correctly. The broad claim that adding a vector database makes an assistant factual is not supported. No cited paper establishes one universal chunk size, search method or model for all document collections.

12. Competing designs and trade-offs

Keyword searchExact names, codes and phrasesMay miss paraphrases
Dense searchMeaning matters more than exact wordsCan miss identifiers

More passagesEvidence is spread across documentsNoise and cost increase
Full-document contextCorpus is short and stableContext cost and position effects
Adaptive retrievalSome questions need several searchesHarder to test and control

One engineering approach uses a simple retriever and a strong generator. It is easy to maintain and can be sufficient for a small, clean document set. Another uses hybrid retrieval, reranking, query decomposition and claim-level verification. That design may solve difficult cases, but it adds latency, expense and failure modes.

Some tasks do not need retrieval at all. A short, stable document may fit directly into a model's context. Structured data queries may be safer through a database operation. Fine-tuning can teach terminology and response style, but it is not usually the best way to keep changing records current.

Choose based on measured errors, not architectural fashion. Begin with the simplest system that meets the required accuracy, privacy and cost limits. Add a component only when it addresses a demonstrated failure. The aim is a dependable answer, not the longest pipeline.

13. Why this matters and how to test it

RAG can make large collections of knowledge easier to use. It can help employees find current procedures, researchers locate original studies, and customers understand product rules. It can also expose weak document management: conflicting versions and missing definitions become visible when people ask precise questions. The benefit depends on source quality as much as model quality.

A practical test set should include ordinary lookups, dated rules, exceptions, multi-document questions, conflicting sources and questions with no answer in the collection. For each, record the expected answer or abstention and the passage that supports it. Check whether that passage was indexed, retrieved and used correctly. Review citations at the level of individual claims.

Repeat tests after changing the model, chunking, search, prompt or document versions. Include permission tests with users who have different access rights. Add deliberately irrelevant passages and check whether they distract the model. Track response delay and operating cost alongside correctness.

The lasting principle is that fluency and evidence are separate properties. A well-designed assistant makes the evidence path visible and admits uncertainty when it cannot establish a claim. That remains true even when future models have larger context windows or better reasoning.

Key lessons

  1. RAG adds external documents to a language model; it does not guarantee truth.
  2. Source quality, dates and permissions matter before retrieval begins.
  3. Chunk boundaries can separate a rule from its exception.
  4. Keyword and dense search solve different matching problems.
  5. More passages can increase recall and also add noise.
  6. A model can ignore correct evidence even when it is present.
  7. A citation must support the exact claim it accompanies.
  8. Retrieval recall, answer correctness and faithfulness are different measures.
  9. Abstention is useful when evidence is too weak for the risk.
  10. Test each pipeline stage and track failures after changes.

Knowledge check: five questions with answers

1. What is parametric memory?

Information reflected in a model's trained weights. An external document index is non-parametric memory.

2. Why can an answer be wrong even when retrieval succeeds?

The generator can overlook a condition, mix incompatible passages or add unsupported details.

3. Does a long context guarantee use of all evidence?

No. Research shows position effects in tested models, and the effect must be checked for the target task.

4. Why inspect citations separately from correctness?

A correct claim may cite an unrelated source. That citation does not establish the claim's basis.

5. When should a system abstain?

When necessary evidence is missing, contradictory, unauthorized or too weak for the decision's risk.
