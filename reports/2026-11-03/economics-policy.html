Can High-Quality Early Childhood Education Pay for Itself?

Which early-years programmes create lasting public value, and what must governments pay for?

Executive summary

Public funding for early childhood education is often justified by the claim that early support can improve health, learning and later earnings. The claim has a sound basis, but the size of the return depends on programme quality, age, duration and the schools that children enter next. Long-run studies of the Perry Preschool and Abecedarian projects report important gains, yet both programmes were intensive and served small, specific groups. The large Head Start evaluation found clear gains during preschool, with many test-score differences smaller by the end of first grade. A randomized study of Tennessee pre-kindergarten found a more difficult pattern: early gains faded and some later outcomes favoured the comparison group. These results do not support one fixed return for every preschool place. They show why governments must fund trained staff, safe care, family links and strong primary schools. India’s Aadharshila and Navchetana frameworks set out a national approach, while official school data still exclude many Anganwadi and private kindergarten places. This guide explains the mechanisms, costs, evidence and limits. Two marked illustrative calculations show how to test a public investment without treating assumptions as proven returns.

Central question and decision rule

Central question: Can public spending on high-quality early childhood education create enough lasting gains in health, learning, family welfare and future income to justify its cost?

The answer is conditional. A programme must improve the care and learning that a child would otherwise receive. It must reach children who can benefit and keep its quality as it grows. The next stage of education must also support the gains. A good preschool cannot compensate for years of weak teaching, unsafe classrooms or poor health services.

Use four tests: child development during the programme; gains after entry to primary school; later outcomes such as education, health or earnings; and benefits compared with the full public cost. A positive result on the first test does not prove success on the other three. Also ask who receives the benefit. Average gains can hide gaps for rural, poor, disabled or language-minority children.

1. How early education became public policy

Governments support early-years services for several reasons: child protection, nutrition, support for working parents and preparation for school. These aims overlap, but they are not identical. A safe child-care place can help a parent work. A learning programme can build language and social skills. A nutrition service can improve health. A strong system must understand all three.

In the United States, Head Start began in 1965 as part of the War on Poverty. The HighScope Perry Preschool experiment began in Michigan in the 1960s. The Carolina Abecedarian Project began in the 1970s and tested full-time educational care from infancy. These projects shaped the debate because researchers followed participants for decades. They were intensive programmes, not short courses.

Policy later moved from small demonstrations to large systems. That shift created a new question: can a programme keep the same quality when it reaches thousands of centres? Scale changes staffing, buildings, language, travel and local management. India placed early childhood care and education within the foundational-stage system in its National Education Policy 2020. The Ministry of Women and Child Development launched Poshan Bhi Padhai Bhi in May 2023 and issued Navchetana and Aadharshila frameworks in 2024. [8]

2. Terms that make the evidence clear

Early childhood care and education (ECCE) covers care, health, protection and learning in the early years. Structural quality includes staff training, group size, safe buildings and materials. Process quality describes what children experience each day: warm interaction, talk, play, responsive care and support for language. Good structures help. They do not guarantee good daily practice.

Randomised controlled trial (RCT) means eligible people are assigned by chance to an offer or a comparison group. It can reduce selection bias. External validity asks whether a result applies to other places and groups. Fade-out means an early measured gain becomes smaller over time. It does not prove that every benefit has disappeared.

Social rate of return estimates the annual return to society after costs and benefits are counted. Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative use of the same money, staff or land. A programme should be compared with realistic alternatives, not only with doing nothing.

3. How an early-years programme works

  1. Reach the child. Offer a safe, affordable place at a useful time and distance. A place that families cannot reach has little value.
  2. Build trust. Staff notice a child's signals, respond to needs and help the child feel safe. Parents need clear information and a way to raise concerns.
  3. Support language and thinking. Adults talk, read, sing, ask questions and guide play. Children sort, build, count and solve simple problems.
  4. Support health. Meals, growth checks, immunisation links and referrals can remove barriers to learning.
  5. Build social skills. Children practise sharing, turn-taking and managing frustration with adult guidance.
  6. Work with families. Staff share simple home activities and identify language, disability or health needs.
  7. Connect to school. Primary schools should build on early learning rather than repeat it or move too fast.
  8. Measure and improve. Managers track attendance, staff support, development and safety. They use results to improve service.

These steps form a chain. Toys without trained adults may have little effect. Skilled staff cannot help much if attendance is very low. Gains can also weaken if the next school does not build on them. Evaluation must study the whole pathway.

4. Diagram: the route from care to later outcomes

There are two main routes to public value. The child route may improve learning and health. The family route may give parents more time to work or study. These effects overlap but should be measured separately. A centre that closes before a parent's shift ends may have little effect on employment.

5. What quality means in practice

Quality is not the same as an attractive building or a large set of teaching materials. Children need adults who can respond to them. A strong service has enough staff, manageable group sizes, regular training and supervision. It provides safe water, toilets, ventilation and protection from injury. It has referral links for health and disability support.

Play-based learning does not mean adults leave children alone. An adult plans an activity, observes, asks questions and gives help when needed. A block game can teach shape, balance, language and turn-taking. A story can support vocabulary and memory. The activity should have a purpose but remain flexible.

Staff conditions matter. Low pay, heavy paperwork and unstable contracts can cause turnover. A short course does not prove that a worker can use a method with a group. Training needs practice, feedback and follow-up. Inclusion is also part of quality: children with disabilities may need adapted materials or referrals, and local languages should be treated as an asset.

6. Worked example one: the cost of a cohort

Illustrative calculation only. Assume a local government offers two years of early education to 1,000 children. It spends ₹25,000 per child per year on staff, meals, materials, building use and supervision.

1,000 children × ₹25,000 × 2 years = ₹5 crore.

Assume 800 children attend often enough to receive the planned service. The cost per regular attendee is ₹5 crore ÷ 800 = ₹62,500 over two years. The cost per enrolled child is ₹50,000. Both figures are correct, but they answer different questions. The first describes the budget per enrolment. The second describes the cost for each child who receives regular exposure.

This does not say that 800 children improved or that the programme caused a benefit. It shows why attendance matters. A programme with high enrolment but low attendance can look efficient while providing little service. A budget should also report staff vacancies, opening days, meals and additional support.

7. What the Perry Preschool study found

The HighScope Perry Preschool Project was a small experiment in Ypsilanti, Michigan. It enrolled young children from disadvantaged families in the 1960s. The treatment combined preschool sessions with home visits. Researchers followed participants for decades. The sample was small and the programme was intensive.

Heckman, Moon, Pinto, Savelyev and Yavitz re-examined the experiment in a paper first published on 3 August 2010. They studied departures from the planned random assignment, the small sample and the testing of many outcomes. After those adjustments, they still found statistically significant and economically important effects for men and women. They also examined how far the sample represented other groups. [1]

A related rate-of-return analysis was issued as an NBER working paper in November 2009 and published in the Journal of Public Economics in 2010. The authors estimated social rates of return generally between 7% and 10% under their preferred assumptions. Many estimates were lower than earlier claims. This is not a universal return for every preschool. It is an estimate for one intervention, population and set of long-run outcomes. [2]

8. What the Abecedarian follow-up found

The Carolina Abecedarian Project tested high-quality educational child care for infants from low-income families. The original sample included 111 infants. A follow-up published in 2012 examined outcomes at age 30 for 101 participants. The treatment group attained more years of education. The study found mixed evidence for economic outcomes and little evidence of treatment-related differences in criminal involvement. [3]

This is an important distinction. A programme can improve education without changing every adult outcome. The small sample also means that estimates can be uncertain. Abecedarian was an intensive service that began in infancy and continued for years. It was not a test of a standard part-time preschool. Policy makers should describe the treatment and population, not only the headline result.

9. What the Head Start evaluation found

The US Head Start Impact Study was a large randomised evaluation. It followed nearly 5,000 children who applied to programmes in areas where demand exceeded available places. The final report was published in January 2010. It found positive effects on several school-readiness measures during the programme year. By the end of first grade, many differences between the offer group and the comparison group were small or no longer statistically significant. [4]

This is often called fade-out. A smaller test-score gap does not prove that a child gained nothing. Children in the comparison group may have entered other preschool services. Later schools may have taught similar material. Tests may miss health or family effects. Still, the result warns that early gains can weaken when later services do not build on them.

Bailey, Sun and Timpe published a long-run analysis of Head Start in the American Economic Review in December 2021. They used historical programme roll-out and linked records in a quasi-experimental design. They estimated gains in schooling and later education outcomes. This method differs from random assignment and depends on assumptions about historical roll-out and comparison groups. [5]

10. Why the Tennessee results matter

The Tennessee Voluntary Pre-K study gives a serious warning against simple claims about universal preschool. Researchers randomly assigned offers to nearly 3,000 children who applied to oversubscribed programmes. The 2018 paper followed outcomes through third grade. Children offered a place did better on some tests at the end of pre-K. The comparison group caught up. By third grade, the full randomized sample showed weaker achievement for the pre-K group on some measures, with some unfavourable behaviour outcomes. [6]

This does not prove that preschool harms children in general. It describes one programme and the later school settings that followed it. Researchers raised questions about the quality and fit of instruction. If pre-K repeats material that children meet again in kindergarten, or later teaching is weak, early gains may not grow.

A 2021 statewide study by Pion and Lipsey used a regression-discontinuity design with 5,189 children across two cohorts. It found positive effects on cognitive skills at kindergarten entry, with larger effects for literacy than language. This supports a positive effect at the point of transition. It does not remove the later questions raised by the randomized follow-up. [7]

11. Why results differ across programmes

Programmes differ in age of entry, hours, group size, staff training, family support and the quality of the comparison service. A full-day infant programme is not the same treatment as a few hours of preschool. A study in one city cannot show what will happen in a rural district with different languages and staffing.

Children also start from different positions. A child with limited access to books or health care may gain more from a high-quality service. This is a reason to address unequal access, not to label a child as deficient. Timing matters too. Some gains appear in language soon after the programme. Other effects may appear later through school attendance, health or parent work.

Comparison groups matter. In a place where many children already attend private preschool, a new public place may add less than in a place with little safe care. A trial measures the added effect of the offer against what families would otherwise do. It does not measure the value of all early learning in society.

12. India's early childhood policy and data

The Ministry of Women and Child Development says Poshan Bhi Padhai Bhi was launched on 10 May 2023 under Saksham Anganwadi and Mission Poshan 2.0. It aims to strengthen early stimulation and pre-primary education for children from birth to six years. Navchetana covers birth to three years. Aadharshila covers ages three to six and sets out play-based and activity-based learning. The ministry describes training for Anganwadi workers and a curriculum with almost 150 activities. [8]

The official UDISE+ 2024–25 report records 14,047,080 children in pre-primary classes in recognised schools. It states that this figure excludes ECCE enrolment in Anganwadi centres and standalone private kindergartens. It is therefore not a national count of all children receiving early education. This matters when governments calculate coverage, cost or need. [9]

Some children attend Anganwadi centres, some attend private kindergartens and some receive care at home. A reliable system needs clear definitions and privacy-safe records. It should avoid counting a child twice and should report eligible children who do not attend. National frameworks provide a basis for common standards, but states and local bodies still need funds for staff, training, safe spaces, nutrition and monitoring.

13. How to calculate public value

A cost-benefit study should count staff pay, training, buildings, repairs, meals, transport, materials, administration and monitoring. A free building is not costless: it has another possible use and needs maintenance. Benefits can include better health, learning, school completion and later earnings. Parents may gain time to work or study. Government may receive more tax revenue if earnings rise.

Benefits occur at different times and may overlap. Higher earnings can already reflect more years of education. A study must avoid counting the same benefit twice. Researchers discount future benefits because a rupee received later is not equal to a rupee available today. Results should be shown across more than one discount rate because long-run estimates can be sensitive to this choice.

Distribution also matters. A programme may create a modest average gain but a large gain for children with the least access. Cost-benefit analysis can inform a decision, but it cannot decide how society should value fairness. Governments should report both the total return and who receives it.

14. Worked example two: a present-value test

Illustrative calculation only. Assume a programme costs ₹1,00,000 per child today. Assume it creates an additional social benefit of ₹12,000 per year for 15 years, in today's purchasing power. Use a real discount rate of 3%. The present-value factor for 15 equal annual benefits is about 11.94.

Present value = ₹12,000 × 11.94 = about ₹1,43,300.

Net present value = ₹1,43,300 − ₹1,00,000 = about ₹43,300.

Under these assumptions, the programme passes the simple test. The annual benefit is an input chosen to show the calculation. It is not an estimate from a real programme. A real study must estimate additional outcomes, convert only defensible effects into money, account for uncertainty and compare the programme with the best alternative use of funds.

If the annual benefit were ₹8,000, its present value would be about ₹95,500 and net value would be negative. A robust case should test reasonable values for attendance, effect, cost and discount rate.

15. Serious objections and competing choices

Small trials may not scale. This is correct. Perry and Abecedarian were not cheap national systems. They show what an intensive treatment can do in a specific setting. They do not provide a ready-made effect for a large public programme. Governments should test delivery at scale and report when quality changes.

Gains may fade. Head Start and Tennessee show that early test-score effects can shrink or change. A programme should be judged as part of a child's learning path. Primary schools must build on early skills. Governments should also measure health, attendance and family outcomes.

Cash transfers may be a better use of funds. Cash can reduce hardship and give families choice. It does not always create access to trained care where services are missing. The right comparison is local. Some places may need income support first. Others may need both cash and public services.

Formal preschool can push academic work too early. A rigid curriculum can reduce play and raise stress. The answer is age-appropriate learning through interaction and play. A curriculum should guide adults while leaving room for children to explore.

Families already provide care. Family care has value and should not be treated as a problem to replace. Public support can help parents while providing services that are hard for one household to supply, such as trained staff, nutrition and safe space.

16. How to design a credible programme

  1. Define the service. State whether it is a crèche, preschool, home-visiting programme or a combined offer.
  2. Fund people first. Set pay, staffing and group-size standards. Provide training, coaching and time for interaction.
  3. Use a local curriculum. Build language, play, movement, early number skills and social development into daily routines.
  4. Link care to health. Set clear referral routes for nutrition, medical and disability support.
  5. Make access practical. Choose hours and locations that fit family needs. Track attendance and absence.
  6. Support families. Share simple activities and listen to parents. Avoid complex forms for basic help.
  7. Protect the next stage. Work with primary schools so that teaching continues from the child's current level.
  8. Evaluate before expansion. Test local models, publish costs and outcomes, and change the programme when evidence shows a problem.

17. How to measure results over time

Start with implementation data: enrolment, attendance, opening days, staff vacancies, turnover, training, group size, safety incidents and meals. These data show whether the service was delivered. They do not show whether it improved child outcomes.

Measure development with age-appropriate tools for language, early number, motor, social and emotional skills. Avoid tests that reward familiarity with one language or culture. Keep assessments short and use them to improve support, not to deny a place.

Use a credible comparison where possible. An RCT may suit a fair phased roll-out. A difference-in-differences or regression-discontinuity design may help when randomisation is not possible. Each method has assumptions. Publish them and explain where the design may fail.

Follow children after the programme. Measure transition to school, attendance, learning, health and family outcomes. Link records only with a lawful basis and strong privacy protection. Report results by income, location, disability and language where sample sizes allow.

18. Wider effects on families and the economy

Reliable child care can change parents' work choices, especially for mothers. The effect depends on opening hours, location, trust and price. A centre that closes before a parent's shift ends may have little effect on employment.

Early-years services create jobs for educators, cooks, health workers and support staff. These jobs need training and fair pay. If a system depends on poorly paid women doing more care work, it can reproduce inequality. Labour standards are part of quality and cost.

Local demand can support suppliers of food, learning materials, maintenance and transport. Procurement should let small firms compete while meeting safety standards. Large contracts may lower costs but can make a local system depend on one provider.

Better learning and health may support productivity and public revenue, but these gains take years and are uncertain. They should not be used to promise that a programme will pay for itself in a fixed period. The case can also rest on child rights and equity.

19. Eight lessons to retain

  1. Early education can create long-run value, but effects depend on quality and context.
  2. Small intensive trials do not give a guaranteed return for a national programme.
  3. Process quality depends on daily interaction, trained staff and safe care.
  4. Early test-score gains can fade when later schools do not build on them.
  5. Child outcomes and parent employment are separate routes to public value.
  6. UDISE+ pre-primary data exclude Anganwadi and standalone kindergarten enrolment.
  7. Cost-benefit analysis must avoid double counting and show uncertainty.
  8. Measure the service children receive, not only the places announced.

Five knowledge-check questions and answers

1. Why does a positive preschool test result not prove a high long-run return?

It measures one outcome at one point in time. Later school quality, family conditions, programme cost and other outcomes affect the full return.

2. What is the difference between structural and process quality?

Structural quality includes staff training, group size and safe buildings. Process quality describes the daily interactions and learning children experience.

3. What did the Tennessee randomized study warn policy makers about?

Some early gains faded, and some later outcomes favoured the comparison group. The next school stage and programme design can affect long-run results.

4. Why is the UDISE+ 2024–25 pre-primary figure not India's total ECCE enrolment?

The report excludes Anganwadi ECCE centres and standalone private kindergartens. It covers pre-primary classes in recognised schools.

5. What should a government measure before it expands a programme?

Full cost, attendance, staff quality, safety, child development, family outcomes and results after children enter primary school.
