Can Repairability Become a Profitable Business Model?

When can fixing a product create value for the customer, the business and the environment?

Executive summary

Repair can reduce material demand and create skilled local work. Yet a repairable product does not create a repair business by itself. Customers need a clear diagnosis, a fair price, available parts, trusted technicians and software support. Firms need enough repair volume, reliable suppliers, useful data and control of warranty costs. Research shows that repair can avoid much of the impact of making a new device, but the result depends on the part replaced and the life added. Studies of repair behaviour also show that access, cost and ease matter as much as good intentions. The European Union now combines product design rules with a wider right-to-repair framework. India has a government repair information portal and an e-waste regime that recognises registered refurbishers, but these measures are not the same as a broad statutory right to repair. This guide explains the technical, commercial and policy conditions for a viable repair model. Two clearly illustrative calculations test carbon savings and service economics. The key business test is whether repair extends useful life at a fair total cost while meeting safety, quality and data-security needs.

Central question and decision rule

Central question: Can a company earn a fair return by keeping products in use for longer, while reducing the need for new materials and avoiding unsafe or poor-quality repairs?

Repair works best when faults can be diagnosed, parts are available, labour time is predictable and customers trust the result. The product must also have useful life left after repair. A cheap fix to a device without security updates may not create much value.

Use five tests: does repair restore a product the customer still needs; is its total price below a suitable replacement; can the job be done safely and on time; does it extend useful life enough to avoid some new production; and can the business earn a margin after parts, labour, warranty and support? Keep customer value, business value and environmental value separate. One does not prove the others.

1. From local repair shops to product-life services

Repair was once a normal part of ownership. Local technicians fixed radios, watches, bicycles and household machines. Many products used common parts and ordinary tools. As mass production expanded, some goods became cheaper to replace. Electronics became smaller and more complex. Adhesives, sealed cases, software locks and integrated parts made some repairs harder.

Manufacturers built service networks to protect quality and warranty claims. Independent repair shops remained important, but they often lacked parts, diagnostic tools or service information. Online sales made it easy to compare a new product price with a repair quote. Repair required diagnosis, travel and uncertainty.

Environmental policy now asks firms to consider a product's full life. Repair, reuse, refurbishment, remanufacturing and recycling are different options. A product-life business can earn revenue from maintenance, parts, repair, resale and take-back instead of relying only on new-unit sales.

2. Terms that matter

Repairability is how easily a product can be restored to working order. It depends on access, tools, parts, instructions, software and skill. Durability is the ability to keep working over time. A durable product may rarely fail. A repairable product may fail but can be fixed.

Refurbishment is the inspection, repair and preparation of a used product for another period of use or resale. Remanufacturing is a more extensive process that restores a product or component to a defined standard. Total cost of ownership includes purchase, use, maintenance, repair, downtime and end-of-life costs. Life-cycle assessment estimates environmental impacts across a stated product life.

First-time fix rate is the share of jobs solved in one service event. Obsolescence is the point at which a product is no longer useful or supported. A device may still work but lose security updates. A machine may be repairable but fail a safety standard. Physical operation is not the same as safe, useful life.

3. How a repair business creates value

  1. Design for service. Use parts and fasteners that can be opened and replaced without damage.
  2. Diagnose the fault. Use error codes, test tools and a clear intake process to avoid wrong-part orders.
  3. Secure parts. Stock common items and set lead times for less common parts.
  4. Quote before work. State fault, price, time, warranty and risks. Give the customer a choice.
  5. Repair safely. Use trained staff and protect personal data on devices.
  6. Test and return. Check the product and record the parts and work completed.
  7. Learn from failures. Send repeated fault data to design and quality teams.
  8. Recover value at end of use. Offer take-back, refurbishment or approved recycling.

The customer may avoid a new purchase. The repairer earns a fee. The manufacturer may retain trust and learn about failures. The environment may benefit if repair avoids new production and delays disposal. The model fails if work is poor, the price is too high or the product is replaced soon after repair.

4. Diagram: the repair service loop

If the fault is dangerous, the product should not return to use. If parts are unavailable or the product has no security support, the repairer should explain the limit. Honest refusal protects customers and the service's reputation.

5. The economics of a repair

A repair quote must cover more than the part. It includes diagnosis, technician time, tools, training, rent, software, logistics, warranty risk and support. A service with many short jobs may use staff time better than one with rare, complex jobs. A high repair price can reduce demand even when the repair is technically sound.

Parts supply is often the main constraint. Too little stock creates delays. Too much stock ties up cash and may become waste when models change. Common parts and long-term supply commitments can reduce this risk. Diagnosis also matters: if a repairer cannot identify the fault before opening a device, the job may need repeat visits. A disclosed inspection fee can cover some cost.

Warranty and rework can erase margin. Record repeat visits by product, fault and technician. Track first-time fix rate and claims. There can also be a conflict between repair revenue and new-product sales. A manufacturer can reduce this conflict by earning from service, parts, maintenance contracts or refurbished products. It must still avoid unnecessary repairs.

6. Worked example one: a carbon test

Illustrative calculation only. Assume a new device causes 60 kg of CO₂-equivalent emissions from production and distribution. A repair uses a part and local service that cause 10 kg. Assume the repair gives two more years of use and avoids a new purchase.

Potential emissions avoided = 60 kg − 10 kg = 50 kg CO₂-equivalent.

Now allow for repair failure. If only one in four repairs extends use enough to avoid a new purchase, expected avoided production is 0.25 × 60 = 15 kg. After the 10 kg repair impact, expected net saving is 5 kg. Under these assumptions, the break-even success rate is 10 ÷ 60, or 16.7%.

This test leaves out changes in electricity use, transport, spare-part production and the chance that a customer buys another device anyway. It shows why a company must measure added useful life, not only the number of jobs completed.

7. What life-cycle studies show

Cordella and colleagues published a smartphone life-cycle analysis on 31 March 2021. Material extraction and component production were major sources of impact. In their European model, extending the replacement cycle from two to three years could reduce annual carbon impact by 23% to 30%, depending on whether repair was needed. These are scenario results, not a guarantee for every phone. [1]

A 2023 study of independent repair businesses in Lima, Peru, used fieldwork and life-cycle assessment for phones and tablets. It found that repair can conserve carbon and water compared with making a new device, but results vary by the part repaired. Some screen repairs using replacement parts approached the impact of a new device. Repairs using components already in the market could save about 10% of total global-warming impact in the studied cases. [2]

The evidence supports repair as a useful strategy, but not as a universal rule. A small part may avoid much new production. A large module may use more resources. A repair that fails quickly may not extend service. A device that uses much more energy than a new model also needs a wider comparison. An LCA should state its functional unit and include parts, travel, use and end-of-life treatment.

8. Why customers do or do not repair

Good intentions do not always lead to repair. Parajuly and colleagues surveyed 922 people in 14 countries who had experience with public repair events. The 2023 study found that values and attitudes shaped intention, but ability, service access, cost and ease were also important. The volunteers were not a representative sample of all consumers. [3]

Roskladka, Jaegler and Miragliotta published a study on 20 December 2023 that identified 26 consumer barriers. Important barriers included diagnosis cost, repair cost, lack of parts, lack of service access and lack of information. Awareness campaigns alone cannot solve these problems. A customer cannot choose repair if the service is too costly, slow or hard to find. [4]

Reduce uncertainty with fixed prices for common faults, clear diagnosis, a stated warranty and simple booking. Show common part prices and repair times. Explain when a repair is not worth the cost.

9. Can a repairability score change product design?

France introduced a repairability index for selected products. Its government published a durability-index policy page on 9 April 2024 and updated it on 30 June 2025. In 2025, the durability index began replacing the repairability index for televisions and washing machines. This reflects a wider idea: a product should resist failure as well as be easy to repair. [5]

Barros and Dimla studied French and iFixit smartphone scores in a paper first published on 24 April 2023. They found that a high score could reflect good service and information even when product design was difficult to open. This shows that repairability depends on design and service. A single score can hide different strengths and weaknesses. [6]

A 2023 IEEE conference study used a choice experiment with 201 respondents. It estimated that participants would pay an average extra €65.70 for a one-point increase in a smartphone repairability score. This was stated preference, not actual purchase data. It suggests repairability can matter at the point of choice, but real demand must be tested in sales. [7]

10. Five repair business models

Manufacturer service network. The brand controls training, parts and warranty work. It can protect quality, but has high fixed costs and may limit access for independent repairers.

Independent repair shop. A local business serves several brands. It may respond quickly and have lower overhead. It needs broad skills, reliable parts and clear rules for safety and warranty.

Repair subscription or maintenance contract. A customer or business pays a regular fee for inspection, repair and support. This creates predictable demand, but the provider must price repeat failures and heavy use.

Refurbishment and resale. A company collects used products, repairs and tests them, then sells them with a warranty. It must manage grading, data deletion, returns and unsold stock.

Product-as-a-service. The customer pays for access or performance rather than ownership. The provider retains the product and has a reason to maintain it. This creates capital and asset-tracking risks. It is not automatically sustainable if it increases consumption or transport.

Models can work together. A manufacturer can supply parts to independent shops, collect failed units and resell refurbished products. Contracts must state who owns the product, who pays for a failure and who is liable for unsafe work.

11. Worked example two: repair-shop unit economics

Illustrative calculation only. Assume a shop completes 1,200 paid jobs in one year. Average revenue is ₹2,500. Average direct costs per job are ₹900 for parts, ₹650 for labour, ₹250 for logistics and warranty, and ₹500 for allocated rent, tools and support.

Logistics and warranty₹250₹3,00,000
Rent, tools and support₹500₹6,00,000
Surplus before tax and finance₹200₹2,40,000

The shop earns ₹200 per job. If set-up cost was ₹6 lakh, simple payback would be 2.5 years if volume and margin stayed constant. This is not a forecast. It ignores seasonality, working capital and bad debt. If warranty claims add ₹150 per job, surplus falls to ₹50. If better diagnosis saves ₹100 in labour, surplus rises to ₹300. Small changes matter.

12. What EU rules require

Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/1670 sets ecodesign rules for smartphones, mobile phones, cordless phones and slate tablets placed on the EU market from 20 June 2025. The rules include battery durability, disassembly, repair information, spare parts and software updates. The Commission says covered batteries must withstand at least 800 charge cycles while retaining 80% of initial capacity. Key parts must be available for seven years after a model leaves the market, with delivery within five to ten working days. [8]

Directive (EU) 2024/1799 entered into force on 30 July 2024. Member States had to transpose it into national law by 31 July 2026. It sets a wider framework for repair of goods covered by EU law. If a consumer chooses repair during the seller's liability period, that period is extended by at least 12 months. National laws and enforcement details still matter. [9]

These rules do not require every product to be repaired in every case. A business that sells into the EU must check the product category, national implementation, parts duties and consumer information requirements.

13. India's repair and e-waste framework

India's Department of Consumer Affairs runs the Right to Repair India portal. It provides information about warranty, service terms, spare parts and service networks for listed brands. It is an information initiative; it should not be described as a broad statutory right to repair for every product. [10]

The E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022 took effect on 1 April 2023 and were amended in 2023 and 2024. They require registration of producers, manufacturers, refurbishers and recyclers under the extended producer responsibility system. They define refurbishing as work that extends the working life of used equipment. Refurbishing can defer part of a producer's recycling obligation for a stated period, but it does not remove the end-of-life recycling duty. [11]

The Global E-waste Monitor 2024, released by ITU and UNITAR on 20 March 2024, estimated 62 million tonnes of e-waste in 2022. It documented 22.3% as formally collected and recycled. Small IT and telecommunications equipment had a documented rate of 22%. These are global figures, not India's national rate. They show why repair, reuse and safe recycling must be connected. [12]

Indian firms can make warranty terms, spare prices, service locations and repair times easy to find. They should use registered refurbishers and recyclers where required. Record how many repairs extend use, how many devices are refurbished and how much material reaches authorised recycling. Do not count every item collected as repaired or recycled.

14. Limits and competing views

Repair can cost more than replacement. This can happen when labour is costly, parts are scarce or a product is old. A customer may also value a new feature or warranty. Do not pressure customers to repair when replacement is safer or better value.

Repair can create new impacts. Parts must be made and transported. A repair may require repeat travel. A failed repair can add cost without extending life. LCA should compare the full service.

Old products can be less efficient or less secure. A refrigerator may use much more electricity than a new model. A phone without security updates may expose its owner to risk. The right decision depends on use-phase impacts and support.

Scores can be incomplete. A score may reward service access while a product remains hard to open. Another may focus on disassembly but ignore part prices. Scores should show their criteria and be tested against real repair rates.

More repair can increase total consumption. Cheap repair may lead people to keep or buy more devices. This rebound effect can reduce the environmental gain. Measure whether repair replaces new production, not only whether repair volume rises.

15. How a company can build a repair offer

  1. Select a narrow range. Start with products that have common faults, available parts and useful life remaining.
  2. Map the customer journey. Record fault reporting, quotes, approvals and collection.
  3. Build a parts plan. Set suppliers, lead times, stock levels and storage rules.
  4. Train staff. Set procedures for electrical safety, batteries, data and quality checks.
  5. Publish prices. State diagnosis fees, common repair prices, time ranges and warranty terms.
  6. Set a repair-or-replace rule. Include price, safety, reliability, software support and remaining life.
  7. Track every job. Record fault, part, labour time, repeat visit and customer outcome.
  8. Review design. Send recurring fault data to product teams and change parts or design when justified.

Run a pilot long enough to include slow months and repeat claims. Compare it with the old service process. Do not claim environmental savings from repair counts alone. Estimate new products avoided and state the assumptions.

16. Metrics that test the model

Track customer measures: quote acceptance, time to repair, price against replacement, satisfaction and complaints. Track operations: first-time fix, technician use, parts availability, waiting days, warranty claims and rework. Track finance: revenue and margin per job, stock cash, contribution by fault and customer acquisition cost.

Track environmental outcomes separately. Record product age, repair type, parts mass, transport and added useful life. Use an LCA method that states the baseline, boundary and functional unit. Estimate whether a new product was actually avoided. Report uncertainty.

Use repair data to improve design. If batteries fail early, review quality and access. If screens break often, review protection and repair method. If parts arrive late, qualify another supplier. Data only helps when product teams act on it.

17. Wider effects on work and supply chains

Repair can create local jobs in diagnosis, electronics, logistics and refurbishment. These roles need training and safe working conditions. Battery repair can create fire and chemical risks. A responsible business provides protective equipment and safe storage.

Independent repair can improve access outside major cities. It also raises questions about counterfeit parts, safety and liability. Shared standards, traceable parts and clear warranties can help. Rules should not make compliance so costly that only large chains can operate.

Longer product life can reduce demand for raw materials and waste. It can change manufacturer revenue toward maintenance, parts and refurbishment. Repair is one part of the system: products that cannot be fixed must still reach safe collection and recycling. Informal repair workers need a transition path, not exclusion without support.

Nine lessons to retain

  1. Repairability and durability are related but different qualities.
  2. A repair business needs parts, diagnosis, skilled labour and trust.
  3. Environmental value depends on added useful life and avoided production.
  4. Life-cycle results vary by part and use phase.
  5. Consumer barriers include cost, parts, service access and uncertainty.
  6. A score must show its criteria and be tested against real outcomes.
  7. EU ecodesign rules and the right-to-repair directive have different scopes.
  8. India's portal is an information service; e-waste rules govern registered actors and end-of-life duties.
  9. Measure customer, business and environmental outcomes separately.

Five knowledge-check questions and answers

1. Why is repairability different from durability?

Durability describes how long a product resists failure. Repairability describes how easily it can be restored after a fault.

2. Why can a repair reduce emissions?

If it extends useful life and avoids a new product, it can avoid some material extraction and manufacturing. Parts, transport and repeat failures still count.

3. What did the repair-behaviour survey find?

Attitudes mattered, but ability, service access, cost and ease were also important. Its participants had experience with repair events, so the sample was not representative of all consumers.

4. How do the EU ecodesign regulation and repair directive differ?

The ecodesign regulation sets product requirements for covered devices. The directive sets a wider repair framework that Member States had to transpose by 31 July 2026.

5. Which measures show whether a repair business is viable?

Track quote acceptance, first-time fix, parts availability, labour time, repeat claims, margin per job and useful life added. Track environmental impact separately.
