Why Is Earth's Magnetic Field Changing—and Could It Flip?

How moving liquid iron makes a magnetic field, how rocks preserve its past, and what current satellite measurements do—and do not—tell us about a future reversal.

Executive summary

Earth's magnetic field changes because the source of most of it is moving liquid metal deep inside the planet. The outer core contains electrically conducting iron-rich liquid. Heat loss, changes in composition and the planet's rotation help drive complex flows. These flows generate electric currents. The currents sustain a magnetic field, and that field changes as the flow changes. Scientists call this process the geodynamo.

Measurements show that the field is not a fixed bar magnet. Magnetic north moves. The field grows stronger in some regions and weaker in others. The South Atlantic Anomaly, a broad region of weak field above the South Atlantic and South America, has expanded in recent satellite records. A 2025 study using eleven years of European Space Agency Swarm data found that the weak-field area below 26,000 nanotesla grew by about 0.9% of Earth's surface area between 2014 and 2025. The same study found that strong-field regions changed in different ways over Canada and Siberia.

These changes are real. They do not prove that a pole reversal is about to happen. Geological records show that Earth's field has reversed many times, but the intervals are irregular. The last full reversal occurred about 780,000 years ago. A reversal takes much longer than a human lifetime. The South Atlantic Anomaly has appeared in past field reconstructions without leading to a reversal. The evidence supports close monitoring, not a date for the next flip.

1. The central question

Why does a compass direction change over time? Why do some satellites pass through a weaker part of the field? Could the north and south magnetic poles exchange places? These questions are connected, but they describe different parts of geomagnetism.

The key is to separate three things: the magnetic field at one place and time, the slow changes in that field, and a full reversal of its dominant polarity. A compass records the local field. A global model describes the large-scale pattern. A reversal is a major change in the direction of the field generated by the core. A local weak patch or a moving magnetic pole is not, by itself, evidence of a reversal.

2. Terms that prevent confusion

Geomagnetic field
The magnetic field measured around Earth. It combines sources inside and outside the planet.
Geographic pole
A point where Earth's rotation axis meets its surface. It is not defined by magnetism.
Magnetic dip pole
A place where the magnetic field points vertically into or out of the ground. It moves and does not have to sit opposite its partner.
Geomagnetic dipole axis
The axis of a simplified bar-magnet model fitted to the large-scale field. It is tilted relative to Earth's rotation axis.
Declination
The angle between geographic north and the horizontal direction shown by a magnetic compass.
Secular variation
Slow change in the main magnetic field over years to centuries.
Geodynamo
The self-sustaining process in which moving, conducting fluid in the outer core generates magnetic fields and electric currents.
Paleomagnetism
The study of magnetic records preserved in rocks, sediments and archaeological materials.
Excursion
A major but temporary departure from the usual field direction. The field returns without completing a lasting polarity reversal.
Polarity reversal
A long-term change in which the dominant north–south magnetic polarity changes sign.
Nanotesla (nT)
A unit of magnetic field strength. One nanotesla is one billionth of a tesla.

3. How scientists discovered a changing field

People used magnetic needles long before they understood Earth's field. A compass does not point to geographic north, and the difference varies by place and time. Navigators recorded it to keep charts accurate.

In the nineteenth century, observatories and repeat surveys showed that the field changed slowly. This was a real change in Earth's magnetic source, not a fault in one compass.

Geologists later found that some rocks had magnetisation that pointed in a direction different from today's field. At first, many researchers questioned whether this was reliable. Better measurements, rock dating and global comparisons showed that the direction had changed many times. In the 1960s, magnetic patterns on the ocean floor connected these reversals to the growth of ocean basins. That evidence became a key test of seafloor spreading and plate tectonics.

4. Where the field comes from

Earth has a solid inner core surrounded by a liquid outer core. The core begins about 2,900 kilometres below the surface. The outer core is mostly iron with lighter elements mixed in. At the pressure and temperature found there, this metal is electrically conducting and liquid. The inner core is solid because pressure changes the conditions under which iron freezes.

NASA's geomagnetic reference material estimates that about 97–99% of the field measured at Earth's surface comes from electric currents in the core. The rest includes magnetism in the crust and fields linked to electric currents in the ionosphere, magnetosphere and oceans. The core-generated part is the main field that changes over years and centuries.

The field is not produced by a permanent magnet buried at the centre. At core temperatures, ordinary iron cannot remain permanently magnetised in that way. The field is produced by electric currents in moving liquid metal. This distinction matters: the field depends on fluid motion and heat flow, so it can change even when the solid planet appears still.

5. How the geodynamo sustains itself

The outer core loses heat as Earth cools. As liquid near the top cools, it becomes denser and can sink. As the inner core grows, freezing releases latent heat and pushes lighter elements into the liquid. These effects help drive buoyant motion. The exact balance of heat and chemical buoyancy is still an active research topic.

Moving liquid iron carries electric charge. When it moves through an existing magnetic field, it can generate electric currents. Those currents create another magnetic field. If the flow is strong and organised enough, the new field can maintain the original field against electrical resistance, which would otherwise make it decay. This feedback is the dynamo process.

Earth's rotation also matters. The Coriolis effect changes how moving fluid is organised. It can help produce column-like flow patterns aligned with the rotation axis. The real core is turbulent and three-dimensional. The field is therefore a changing collection of broad and local patterns, not a simple dipole at every moment.

A 2022 review in Nature Reviews Earth & Environment assessed possible power sources for the dynamo. It concluded that thermal and chemical convection remain the most likely long-term drivers, while noting that uncertain core thermal conductivity affects how easily convection can sustain the field over billions of years. Scientists can model many features of the present dynamo, but they do not yet know every detail of how it has remained active through most of Earth's history.

6. The field measured at the surface

The field at a compass is a sum of several sources. The core produces the largest, broad-scale part. Rocks in the crust add smaller local variations. Electric currents high in the atmosphere and in the space environment add changes that can be rapid, especially during solar storms. Ocean water also moves through the field and creates small induced signals.

Scientists must separate these sources before they can infer what the core is doing. A satellite magnetometer measures the total field at the spacecraft. Researchers use the satellite's position, time, attitude, other instruments and mathematical models to estimate the different contributions. Ground observatories provide long records at fixed places. The two kinds of data support each other.

7. How we measure it today

Ground observatories record long time series at fixed places. Ships and aircraft map magnetic signals over large areas. Satellites provide broad global coverage above the atmosphere. These records support one another.

ESA launched the three-satellite Swarm constellation in November 2013. Its instruments measure magnetic signals from the core, crust, oceans, ionosphere and magnetosphere. Its consistent global record helps scientists track changes over time.

Researchers then build models. The World Magnetic Model (WMM) is one widely used product. It describes the main field and its expected slow change. The 2025 version was released on 17 December 2024 and is valid through 2029. It is used in navigation and heading systems, including ships, aircraft and civilian devices. It does not predict every short-term magnetic disturbance. A solar storm can change the field faster than a five-year navigation model can describe.

8. What Swarm found from 2014 to 2025

A research paper by C.C. Finlay, C. Kloss and N. Gillet, published in Physics of the Earth and Planetary Interiors in November 2025, analysed eleven years of Swarm observations. It focused on the core-generated field between 2014 and 2025. The authors found that the weak-field region in the South Atlantic expanded. For the area below 26,000 nT, the minimum field strength fell from 22,430 nT to 22,094 nT, a decrease of 336 nT. The area enclosed by that contour grew by about 0.9% of Earth's surface area.

The study also found different changes in the northern strong-field regions. The maximum field strength over Canada fell from 58,832 nT to 58,031 nT, a decrease of 801 nT. Over Siberia it rose from 61,359 nT to 61,619 nT, an increase of 260 nT. These patterns show that the field does not simply weaken everywhere at the same rate.

The authors traced the surface changes to evolving patterns at the boundary between the liquid core and the rocky mantle. They found that some reversed magnetic-flux features beneath southern Africa moved westward, while related features beneath the mid-Atlantic moved eastward. The paper also reports changes at low latitudes beneath Indonesia and the western Pacific. These are model-based inferences from satellite data. They are not direct photographs of liquid iron.

ESA reported the South Atlantic result on 13 October 2025. It noted that the weak region had grown by an area close to half the size of continental Europe since 2014. The peer-reviewed paper gives the exact contour-based measurements. The two descriptions refer to the same broad change, but the paper's definition and thresholds should be used for quantitative comparisons.

10. What the South Atlantic Anomaly means

The South Atlantic Anomaly is a region where the magnetic field is weaker than in many other places at the same altitude. It extends over parts of South America and the South Atlantic. It matters because energetic charged particles can reach lower orbital altitudes there than they do over stronger parts of the field. Satellites that pass through the region can receive higher radiation doses. This can cause instrument errors, memory faults or damage if a spacecraft is not designed to handle the exposure.

The anomaly does not mean that the magnetic field has vanished. The field is still present. Nor does the anomaly create a known direct danger to people at Earth's surface. The atmosphere absorbs much of the energetic particle radiation that would otherwise reach the ground. The practical concern is greatest for spacecraft, some high-altitude operations and systems that depend on reliable measurements in space.

NASA and ESA monitor the anomaly because it changes over time. Its shape, strength and location matter for satellite design and for models of the core. The 2025 Swarm study found that the weak region is not a single uniform patch. Its change differs between the area near South America and the area south-west of Africa.

11. Why magnetic north moves

A compass needle aligns with the horizontal part of the local magnetic field. The direction it points is called magnetic north. The magnetic dip pole is the place where the field points straight down or up. The geomagnetic pole is different again: it is calculated from a simplified dipole that best represents the global field. News reports often use the phrase magnetic north pole without saying which definition they mean.

The magnetic poles move because the flow in the outer core changes the field. The north dip pole has moved across the Arctic, and its speed has varied. Navigation systems therefore use a model for a specific location and date. The World Magnetic Model is updated every five years.

Magnetic declination is the angle between true and magnetic north. Compass users must apply the local value for the correct date. Phones can combine magnetic sensors with satellite positioning, but still need a current field model.

12. How rocks record ancient directions

Some minerals contain tiny magnetic grains. When hot lava cools, magnetic minerals such as magnetite can align with the surrounding field. As the rock cools below a mineral's blocking temperature, that alignment becomes locked in. This is called thermoremanent magnetisation. The rock can preserve both the direction and, with careful methods, information about the strength of the ancient field.

Fine magnetic grains can also settle through water and become fixed in sediment. This process can record the field direction, although later compaction, chemical changes and disturbance can alter the signal. Researchers therefore test several samples and use laboratory heating or alternating magnetic fields to remove weaker overprints. They compare the remaining signal with rock age and geological context.

A single rock is not enough to establish a global reversal. The rock may have moved, been reheated or been magnetised again. Researchers need many samples from well-dated sites. They look for consistent direction changes across separate regions and check that the changes match independent dating evidence. This is why paleomagnetism combines physics, geology, chemistry and chronology.

13. The stripes that helped prove plate tectonics

New oceanic crust forms at mid-ocean ridges. Hot magma rises, cools and becomes basalt. Magnetic minerals in the basalt record the direction of the field as the rock cools. The crust then moves away from the ridge as more magma forms. If the field reverses while this process continues, new strips of basalt record the opposite polarity.

Marine surveys found alternating bands of magnetic signal on the sea floor. The pattern is broadly mirrored on both sides of a ridge. In 1963, Frederick Vine and Drummond Matthews explained how repeated magnetic reversals combined with seafloor spreading could produce these stripes. Lawrence Morley developed the same idea independently. The pattern matched the reversal sequence measured on land and gave scientists a way to test whether ocean crust was moving outward from ridges.

This was important because it connected a changing magnetic field to the movement of tectonic plates. The ocean floor became a record of both magnetic history and crust formation. By dating basalt and matching its polarity to the geomagnetic time scale, researchers could estimate when different parts of the ocean basin formed.

14. What a reversal looks like

During a full reversal, the dominant field changes polarity. A compass that now points roughly north would, after the transition, point roughly south. The transition is not a sudden mechanical flip. Geological records show that reversals unfold over hundreds to thousands of years, although the length and details vary between events and records.

During a transition, the dipole part can weaken and the field can develop several temporary magnetic poles. It need not fall to zero. The atmosphere and magnetosphere would still provide protection, but radiation exposure in space could change. The exact future pattern cannot be forecast.

The last full reversal, from the Matuyama reversed chron to the present Brunhes normal chron, occurred about 780,000 years ago. A shorter event called the Laschamp excursion happened about 41,000 years ago. During that event the field weakened and changed direction for a limited period, then returned to its earlier polarity. An excursion is not the same as a completed reversal.

15. Can we predict the next reversal?

No reliable method can give a date for the next reversal. Reversals do not occur on a regular schedule. The intervals in the geological record vary widely. The fact that the last reversal was about 780,000 years ago does not mean Earth is overdue. A long gap is not a clock.

Scientists can measure the current field and model its recent change. They cannot yet forecast the detailed motion of the liquid core far into the future. The flow is complex, and small differences in the starting state can grow over time. The World Magnetic Model is designed for navigation over a limited period, not for predicting a reversal thousands of years ahead.

NOAA notes that the current dipole strength remains higher than it was for much of the last 50,000 years, even though the recent trend has been downward. A study published in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2018 reconstructed the field over the past 50,000 years. It found that earlier weak-field patterns in the South Atlantic did not lead to a reversal. The authors concluded that the modern anomaly alone is not evidence that a reversal has begun.

16. What could change for people and technology?

During a reversal, navigation based only on a magnetic compass could become less reliable. The field direction could vary more across a region, and a single global dipole model would be a poor description of the field. Satellite navigation systems use radio signals and precise clocks, so they do not depend on a compass direction in the same way. However, satellites and radio systems can still be affected by space weather and by changes in the radiation environment.

Charged particles can affect spacecraft electronics. A weaker or more complex field could change where particles reach low orbit, so space agencies would need updated radiation models and protection plans. Solar storms can also disturb radio and navigation systems; a reversal is a separate, much slower process.

Many animals use the geomagnetic field as one cue during migration or orientation. Experiments show that some birds and other animals can detect aspects of the field. The exact biological mechanisms and the degree of dependence differ by species. A reversal could change the cues available to them, but it is not possible to predict a single outcome for all migrating animals.

There is no strong evidence that past reversals directly caused mass extinctions. A reversal is a major geophysical event, but it unfolds over a long period. The atmosphere remains in place. The field does not simply switch off. Claims that a reversal will cause an immediate global catastrophe go beyond the evidence.

17. Worked example: converting the anomaly area

The 2025 Swarm study reports that the area inside the 26,000 nT contour in the South Atlantic grew by about 0.9% of Earth's surface area between 2014 and 2025. The following calculation converts that fraction into an approximate area. It is an illustrative conversion, not a new measurement.

Earth's total surface area is about 510 million square kilometres. Multiply this by 0.009:

510,000,000 km² × 0.009 = 4,590,000 km².

This means the reported increase is about 4.6 million square kilometres when expressed as a fraction of Earth's full surface area. The result depends on the paper's chosen magnetic contour and model. It does not mean that a 4.6-million-square-kilometre region has lost its magnetic field. It describes the increase in the area below a defined field-strength threshold.

18. Worked example: correcting a compass bearing

Assume a compass shows a magnetic bearing of 70°. Assume the local declination is 4° east. For this illustrative example, east declination is positive. To convert a magnetic bearing to a true bearing, add the east declination:

70° + 4° = 74° true.

If declination were 4° west, it would be negative and the true bearing would be 66°. These values are invented for teaching. A real user must obtain the declination for the exact location and date from a reliable chart or magnetic model. The World Magnetic Model provides the main field estimate for this purpose, but local magnetic anomalies and temporary disturbances can still matter.

19. What scientists still debate

The present field is measured in great detail, but the deep core cannot be sampled directly. Researchers infer its flow from changes in the magnetic field at the surface and from physics-based models. Different models can explain some observations in different ways. The 2025 Swarm study uses one consistent satellite record to examine changes from 2014 to 2025. Its authors also explain that estimates of the core field require assumptions about the crustal and external fields.

The long-term energy budget of the geodynamo is another open question. Thermal conductivity in the core is difficult to measure under core conditions. Different estimates affect how much heat can drive convection. Chemical buoyancy from the growth of the inner core may help. The 2022 Nature review explains why this question remains active.

Researchers also debate how to interpret short-lived changes in the field and how to distinguish a normal fluctuation from the start of an excursion or reversal. A single weak region is not enough. The strongest evidence comes from global observations, long records, geological data and models that make testable predictions.

Scientists know the field comes from a liquid-core dynamo and can reconstruct many past reversals. They cannot yet date the next one or forecast its exact path. This is a limit of prediction, not a lack of evidence about how the field works.

20. Ten lessons

  1. Earth's main magnetic field is generated by electric currents in moving liquid metal in the outer core.
  2. The field changes because the flow in the core changes.
  3. Magnetic north, the magnetic dip pole and the geographic North Pole are different definitions.
  4. Magnetic declination depends on both location and date.
  5. Swarm satellites and ground observatories measure different parts of the same changing system.
  6. The South Atlantic Anomaly is a weak-field region, not a hole in the field.
  7. Rocks can preserve ancient magnetic directions when magnetic minerals become locked in during cooling or deposition.
  8. Magnetic stripes on the sea floor helped confirm seafloor spreading and plate tectonics.
  9. Reversals are irregular and take a long time. The last full reversal was about 780,000 years ago.
  10. Current weakening and pole motion do not provide a reliable date for the next reversal.

21. Five questions and answers

1. What creates Earth's magnetic field?

Moving, electrically conducting liquid metal in the outer core creates electric currents. Those currents sustain the main magnetic field through the geodynamo process.

2. Why does magnetic north move?

The flow in the outer core changes over time. This changes the field's direction and strength at Earth's surface, so magnetic poles and compass declination move.

3. What is the South Atlantic Anomaly?

It is a broad region over the South Atlantic and South America where the magnetic field is weaker than in many other regions. It matters most for radiation exposure to satellites in low Earth orbit.

4. How do rocks show that the field reversed?

Magnetic minerals can lock in the field direction when lava cools or sediment forms. Scientists compare many dated samples from different places to reconstruct changes in polarity.

5. Does the current field change prove a reversal is near?

No. The field changes all the time. Weak regions have existed in the past without leading to a reversal. Scientists cannot reliably predict the date of the next reversal.
