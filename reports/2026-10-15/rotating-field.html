Why Sea Level Rises Even in Years When It Slows

Understanding ocean heat, melting ice, and short-term changes

Independent report · Read on its own · Source links at end

1. Executive summary

Sea level is rising over the long term, but it does not rise by the same amount every year. NASA reported that global average sea level increased by about 0.59 centimetres in 2024, then by only about 0.08 centimetres in 2025. A mild La Niña pattern moved more water onto land through rain, offsetting some of the effect of warming oceans in 2025. This short-term change does not mean the long-term rise has stopped.

Global sea level increases mainly because warming water expands and land ice, such as glaciers and ice sheets, melts into the ocean. Weather and climate patterns change how much water remains on land and how it moves from one region to another. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) concludes that further sea-level rise over this century is virtually certain, with the amount depending greatly on greenhouse-gas emissions. This report explains the measurements, physical causes, regional differences and choices facing coastal communities.

2. Background: what scientists measure

Global mean sea level is an estimate of the average height of the sea surface across the world's oceans, measured relative to a consistent reference. It does not mean the sea stands at one height everywhere. Winds, currents, gravity, water temperature and the shape of the coast change local levels. Satellites have tracked global sea-surface height since the early 1990s.

Instruments on the coast, called tide gauges, add longer local records. Comparing both kinds of measurement helps scientists distinguish worldwide change from local land movement and regional ocean patterns.

3. Cause one: warmer water takes up more space

When ocean water warms, its volume usually increases. This is thermal expansion. Because oceans contain enormous amounts of water, small changes in their average temperature can produce a measurable rise in sea level. Much of the extra heat held by Earth's climate system enters the ocean.

Warming in deep layers can continue to affect sea level for long periods because the ocean responds slowly. Scientists estimate ocean heat using instruments, drifting floats and other observations. This is one reason rising sea level continues even when one year brings cooler weather in a given region.

4. Cause two: ice moves from land to sea

Glaciers and the large ice sheets in Greenland and Antarctica store frozen fresh water on land. When land ice melts or flows into the ocean, it adds water and raises sea level. Floating sea ice works differently: its weight already displaces seawater, so melting it does not directly raise global sea level by the same amount. Yet losing floating ice can influence the climate and speed the flow of some land ice.

A clear explanation must separate the direct effect of land ice from the indirect effects of sea ice.

5. Cause three: water moves between land and ocean

Rain, snow, rivers, groundwater and reservoirs shift water between continents and the ocean. The El Niño–Southern Oscillation, a climate pattern in the tropical Pacific, can alter rainfall across many regions. NASA linked much of the unusually small 2025 rise to La Niña conditions that increased rainfall over the Amazon basin and temporarily kept more water on land. This change was large enough to offset part of the year's ocean warming effect.

It cannot reverse the decades-long increase caused by added heat and melting land ice.

6. Visual model

Greenhouse gases rise → Earth retains more heat → Ocean warms and expands → Sea level rises. A parallel path is warming → Land ice melts → Water enters ocean → Sea level rises. A third, variable path is rain and stored water on land ↔ Ocean water. The third path explains some year-to-year variation without removing the first two trends.

7. Understanding recent figures

NASA estimated a global rise of about 0.59 cm for 2024 and about 0.08 cm for 2025. The two values refer to single-year changes, not the total rise since measurements began. The difference is a warning against judging a trend from one point. A proper trend uses many years of observations, checks measurement error and accounts for the way climate patterns move water around.

The IPCC's 2021 physical-science assessment concluded that sea level would continue rising through the century in all assessed emissions scenarios.

8. Future change: not one fixed number

Relative to 1995–2014, the IPCC assessed a likely global average rise by 2100 of about 0.28–0.55 metres under a very low emissions path and 0.63–1.01 metres under a very high emissions path. These ranges describe scenarios and scientific uncertainty, not a schedule for any single coast. Ice-sheet behaviour is a major uncertainty in the far future. The IPCC also warned that larger rises cannot be ruled out under some high-warming conditions.

The central point is that emissions choices change the size of future risk.

9. Two practical examples

A low-lying port planning a quay cannot rely only on the current average sea level. It must consider local land movement, high tides, storms and years of operation. A coastal farming area may experience saltwater in groundwater before its fields become permanently flooded. Both settings need local measurements rather than a global average copied directly onto the shoreline.

Engineers use different design standards depending on the consequences of failure and the useful life of the asset.

10. Competing views and choices

One approach builds barriers, pumps and stronger drainage to defend existing communities. Another reduces exposure by limiting new construction in risky places or moving vulnerable buildings. Hard barriers can protect valuable infrastructure but may be costly, shift problems to nearby areas or harm coastal habitats. Planned retreat can reduce future risk but may disrupt lives and raise serious fairness issues.

Good policy considers who gains, who loses, what can be maintained and how risks change over time.

11. Why it matters and key lessons

Sea-level rise affects housing, ports, drinking water, insurance and public budgets. Remember: yearly changes are not the same as long-term trends; warming water expands; melting land ice adds water; floating ice differs from land ice; rain can temporarily store water on land; sea level varies by region; and planning must account for storms as well as average height. Future risk depends on both emissions and local decisions.

12. Knowledge check

1. Name the two main long-term causes of sea-level rise. Ocean warming and melting land ice. 2. Did sea-level rise stop in 2025? No; the annual increase was smaller. 3.

Why can rain in the Amazon affect global sea level? It stores water on land temporarily. 4. Does melting floating ice directly add the same water as melting land ice? No. 5. Why do local plans need more than global averages? Local land, tides and storms change the risk.
