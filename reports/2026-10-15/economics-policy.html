Why Middle-Income Countries Struggle to Grow Rich

The World Bank's three-stage explanation of long-run growth

Independent report · Read on its own · Source links at end

1. Executive summary

Raising average incomes is easier when a poor country can gain from basic investment than when a middle-income country must match the productivity of richer countries. Roads, electricity, machines and basic education can produce large early gains. Later, the country must learn to use advanced methods well and eventually develop new methods of its own. The World Bank's World Development Report 2024 calls this a move from investment to technology infusion to innovation.

Its analysis found that only 34 middle-income economies had moved into the high-income group since 1990, while 108 were middle-income at the end of 2023. It argues that growth depends on competition, skilled workers, effective public institutions and the ability to replace less productive activities with better ones. Yet the term middle-income trap is debated: income-group thresholds can change and countries have different histories. This report explains the mechanisms, evidence and practical policy choices without treating any one plan as certain to work.

2. Background: what counts as progress?

Gross domestic product, or GDP, measures the value of goods and services made in an economy. GDP per person divides that value by the number of residents. It is a rough measure of average economic output, not a measure of income distribution or quality of life. Productivity means the amount produced from a given amount of labour, machines and other resources.

A country can raise total GDP by adding workers, but sustained growth in living standards usually requires workers and firms to produce more value per hour. The World Bank groups countries by income per person using thresholds that it updates over time. Crossing a threshold is useful as a broad marker, but it does not prove that poverty, inequality or weak services have disappeared.

3. Stage one: investment

Countries with limited roads, power and equipment can raise output by building these basics. A reliable road reduces delivery time. Electricity lets factories run modern machines. Education helps workers use new tools.

At first, these gains can be large. Eventually, adding another machine or road may bring a smaller benefit than the earlier investment. Economists call this diminishing returns. Building more is still necessary, but it is no longer enough.

A country must improve how people and firms use the assets they already have.

4. Stage two: infusion

Technology infusion means bringing useful methods from elsewhere and spreading them across many local firms. It may involve buying equipment, learning quality control, improving logistics or adopting better management. Importing one advanced machine will do little if workers lack training or the firm cannot obtain parts. The wider system matters: finance must reach capable firms, contracts must work, workers must learn and poorly run firms must face pressure to improve.

The World Bank argues that lower-middle-income countries gain greatly by combining investment with infusion rather than moving straight to expensive frontier research.

5. Stage three: innovation

Innovation means producing valuable new ideas, products and ways of working rather than relying only on methods already used abroad. It requires skilled teams, competition, long-term funding and a system that allows new firms to challenge established ones. Governments may support research and education, but they must also avoid keeping failing businesses alive just because they are politically connected. Innovation is not limited to software or laboratories.

A food company that develops a more efficient process can add real value, although the largest frontier gains may need years of scientific work.

6. Visual sequence

Investment → Infusion → Innovation. The stages overlap rather than forming rigid boxes. Countries must maintain roads and education as they develop new technology. The balance of effort changes with the economy's needs. A useful diagram adds a feedback arrow: successful innovation can also increase demand for skilled workers and new infrastructure.

7. Evidence and cautions

The World Bank uses several decades of experience and case studies. South Korea illustrates how investment, adoption of outside technology and later original research can reinforce each other. But success has many causes, and it is hard to isolate one policy as the decisive factor. The historical sample includes countries with different trade access, political systems and population trends.

Some critics question whether the trap describes a distinct economic mechanism or simply the fact that sustained rapid growth is difficult. Threshold-based comparisons can also change when exchange rates or income classifications change.

8. Two real-world examples

South Korea moved from relatively low income to high-income production over several decades. Firms first learned from foreign technology and later developed their own products and research capacity. The World Bank uses Samsung's growth as one illustration. Chile adapted methods from abroad in areas such as salmon farming, showing how local firms can use external knowledge and tailor it to local conditions.

Neither example can be copied without adjusting for a country's skills, ecology, institutions and world markets.

9. Trade-offs and policy

Protecting an emerging industry may give it time to learn, but long protection can reduce the need to improve. Opening markets can bring competition and better goods, yet sudden change can harm workers in weaker firms. Governments must balance change with retraining and basic social support. Public investment should be assessed by results, not only by spending.

A costly industrial park that remains empty does not build productive capacity. Public debate should ask which firms become more effective, how quickly skills spread and whether new opportunities reach a broad share of citizens.

10. Why it matters and key lessons

India and many other economies face a central question: how to raise living standards as basic gains become harder to secure. Remember: GDP per person is an average; basic investment matters early; technology adoption must spread across firms; skills and competition help firms improve; innovation becomes more important as an economy develops; institutions shape the results; and no historical example guarantees a future path. Strong growth policy tracks productivity and human welfare rather than one headline target.

11. Knowledge check

Does high GDP ensure equal gains? No. 5. Why is competition useful? It can reward effective firms and push weak firms to improve.
