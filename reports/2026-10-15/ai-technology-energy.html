The Hidden Energy Cost of Artificial Intelligence

Can electricity systems support the growth of AI without raising costs and pollution?

Independent learning report · 2024–2026 evidence

SummaryHow it worksTrade-offsExamplesKnowledge checkSources

1. Executive summary

Artificial intelligence has a physical cost. Each AI request requires computer chips, power supply equipment, data networks and cooling. These systems operate inside data centres. A large data centre can demand as much power as an industrial plant, and some use power throughout the day.

The International Energy Agency (IEA) estimated that data centres consumed 415 terawatt-hours (TWh) of electricity in 2024, about 1.5% of global electricity use. Its 2025 base case projected 945 TWh by 2030. These numbers cover all data-centre work, including cloud services, storage and conventional computing, rather than AI alone. The IEA reported in 2026 that demand grew rapidly during 2025, while a shortage of grid equipment and long connection times constrained growth.

New US research from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (published in June 2026) placed the potential share of US electricity used by data centres in 2030 at 9.5–15.3%, with a central estimate of 11.8%. These are scenarios, not fixed outcomes.

The main issue is local: electricity generation, cables, substations and water supply cannot always expand as fast as data-centre projects. Some added power may come from solar, wind or nuclear energy; some may come from natural gas or coal. That choice changes pollution. It also matters who pays for new infrastructure. More efficient hardware can reduce electricity per task, but lower costs may encourage more AI use. A sound policy must examine demand, location, energy mix, system upgrades and costs together.

2. Background: why AI needs electricity

Artificial intelligence is a set of software methods that learn patterns from data and use those patterns to make predictions or produce results. Modern AI systems often use specialised chips called graphics processing units (GPUs). They perform many calculations at once. Training a model adjusts its internal settings using large amounts of data. Inference means using a trained model to answer a prompt, sort an image or generate text. Both require electricity.

Computers turn most of the electric energy they consume into heat. Without cooling, server temperatures would rise and damage hardware or cause it to slow down. Cooling equipment can use fans, pumps and chilled water. The rest of the building requires networking equipment, electrical conversion and backup power. Thus the energy cost of an AI response is greater than the chips' energy alone.

Earlier data-centre growth did not always bring equal power growth. Better machines, higher server use and better cooling held electricity use nearly steady during parts of the 2010s. The more recent surge in AI computing has made that balance harder to keep, according to US Department of Energy and IEA studies.

Power versus energy

Power measures the rate of electricity use, in watts or megawatts (MW). Energy measures how much electricity was used over time, in watt-hours or megawatt-hours (MWh). A 100 MW facility running at full power for 24 hours uses 2,400 MWh, or 2.4 gigawatt-hours. Over 365 days it would use 876 gigawatt-hours (0.876 TWh). Real use depends on how busy the machines are and how well the building runs.

The full data-centre bill

Engineers measure power use effectiveness (PUE) as total facility energy divided by energy used by the computing equipment. A PUE of 1.25 means the building uses 1.25 units of energy for every unit used by the IT equipment. The extra 0.25 supports cooling and other systems. A smaller PUE is usually better, but it cannot tell us whether the data centre produces useful results or relies on polluting power.

Worked example. A server fleet uses 80 MWh in one hour. If the building's PUE is 1.25, total demand is 100 MWh. Cooling and other overhead account for 20 MWh. This is an example, not a reading from a real site.

4. How much electricity will data centres need?

Forecasts start with planned machines, their average power draw, expected hours of use and supporting systems. They then make assumptions about new technologies, adoption and limits to construction. Small changes in these assumptions can cause large differences over several years.

The 530 TWh projected increase is substantial. Yet the IEA estimated that data centres would represent less than a tenth of total growth in worldwide electricity demand by 2030. The distribution is not uniform. Countries that host large clusters, especially the United States, face a much larger impact than the global average suggests.

The 2026 Lawrence Berkeley study estimates that US data centres could account for 9.5–15.3% of national electricity demand by 2030. Its central figure is 11.8%. The wide range is a warning against treating any one forecast as certain.

5. Why geography matters more than the world average

Electricity must travel through power lines and substations that have safe operating limits. A region can have enough electricity generation for the year while still facing a shortage at certain hours or locations. A new data centre may need a new transmission line, a larger substation or stronger local cables. These often take longer to plan and build than a server hall.

Grid access also depends on what happens when demand peaks. A region might have spare energy overnight but little spare power on a hot afternoon. Operators must plan for failures and extreme weather, not just the average day. Several large facilities in one area can therefore add stress even if they use a modest share of national electricity.

Some computer jobs can move to another hour or location. Training a model may sometimes be scheduled around spare power. Many live services must answer users quickly, so they need reliable power almost all the time. These two types of work have different effects on the grid.

6. Energy sources, emissions and costs

What will supply the extra power?

In its 2025 base case, the IEA estimated that renewable sources would meet nearly half of the increase in electricity generation for data centres through 2030. Natural gas and coal would supply much of the rest, while nuclear energy could play a larger role later. The mix changes by country and region. Clean power purchase contracts alone do not show what the local grid produces during each hour of a data centre's use.

Who pays for new wires?

Electric utilities recover costs through power prices, fees and contracts. If a data-centre company pays the full cost of a connection and commits to buying power over many years, households face less risk from that project. If the utility builds expensive new equipment based on demand that later fails to appear, other users could share the cost. Rate rules can require firm commitments, deposits and charges for large users. The right design depends on local law and how the network is used.

Does efficiency solve the problem?

Better chips, smaller models and improved cooling reduce power per task. But total electricity use depends on the number of tasks as well as the energy each task needs. If an AI request uses half as much electricity while the number of requests triples, overall power use rises. Economists call this a rebound effect: efficiency can make a service cheaper and encourage greater use. It does not mean efficiency is useless. It means the final result must be measured, not assumed.

7. Water use and local communities

Some data centres use water in cooling towers, where evaporation removes heat. Other systems rely more on air or use closed liquid circuits, but they can still need water indirectly when power plants generate their electricity. Local climate, cooling design and electricity supply determine the actual effect. A facility in a water-stressed region may face very different limits from one in a cool, wet area.

Communities may gain construction jobs, property tax income and new grid investment. They may also face noise, land use, water demand and possible power-rate pressure. A useful review separates short-term construction jobs from long-term local jobs. It also checks whether promised economic gains justify the infrastructure costs.

8. Two worked examples

Example A: a proposed 200 MW data centre

Suppose a company plans a 200 MW facility. At full draw around the clock, its annual use would be 200 × 8,760 = 1,752,000 MWh, or 1.752 TWh. At a fictional energy price of US$80 per MWh, the yearly energy charge alone would be about US$140 million. Real contracts include many other charges, and average draw may be lower. This simple example shows why location, steady power and electricity prices play a large part in investment decisions.

Example B: flexible AI training

Suppose an AI training job needs 100 MWh of electricity and can run at any point during the next day. If its operator shifts work from a crowded evening period to a time when wind output is high, it could lower costs and ease strain. That gain depends on whether spare clean electricity and network capacity truly exist at that time. Moving a task does not automatically make it carbon-free.

9. Competing views

View 1: AI demand can support better energy investment

Supporters argue that large, steady customers can help fund new power plants and network upgrades. AI may also improve power forecasts, grid maintenance and industrial efficiency. Long-term contracts can give clean-energy developers greater certainty. These gains are possible, but they require projects that add useful capacity rather than merely claim existing clean power.

View 2: fast growth could raise bills and pollution

Critics warn that data centres may secure scarce grid connections, delay the closure of fossil-fuel plants or cause costs to fall on other customers. They also question whether some AI uses create enough value to justify their energy use. The risk is highest where supply is tight, projects lack clear commitments or public oversight is weak.

Both views identify real issues. The outcome depends on contracts, grid conditions, clean electricity built in time, actual AI use and public rules. General claims that all AI is good or all AI is wasteful hide those differences.

10. Why this matters

The growing energy demand of AI links technology choices to public infrastructure. A decision to build a data centre affects power stations, transmission plans, land use and in some places water. These projects can last for decades, while individual AI models may change in months. Planners must avoid building too little for demand that arrives, but also avoid building costly equipment for projects that never operate.

To judge a new data-centre project, look for five facts: its expected peak power, projected annual electricity use, where the power will come from each hour, what grid upgrades it needs, and who pays if demand differs from the forecast. Add water use and emissions for a full local assessment.

11. Key lessons

  1. AI services run on physical machines that consume electricity and produce heat.
  2. Data-centre totals include much more than AI; never confuse the two.
  3. The IEA estimated 415 TWh for 2024 and forecast 945 TWh by 2030 in its 2025 base case.
  4. US data-centre demand could form a far larger share of national electricity than the global average.
  5. A local grid can face a shortage even when national generation looks sufficient.
  6. The hour and location of power use matter for cost and pollution.
  7. More efficient chips do not guarantee lower total electricity use if demand rises quickly.
  8. Contracts and rate rules decide how much risk large users place on households.
  9. Forecasts are scenarios, not promises.

12. Knowledge check

1. What is the difference between MW and MWh?

MW is the rate of power use. MWh is the amount of electricity consumed over time. A 10 MW load that runs for 5 hours uses 50 MWh.

2. Why is PUE useful, and what does it miss?

It measures total facility energy relative to computing energy, so it helps compare overhead. It does not measure whether computing is useful, nor the carbon content of electricity.

3. Why can a data centre cause local grid stress even when its global electricity share is small?

Demand is concentrated. Nearby substations and lines may not have enough spare capacity at peak times.

4. Why might total AI electricity use rise despite more efficient chips?

Cheaper tasks may encourage more use. If the number of tasks grows faster than energy per task falls, total use increases.

5. What five facts should planners request before approving a large data centre?

Peak power, annual electricity use, hourly supply mix, grid-upgrade needs and who pays for those upgrades.
