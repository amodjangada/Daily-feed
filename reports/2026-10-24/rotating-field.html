How Can Scientists Measure Heat Hidden in the Ocean?

The observing systems, physics and uncertainty behind one of climate science's most important measurements.

Executive summary

Most of the extra heat retained by Earth's climate system enters the ocean. Yet a thermometer at the sea surface cannot tell us how much energy is stored hundreds or thousands of metres below. To estimate the total, scientists combine millions of temperature and salinity readings from ships, autonomous floats, moorings and other instruments. They map those sparse observations across depth and area, then calculate the energy stored in each layer. Satellites supply an independent view of sea-surface height, gravity and circulation, but they do not directly measure deep-ocean temperature.

The distinction matters because sea-surface temperature and ocean heat content measure different things. A cool season or a La Niña event can reduce surface temperature while the ocean as a whole continues to accumulate energy. According to the World Meteorological Organization's 2025 climate assessment, the upper 2,000 metres reached a new record for heat content in 2025. Its estimated increase from 2024 was 24 ± 16 zettajoules. That uncertainty is substantial, but the multi-decade trend is clearer than any one year's change. This study explains how that conclusion is measured, checked and interpreted.

1. Why heat content is different from temperature

Temperature tells us how hot a particular sample is. Heat content describes the thermal energy stored in a volume of water relative to a chosen baseline. A tiny increase in temperature across an enormous mass of water can store more energy than a large temperature increase in a small patch. Oceans cover more than 70% of Earth and extend to great depths. Their large mass and high heat capacity make them the main store of excess energy in the climate system.

Heat content is usually expressed in joules. One zettajoule, written ZJ, is 10²¹ joules. The number is large because the ocean is large. To calculate a change in energy, scientists multiply the mass of water by its specific heat capacity and by its change in temperature. Ocean water is not perfectly uniform: salinity, pressure and temperature affect its density and heat capacity. Research-grade calculations use those properties instead of one fixed value everywhere.

Surface temperature responds quickly to winds, sunlight, evaporation and ocean circulation. The upper mixed layer can warm or cool within weeks. Deep water exchanges heat much more slowly. A warm surface does not always mean that the full water column gained heat, and a cooler surface does not prove that the planet lost stored energy. Scientists therefore separate the upper 300 metres, the upper 700 metres, the upper 2,000 metres and the deeper ocean when reporting changes.

2. From ship thermometers to global robots

For much of the twentieth century, researchers measured temperature by lowering instruments from ships. Early profiles were sparse and concentrated along shipping routes. Some devices, such as expendable bathythermographs, measured temperature as they descended but did not directly measure salinity. Instrument design and calibration changed over time. Correcting these biases is important because scientists are looking for long-term warming that can be small compared with natural variation.

Modern observing systems improved coverage. The international Argo programme uses autonomous floats that drift through the ocean and periodically measure profiles of temperature and salinity. Many floats operate in the upper 2,000 metres, surfacing roughly every ten days to transmit data. Newer Deep Argo instruments extend observations farther down. Ships, moorings and specialised sensors still matter, particularly near coasts, in polar regions and for calibration.

NOAA's 2025 ocean heat explainer describes a global network of more than 3,000 profiling floats. It also notes that the upper ocean has gained heat for decades, while deeper layers have begun to show the signal more clearly. Better instruments do not erase the gaps in older records. Scientists compare multiple independently processed datasets and publish uncertainty estimates because some regions and periods remain less well observed.

3. How a float becomes a global estimate

The first step is a profile: a series of temperature and salinity measurements at different depths. Instruments also record pressure, which helps determine depth. A profile is checked for impossible values, sensor drift and known measurement problems. Scientists then convert the observations into physical properties, including seawater density and heat capacity.

Next, measurements are placed into a three-dimensional grid of latitude, longitude and depth. The grid cannot be filled by observation alone; most of the ocean is not sampled at every moment. Statistical mapping methods estimate conditions between observations using nearby measurements, historical patterns and knowledge of how ocean properties vary. Some analyses also use ocean models constrained by observations. Different mapping choices produce somewhat different heat estimates, especially in poorly sampled regions.

Finally, researchers calculate the heat anomaly in each grid cell relative to a reference period, multiply by the cell's mass and heat capacity, and sum across the ocean. An anomaly is the difference from a baseline, not an absolute heat content of zero. Changing the reference period changes the reported anomaly's zero point but does not, by itself, change the physical warming trend.

It is essential to distinguish measurement from inference. A float directly measures local properties. The global total is an estimate built from those readings plus a method for filling gaps. This does not make the total arbitrary. It means researchers must show how the answer changes under plausible alternative methods and data selections.

4. What satellites add—and what they cannot see

Satellites measure several properties related to ocean heat. Radar altimeters estimate the height of the sea surface relative to a reference frame. Warmer seawater generally expands, so thermal expansion raises sea level. But sea level also changes when land ice melts, rainfall shifts water between land and ocean, currents redistribute water, or gravity changes. Height alone cannot uniquely identify temperature at each depth.

Scientists combine altimetry with ocean mass estimates from gravity missions and in-water profiles. The difference between total sea-level change and ocean-mass change helps estimate the steric component: sea-level change caused by variations in seawater density. Temperature is a major part of that density change, but salinity also matters. Independent measurements help detect problems in one observing system.

NASA's sea-level research shows why annual sea-level changes are not identical to annual heat changes. Its January 2026 analysis reported that global mean sea level rose only about 0.8 millimetres in 2025, compared with about 5.9 millimetres in 2024. A mild La Niña shifted more water onto land through rainfall and temporarily reduced the sea-level rise. Meanwhile, ocean heat content reached another record. The two results can coexist because they track different physical processes.

5. Worked calculation: a small heat flux, a huge ocean

NOAA reports a full-depth ocean heat-gain rate of about 0.66–0.74 watts per square metre when averaged over Earth's total surface for 1993–2024. A watt is one joule per second. The value looks small until we multiply it by the area over which the rate is defined and by time. Use a midpoint of 0.70 W/m², Earth's approximate surface area of 5.10 × 10¹⁴ m², and 31.56 million seconds in a year.

The energy gain is 0.70 × 5.10 × 10¹⁴ × 3.156 × 10⁷ = about 1.13 × 10²² joules per year, or 11.3 ZJ per year. This is an illustrative conversion of NOAA's multi-decade average flux, not a claim about the exact increase in 2025. The denominator matters: NOAA expressed the heat gain as a flux averaged over the whole Earth's surface, so multiplying by ocean area again would undercount it.

A second useful calculation starts with a small volume. Suppose 1 cubic kilometre of seawater has a density of 1,025 kg/m³ and specific heat capacity of 3,990 J/(kg·°C). A warming of only 0.1°C stores approximately 4.09 × 10¹⁴ joules. The arithmetic is 10⁹ m³ × 1,025 kg/m³ × 3,990 J/(kg·°C) × 0.1°C. Real calculations account for changing properties with depth. The lesson is that a small temperature change across a huge mass stores enormous energy.

6. What the latest assessments say

The World Meteorological Organization's State of the Global Climate 2025, published in March 2026, states that upper-2,000-metre ocean heat content reached its highest value in a 66-year observational record in 2025. The estimated increase from 2024 was 24 ± 16 ZJ. The uncertainty is a reminder that annual increments are harder to estimate than multi-decade change. The report estimates an average gain of 11.0–12.2 ZJ per year over 2005–2025, more than twice the 1960–2005 rate of about 3.05–3.91 ZJ per year.

NOAA's June 2025 ocean heat assessment explains the observing systems and reports that more than 90% of excess heat trapped in the climate system has been absorbed by the ocean. NASA's ocean warming indicator gives a separate presentation of long-run upper-ocean energy change, including an estimate of 372 ± 2 ZJ since 1955 as of December 2024. These are not independent measurements of exactly the same quantity at the same date, so they should not be mixed as if they were interchangeable.

Copernicus' ocean heat content indicator shows the vertical structure of warming. Most added heat is initially stored in the upper ocean, while changes in the 700–2,000-metre layer develop more slowly. Ocean currents carry energy between regions and depths. A map can show cooling in one area at the same time that the global ocean gains heat. This is one reason a local observation cannot establish the global trend.

7. Why uncertainty does not mean no knowledge

Every measurement has limitations. Older ship records have incomplete coverage and known instrument biases. Modern floats may be sparse under sea ice or near some coastlines. Mapping methods must estimate unobserved regions. Heat capacity and density depend on salinity and pressure. Deep-ocean observations remain much fewer than upper-ocean observations. Researchers quantify these uncertainties through instrument calibration, statistical analysis, alternative mapping methods and comparison across datasets.

Uncertainty also depends on the question. Was 2025 exactly 24 ZJ warmer than 2024 in the upper 2,000 metres? The reported ±16 ZJ interval indicates a broad range. Has the ocean warmed over several decades? Multiple datasets, instruments and physical indicators support that conclusion much more strongly. It is incorrect to treat a wide one-year error bar as proof that the long-term signal is unknown.

Scientists also separate changes caused by greenhouse gases from short-term climate variability. El Niño and La Niña move heat between the atmosphere, surface ocean and deeper layers. Winds and currents redistribute heat geographically. These effects can change the year-to-year rate, but they do not remove the need to account for the Earth's overall energy imbalance.

8. Two useful scientific perspectives

Direct observation should lead

One position gives priority to quality-controlled in-water measurements. Floats and ships directly sample the temperature and salinity needed for heat calculations. Supporters of this approach stress transparent calibration, clear coverage maps and avoiding excessive reliance on model assumptions. Its limitation is that direct samples remain sparse in space and time, especially in the deep ocean and polar regions.

Combined observation and models are essential

A second position emphasises combining in-water data with satellite observations and physically constrained ocean models. These systems can estimate unsampled regions and connect heat change to circulation, air–sea exchange and sea-level change. The risk is that model structure and data-assimilation choices can introduce shared errors or make uncertainty appear too small. The strongest evidence comes when different approaches agree on the large-scale trend and researchers report where they differ.

The choice is not simply instruments versus models. Both are necessary for a global estimate. Good practice is to keep raw measurements available, compare independent reconstructions, and disclose sensitivity to coverage and corrections. An honest scientific account can say that the broad warming is robust while individual regional and annual values remain uncertain.

9. Why stored ocean heat matters

Stored heat contributes to sea-level rise because warming water expands. It can also influence marine heatwaves, oxygen availability, coral bleaching and the energy available to storms. The exact impact depends on where the heat is stored and how circulation brings it back toward the surface. Heat that enters the deep ocean can remain there for long periods, so ocean conditions respond more slowly than air temperature to changes in greenhouse-gas emissions.

For coastal planning, surface temperature alone is an incomplete risk measure. Communities need sea-level projections, local land movement, storm surge, tides and erosion data. For fisheries and coral reefs, local temperatures and marine heatwave duration may matter more directly than the global heat total. For climate science, the global total is a powerful indicator of the planet's energy balance. Each measure answers a different question.

A practical reading rule is to ask four things whenever a headline reports “record ocean heat”: which depth range was measured, which baseline was used, whether the number is a total or a change, and what uncertainty accompanies it. These questions prevent confusion between a warm sea surface, a large heat store and a rapid annual increase.

10. Key lessons

  1. Ocean heat content measures stored energy, not the temperature at one point.
  2. Global estimates combine in-water observations, physical properties and mapping methods.
  3. Satellites help cross-check heat-related changes but cannot directly read deep temperature.
  4. A small global heat flux becomes a very large annual energy gain because Earth is large.
  5. Short-term surface cooling can occur while the ocean's total heat content increases.
  6. Annual estimates have uncertainty; multi-decade warming is supported by several independent indicators.

11. Knowledge check

1. Why is a sea-surface thermometer insufficient?

It measures one depth and location. Heat content requires temperature changes through large water volumes.

2. What does an Argo float measure?

Profiles of temperature, salinity and pressure, which researchers use to estimate ocean properties through depth.

3. Why can global sea-level rise slow while ocean heat rises?

Changes in water stored on land, ice melt, circulation and salinity can affect sea level independently of thermal expansion.

4. What is the difference between an anomaly and an annual change?

An anomaly compares heat content with a reference period. An annual change compares two consecutive times.

5. Why does uncertainty in one year's gain not overturn the long-run trend?

Many years of observations and independent datasets show persistent heat accumulation even when individual annual increments are uncertain.
