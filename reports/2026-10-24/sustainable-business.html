Can Industrial Heat Pumps Replace Fossil-Fuel Boilers?

Where factory heat electrification works, what it costs, and how to measure its climate value.

Executive summary

Factories need heat for washing, drying, pasteurising, chemical reactions and many other steps. Today much of that heat comes from burning fuel. An industrial heat pump can recover low-temperature heat and raise it to a useful temperature with electricity. It moves heat rather than making all of it from electricity. This is why it can deliver several units of useful heat for each unit of electrical input. It can reduce fuel purchases and emissions, but only when the required temperature, available waste heat, electricity price, plant schedule and investment cost fit together.

The opportunity is real but narrower than the phrase “electrify industry” suggests. The International Energy Agency (IEA) estimates that commercially available heat pumps could technically supply around one fifth of global industrial heat demand. Technical potential is not the same as financially attractive deployment. Food processing, paper production, washing, district heat and low-temperature chemical processes are promising applications. Steelmaking and many high-temperature kilns need other technologies. This report explains the thermodynamics, a worked factory example, the business case and the reasons good projects still fail.

1. Why factories burn fuel

Industrial heat is easy to overlook because it is often hidden behind a steam pipe. A boiler burns natural gas, coal, biomass or another fuel and transfers energy into hot water or steam. Steam can travel around a site and release heat into many different machines. One central boiler can therefore support processes with different operating schedules. Operators understand the equipment and can often repair it quickly. This existing infrastructure is an important reason boilers remain common.

Industry does not have one heat requirement. Washing or food pasteurisation may need temperatures below 100°C. Drying, evaporation and some chemical processes require more. Cement and steel need much higher temperatures, often hundreds or more than a thousand degrees Celsius. The correct question is not whether heat pumps can replace all boilers. It is which specific heat loads can use recovered heat at a useful temperature without disrupting production.

Refrigeration technology has transferred heat for over a century. A refrigerator removes heat from its inside and releases it into a room. A heat pump uses the same physical principle, but the useful product is the heat released. Industrial systems can use process cooling water, warm exhaust, wastewater or data-centre heat as their source. Modern refrigerants, compressors and heat exchangers have widened the temperature range. Yet each site still needs its own engineering study because the waste-heat supply and demand rarely match perfectly.

2. How a heat pump raises temperature

Heat naturally flows from hotter material to colder material. To move it in the other direction, a heat pump uses work from a compressor. A working fluid called a refrigerant circulates in a closed loop. In the evaporator, the refrigerant absorbs low-grade heat and changes phase. The compressor raises its pressure and temperature. In the condenser, it gives up heat to the factory water or process stream. An expansion device then lowers its pressure so the cycle can repeat.

The key measure is the coefficient of performance, or COP: useful heat output divided by electricity input. A COP of 3 means that 1 MWh of electricity plus roughly 2 MWh taken from the heat source produces 3 MWh of delivered heat. This does not create energy. The input electricity powers the transfer of energy that already exists in the source. COP changes with operating conditions; a manufacturer's peak value is not a guaranteed annual result.

The temperature lift is the difference between the source temperature and the required delivery temperature. A small lift is usually easier and more efficient than a large one. Heating water from a 50°C waste stream to 80°C is typically a better candidate than raising a 15°C source to 170°C. The type of refrigerant, pressure limits, compressor design, heat-exchanger approach temperature and part-load operation all matter. The IEA notes that lower-temperature systems are more mature than high-temperature designs.

Recovering heat also changes how a factory manages cooling. A site that previously paid to remove heat with a cooling tower may save some cooling energy when the heat pump uses that stream. These extra savings are useful, but should be counted only when the cooling load truly decreases. The same kilowatt-hour of savings must not be counted twice.

3. Find the right process first

A practical screening study starts with measurements. Record hourly heat demand, supply and return temperatures, annual operating hours, and the pressure and quality of any steam requirement. Then measure waste-heat flows: temperature, flow rate, hours available and contamination risk. A source that is warm only during one shift may not support a process that runs around the clock. Thermal storage can help, but it adds equipment and losses.

The next step is to divide a large heat system into temperature levels. A plant may have a 180°C steam header while most wash lines need only 70°C water. Keeping every user on high-temperature steam wastes useful temperature difference. A heat pump can serve the low-temperature loads while the existing boiler covers the high-temperature ones. This hybrid design often reduces risk and allows a gradual retrofit.

Integration can be harder than buying the heat pump. A plant may need new water loops, heat exchangers, controls, electrical transformers, floor space and backup equipment. Operators need safe procedures for start-up, maintenance and refrigerant leaks. A reliable project must specify who supplies heat if the heat pump trips during a production run. These costs belong in the financial model, even if a supplier's equipment quote omits them.

4. Worked example: a food factory

Assume a factory needs 12,000 MWh of useful hot-water heat each year. Its existing gas boiler operates at 85% efficiency. The factory considers an industrial heat pump with an annual COP of 3.2. These are illustrative inputs, not measured data from a real factory. The heat pump would use 12,000 ÷ 3.2 = 3,750 MWh of electricity and recover the remaining 8,250 MWh from its low-temperature source.

The boiler would otherwise use 12,000 ÷ 0.85 = 14,118 MWh of fuel, rounded. If delivered gas costs ₹4.50 per kWh of fuel energy, annual fuel cost is about ₹6.35 crore. If industrial electricity costs ₹8 per kWh, heat-pump electricity costs ₹3.00 crore. Before maintenance, finance and other site costs, the simple operating-energy saving is about ₹3.35 crore per year.

Suppose the installed project—including controls and piping—costs ₹10 crore. A simple energy-only payback would be about 3.0 years. This is not a full investment appraisal. Real projects must include additional maintenance, refrigerant management, backup fuel, financing, taxes, downtime, tariff demand charges and any cooling benefit. Annual COP should come from hourly simulation or a measured pilot, not a brochure.

The result is sensitive to tariffs. At ₹12 per kWh, heat-pump electricity costs ₹4.50 crore. If gas falls to ₹3 per kWh, boiler energy costs roughly ₹4.24 crore, making the heat pump more expensive to operate under those assumptions. Price risk therefore matters as much as equipment efficiency. A contract that fixes one energy price but leaves the other variable can change the investment decision.

5. When does it cut emissions?

Electric equipment has no combustion emissions at the point of use, but electricity generation may emit carbon dioxide. The right comparison uses the emissions per delivered unit of useful heat. A boiler using gas with an illustrative combustion factor of 0.202 kg CO₂ per kWh of fuel produces about 0.238 kg CO₂ per useful kWh at 85% efficiency. A heat pump with COP 3.2 on a grid emitting 0.65 kg CO₂ per kWh of electricity produces about 0.203 kg per useful kWh. It cuts operating emissions in this example, but by a smaller margin than its energy-cost saving might imply.

If grid emissions fall to 0.30 kg CO₂ per kWh, the heat-pump figure falls to about 0.094 kg per useful kWh. Conversely, if the heat pump has poor annual performance or uses a high-emission electricity source, its advantage can disappear. For an exact project, use the applicable grid accounting method, the relevant time period, and the real fuel specification. Distinguish direct operating emissions from equipment manufacturing and refrigerant leakage. The latter can matter if a refrigerant has a high global-warming potential.

The climate benefit is strongest when three conditions coincide: a good waste-heat source, high COP, and low-carbon electricity. It may improve over time as the grid becomes cleaner, while the equipment remains in service. A business case should show today's emissions and a reasonable range for future grid conditions rather than assume that electricity is always carbon-free.

6. What research actually shows

The IEA's Heat Pump Monitor 2026 reports that commercial heat-pump systems could technically supply around 20% of industrial heat demand worldwide. It also finds that industry deployment is still limited. The same report notes that Japan had nearly 650 MW of industrial heat-pump capacity in 2024, enough to cover roughly 1% of its light-industry heat demand. These numbers show that the technology works, while its diffusion remains early. A technical potential figure should never be presented as a forecast of near-term market share.

The IEA's 2025 levelised-cost comparison evaluates boilers and heat pumps using a COP assumption of 3.5 and specified energy-price ranges. The chart makes clear that regional electricity and gas prices, capital costs and finance can reverse the ranking. The published comparison excludes some site-specific grid connection costs. It is therefore a useful starting point for sensitivity analysis, not a ready-made factory quotation.

Researchers at Oak Ridge National Laboratory studied recovering waste heat from a high-performance computing facility to supply hot water on the laboratory campus. Their March 2024 project report modelled scenarios with emission savings and payback times under five years. The study is valuable because it combines a specific source, a defined heat demand and real engineering constraints. Its numbers are site-specific, however, and should not be transferred to a textile or food plant without new measurements.

7. Two credible perspectives

The case for rapid deployment

Supporters argue that low-temperature process heat is a large and underused efficiency opportunity. A heat pump can use energy that a factory already pays to reject. It may reduce fuel purchases, make better use of low-carbon electricity and cut local combustion pollution. Some systems also deliver cooling, which improves total plant value. In regions with costly imported gas and competitively priced electricity, these advantages can be substantial. Industrial electrification can also reduce exposure to fossil-fuel price swings.

The case for caution

Plant managers and cautious investors note that heat delivery is a core production function. A process interruption may cost more than a year's energy saving. Custom retrofits are difficult to standardise, electricity network upgrades can take time, and a higher temperature lift can reduce COP. If electricity is costly or carbon-intensive, the financial and environmental cases weaken. Refrigerant regulation and service capacity also add uncertainty. This view does not reject heat pumps; it demands site-specific proof and reliable backup.

Both positions can be true. The best decision is based on a measured heat map, an hourly energy model, realistic maintenance assumptions, a verified installation design and a staged commissioning plan. A pilot can test controls and reliability before the plant replaces a major boiler load.

8. A practical investment checklist

First, classify heat loads by required temperature, quality and criticality. Second, map waste-heat sources and verify their availability over a full production cycle. Third, estimate annual COP at the real source and sink temperatures. Fourth, request a complete installed-cost estimate that includes electrical work, pipes, civil work, controls and safety. Fifth, model at least three energy-price scenarios and include demand charges. Sixth, calculate emissions using transparent grid and fuel factors. Seventh, plan redundancy and a controlled handover from the existing boiler.

Contract terms matter. A supplier may guarantee a COP only at a laboratory point. A buyer needs performance at defined operating conditions and clear responsibility for source-water quality, maintenance, refrigerant loss and process uptime. Independent measurement after commissioning should compare heat delivered, electricity consumed and hours operated. Without these measurements, the company cannot know whether its investment met the expected result.

Policy also affects adoption. Time-of-use electricity prices, industrial efficiency grants, carbon prices and low-interest finance can change economics. These tools can accelerate sound projects, but poor incentives may reward equipment purchases instead of verified heat savings. Public support should therefore focus on measured output and transparent baseline comparisons.

9. Key lessons

  1. Industrial heat pumps transfer waste heat; their advantage comes from moving heat efficiently.
  2. Temperature lift and annual operating hours strongly influence COP and financial return.
  3. Low- and medium-temperature processes are the main near-term opportunity.
  4. A full factory retrofit needs more than a heat-pump purchase: controls, piping, power and backup matter.
  5. Carbon savings depend on actual electricity emissions, refrigerants and annual performance.
  6. Good economics are site-specific. Energy-price ratios can reverse the decision.

10. Knowledge check

1. Why can a heat pump deliver more heat energy than the electricity it uses?

It transfers additional heat from a source such as warm wastewater. Its electricity drives the compressor; it does not provide all delivered heat.

2. A system delivers 4,800 MWh and consumes 1,600 MWh. What is its COP?

COP = 4,800 ÷ 1,600 = 3.0.

3. Why is a 70°C wash line a better early target than a 900°C furnace?

The lower delivery temperature needs a smaller temperature lift and uses mature industrial heat-pump technology.

4. Why might a heat pump cut costs but not cut much carbon?

Electricity may have a high emissions factor even when its price divided by COP is attractive.

5. What should an investment model include beyond equipment price?

Installed infrastructure, tariffs, maintenance, downtime, backup, financing, cooling benefits, annual COP and emissions factors.
