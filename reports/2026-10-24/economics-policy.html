Why a Government Can Run Deficits and Still Stabilize Its Debt

What determines whether public debt remains manageable—and why is the deficit only part of the answer?

Executive summary

Public debt is the money a government owes after years of borrowing. A deficit is the gap between what it spends and what it receives during one period. These are related but different measures. A government can borrow more money while its debt becomes smaller relative to the size of its economy. The result depends on four forces: existing debt, interest paid on it, growth of nominal national income, and the budget balance before interest payments. Other adjustments can matter too. This report explains the arithmetic, then shows why it is only the starting point for policy. Debt that appears stable today can become difficult to finance if investors demand higher interest rates or many bonds mature together. Borrowing can support growth when it pays for valuable services and investment, but it can also transfer costs to future budgets. Recent IMF and OECD studies show rising debt and refinancing pressures. There is no single safe debt percentage for every country. A useful judgment combines the debt path with funding conditions, institutions and the quality of public spending.

1. Background: why governments borrow

Governments collect taxes, fees and other income. They use these funds for public services, infrastructure, security, transfers to households and interest on earlier loans. The timing of spending rarely matches the timing of receipts. During recessions, tax revenue often falls just when more people need support. During wars, disasters or major infrastructure projects, spending can rise sharply. Borrowing lets governments spread payments over time.

Government debt is centuries old. States first used loans heavily to fund wars. As financial systems developed, governments issued bonds more regularly and created debt-management offices. A government bond is a promise to make specified payments. A bond may pay interest each year and repay its principal—the amount borrowed—at maturity. Some bonds adjust for inflation. Others are sold at a discount and make a single payment later.

Debt rose during major twentieth-century wars and crises. After the Second World War, some countries reduced their debt ratios through economic growth, inflation, budget policy and financial rules. Those routes had different costs for savers and taxpayers. Borrowing also expanded after the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 shock. History shows that debt responds to policy choices and events outside a government's control.

The issue is current. The IMF's April 2026 Fiscal Monitor estimated that global public debt was just under 94% of world GDP in 2025 and projected it to reach 100% by 2029. The IMF's 14 October 2026 briefing warned about higher interest rates and persistent spending pressures. These are forecasts and policy assessments, not fixed outcomes. A global figure also hides wide differences among countries.

2. Essential terms

Debt stock versus annual deficit

Debt stock is the amount owed at a particular date. A deficit is the excess of spending over revenue during a period. A smaller deficit still adds to debt, unless other transactions offset it. A reduction in the deficit is not the same as paying down debt.

Primary balance

The primary balance equals revenue minus spending other than interest. A positive balance is a primary surplus; a negative one is a primary deficit. Economists exclude interest to separate the cost of earlier borrowing from current spending and tax choices.

Nominal GDP and effective interest

Gross domestic product (GDP) is the value of goods and services produced within an economy. Nominal GDP uses current prices, so it changes with production and prices. Debt-to-GDP ratios use nominal debt and nominal GDP. The effective interest rate is the average interest cost on existing debt. It differs from the yield offered on a new bond today.

Gross and net borrowing

Gross borrowing includes loans used to repay maturing loans and loans used for new financing needs. Net borrowing measures the increase after repayments, subject to accounting rules. This difference matters when headlines describe record government bond issuance.

3. The debt arithmetic, step by step

Debt-to-GDP is a fraction. Debt is the numerator; the economy's annual output is the denominator. Interest adds to the financing need. A primary deficit adds more, while a primary surplus offsets some of the burden. Nominal GDP growth makes a given debt stock smaller relative to the economy.

The simplified identity is:

New debt ratio = old debt ratio × (1 + i) ÷ (1 + g) − primary balance + other adjustments

Here i is the effective nominal interest rate and g is nominal GDP growth. The primary balance is measured as a share of current GDP and is positive for a surplus. Other adjustments, often called stock-flow adjustments, include changes in debt that do not pass through the primary deficit. The IMF's debt-sustainability training material derives this formula.

If the effective interest rate is above nominal growth, inherited debt tends to rise relative to GDP unless a sufficient primary surplus offsets it. If growth exceeds the interest rate, the inherited debt ratio tends to fall. This can leave room for a primary deficit without an increase in the ratio. The statement holds only under the stated assumptions.

The debt-stabilizing primary balance is the primary balance that would hold the debt ratio constant for given interest, growth and initial debt. With no other adjustments, it equals the initial debt ratio times (i − g), divided by (1 + g). This is a benchmark, not a recommendation. Governments may need stronger balances to prepare for shocks or may borrow temporarily during recessions.

Inflation needs special care. Higher prices can increase nominal GDP and reduce the real value of existing fixed-rate domestic-currency debt. But inflation also harms purchasing power. Investors may demand higher yields, and inflation-linked or foreign-currency debt may become more expensive. A country cannot assume that faster inflation is a harmless way to reduce debt.

4. Two worked examples

Example 1: a deficit with nearly stable debt

Consider an illustrative government with initial debt of 80% of GDP, an effective nominal interest rate of 6%, nominal GDP growth of 8%, and a primary deficit of 2% of current GDP. Assume no other adjustments.

Illustrative inputs and result
MeasureValue
Initial debt ratio80%
Effective nominal interest6%
Nominal GDP growth8%
Primary balance−2% of GDP
New debt ratio80.52%

Calculate 80 × 1.06 ÷ 1.08 + 2 = 80.52%, rounded. Debt rises by about 0.52 percentage points of GDP. Growth partly offsets the interest burden and new borrowing. The stabilizing primary balance would be 80 × (0.06 − 0.08) ÷ 1.08 = −1.48% of GDP. A primary deficit of about 1.48% would hold the ratio flat in this simplified case. The actual deficit is larger, so the ratio rises slightly.

Example 2: a surplus that fails to stabilize debt

Consider a second illustrative government with the same initial debt ratio of 80%. Its effective nominal interest rate is 9%, nominal GDP grows by 5%, and it runs a primary surplus of 1% of GDP. Assume no other adjustments.

Calculate 80 × 1.09 ÷ 1.05 − 1 = 82.05%, rounded. The ratio rises by about 2.05 percentage points despite the primary surplus. The stabilizing primary surplus would be 80 × (0.09 − 0.05) ÷ 1.05 = 3.05% of GDP, rounded. That is a much larger budget adjustment. Achieving it suddenly might damage services or demand. Debt arithmetic alone cannot decide how fast adjustment should occur.

Key point: A primary deficit does not always cause a rising debt ratio, and a primary surplus does not always prevent one. The interest-growth difference and inherited debt stock matter.

5. What the evidence tells us

Global debt is high, but countries differ

The IMF's April 2026 report put global public debt just below 94% of GDP in 2025 and projected 100% by 2029. The IMF's October 2026 briefing urged earlier action where fiscal risks are high. These statements do not imply that every country is near default. Global figures combine economies with different currencies, financing terms and institutions. [1] [2]

The OECD's Global Debt Report 2026, published 4 March 2026, found that central governments in OECD countries borrowed about US$17 trillion from markets in 2025, with about US$18 trillion projected for 2026. Outstanding OECD sovereign bond debt reached about US$61 trillion in 2025. These figures describe marketable debt within the OECD report's coverage. They are not the same measure as the IMF's wider public-debt series. [4]

Refinancing dominates many borrowing headlines

The OECD found that around US$13.5 trillion of OECD sovereign borrowing needs in 2025 related to refinancing existing debt—close to 80% of gross borrowing. Record bond issuance can therefore reflect replacement of old debt rather than an equally large net increase. Refinancing still carries risk: the government must attract lenders when bonds mature. [4]

Interest costs change with a delay

Governments have bonds issued at different times and rates. A rise in current market yields affects new borrowing first. The effective rate on all outstanding fixed-rate debt often rises gradually as older bonds mature. The OECD notes that some issuers shifted toward shorter maturities to reduce immediate costs. That can increase exposure to future rates because more debt must be refinanced sooner. [4]

External debt creates special pressure

The World Bank's International Debt Report 2025, released 3 December 2025, found that low- and middle-income countries paid US$741 billion more in principal and interest on external debt over 2022–2024 than they received in new external financing. This is a cumulative net-flow figure, not a one-year interest bill. The report also put these economies' external debt stock at US$8.9 trillion at end-2024. High debt-service costs can compete with spending on health, education and infrastructure. [5]

External debt is only one part of the picture. Domestic borrowing also matters. A country may have limited foreign debt but substantial domestic obligations. Comparing two countries without checking debt definitions, currency and maturity can mislead.

Severe outcomes can exceed the central forecast

A 2025 IMF working paper by Furceri and co-authors studies debt-at-risk, a high-debt outcome in the upper tail of a forecast distribution. The authors find that adverse financial conditions can produce debt far above the central projection, especially when initial debt is high. Their severe global scenario is around 20 percentage points of GDP above baseline. This is a modeled risk, not a prediction that the event will happen. [6]

These studies agree on a central point: the debt ratio matters, but its future path and financing conditions matter too. No single headline can measure the full risk.

6. Risks the simple formula misses

Liquidity and solvency

Liquidity risk means a government may struggle to make a payment on time even when its long-run resources could cover the debt. Many bonds may mature together, or investors may stop buying new issues. Solvency risk means expected future resources cannot support the promised payments on their terms. The two problems can reinforce each other. A funding shock may raise yields, harm banks and weaken growth, making a previously manageable debt stock harder to support.

Foreign-currency borrowing

A government that borrows in its own currency faces different constraints from one that owes large amounts in foreign currency. When the local currency falls, foreign debt becomes larger in local-currency terms. Countries issuing widely accepted currencies may have more financing options, but they remain exposed to inflation and market confidence. Central-bank bond purchases do not create real goods or services; financing has economic consequences.

Hidden obligations

A contingent liability is a possible future payment triggered by an event. Government guarantees, public-enterprise losses, bank rescues and deposit insurance can create such obligations. They may remain outside a narrow debt headline until a crisis. Debt can then jump even without an equivalent current primary deficit. Analysts must read fiscal-risk statements as well as budget tables.

Bond maturities and investors

Long-term fixed-rate local-currency bonds can reduce near-term funding risk, though they may cost more to issue. Short-term debt can be cheaper today but needs frequent renewal. Reliance on a small group of lenders or leveraged investors can make borrowing conditions less stable. The OECD's 2026 report highlights changes in the investor base as a source of market sensitivity. [4]

Uncertain growth

Debt forecasts rely on growth assumptions over many years. A recession reduces tax receipts and may increase support spending. Borrowing rates can rise just when growth slows. A sudden spending cut may also weaken demand, reducing GDP and making the ratio harder to improve. Sound plans test lower growth, higher rates and unexpected costs instead of relying on one optimistic forecast.

7. Policy choices and trade-offs

Improve revenue

A government can raise revenue through rates, wider tax bases, better compliance and fewer poorly designed exemptions. The effects depend on incentives and administration. The IMF's October 2026 Fiscal Monitor special edition examines how tax design may support growth without sacrificing revenue. Its argument concerns the structure and collection of taxes; it does not establish that every tax cut pays for itself. [7]

Improve spending quality

Spending cuts can lower borrowing but can also remove valuable services. Delayed road maintenance may create larger future costs. Poorly targeted subsidies may deliver little benefit at high expense. The IMF's October 2025 Fiscal Monitor studied how more effective spending on infrastructure, education, health and research can improve long-run output without increasing total spending. Outcomes depend on the quality of institutions and implementation. [8]

Support productive growth

Growth raises incomes and can strengthen tax receipts, but a government cannot simply choose its growth rate. Better infrastructure, competition, education and reliable rules may help over time. Some investment requires borrowing before benefits appear. Policy must judge the expected public return, timing, maintenance costs and risk of failure.

Manage maturities and prepare for shocks

Debt managers can spread repayment dates, build cash buffers, broaden the investor base and disclose clear borrowing plans. A government facing unsustainable obligations may need restructuring, which changes repayment terms, interest or principal. Restructuring can restore a workable debt path but may impose losses on lenders and reduce market access. It is a serious remedy with costs.

Judge the complete plan

Start with the debt measure and years covered. Check primary balances, effective rates and nominal growth assumptions. Inspect currency exposure, maturities, guarantees and financing needs. Ask whether planned tax and spending changes are realistic. Then test what happens after a negative shock. A stable debt ratio based on unlikely assumptions provides weak evidence of safety.

8. Competing views: how much debt is too much?

Case for stronger limits

Advocates of fiscal restraint argue that high debt leaves less room for emergencies. Interest payments can displace essential programs. Persistent borrowing may raise financing costs or reduce funds available for private investment. If confidence falls, the government may face a sudden, painful adjustment. The IMF's recent assessments emphasize these risks, especially where financing is fragile and debt is high. [1] [2]

Case for a more flexible approach

Other economists warn that rigid debt ceilings can prevent useful investment or force cuts during recessions. Olivier Blanchard's 2019 research showed that when safe interest rates stay below growth rates, the fiscal cost of rolling over debt may be lower than simple comparisons with household budgets suggest. He also examined possible welfare costs and the danger that changing investor beliefs raise borrowing costs. His conclusion was conditional, not a claim that unlimited debt is safe. [9]

What remains uncertain

Both positions accept the arithmetic. They differ about future interest and growth, the value of spending, financial risk and the social cost of adjustment. Fiscal restraint may be prudent where market access is weak. Borrowing may be justified when it prevents a deep recession or funds high-value investment under credible institutions. There is no universal numerical threshold beyond which every government becomes insolvent. Debt composition, public trust and the available policy choices matter.

9. Why it matters

Public debt affects households through taxes, services, inflation risk and financial stability. Government bonds are also major assets for banks, insurers, pension funds and savers. A sudden fall in bond prices can damage these institutions even before a government misses a payment. A stable bond market can support saving, lending and investment.

Debt policy also affects future generations. A productive project may leave better transport, water systems or higher incomes. Borrowing for weak programs can leave costs with few lasting benefits. The distinction requires evidence about results, maintenance and who benefits. It cannot be read from the deficit alone.

When assessing a budget, separate three questions: How much does the government owe? How hard will it be to finance that debt? What does the borrowing achieve? These questions provide a stronger basis for judgment than a single debt percentage.

10. Key lessons

  1. A deficit is a yearly flow; debt is a stock at a point in time.
  2. The primary balance excludes interest and shows current budget choices more clearly.
  3. Interest, nominal growth, primary balances and other adjustments jointly determine the debt ratio.
  4. A primary deficit can coexist with a stable ratio when growth sufficiently exceeds interest.
  5. A primary surplus may fail to stabilize debt when interest greatly exceeds growth.
  6. Gross bond issuance often includes large amounts of refinancing.
  7. Currency, maturity, investors and hidden guarantees change the risks.
  8. Debt forecasts need tests for weaker growth and higher borrowing costs.
  9. The value of what borrowing finances matters for public welfare.
  10. No single debt-to-GDP threshold determines safety for every country.

11. Knowledge check

1. Why can the amount of debt rise while debt-to-GDP falls?

Nominal GDP can grow faster than the debt stock. The denominator rises more quickly than the numerator, reducing the ratio.

2. How does a primary deficit differ from the overall deficit?

The primary deficit excludes interest payments. The overall deficit includes them, along with other spending and revenue.

3. Why do higher market yields not instantly raise interest on every bond?

Many bonds have fixed coupons set when issued. New yields affect new debt and refinancing first. The average cost changes as the portfolio turns over.

4. Why might a country with a lower debt ratio face greater risk?

It may rely on short-term or foreign-currency borrowing, have weak revenue or face large hidden guarantees. Market access can also be fragile.

5. What should you check before trusting a debt-stabilization plan?

Check primary balances, nominal growth, effective interest, maturity and currency exposure, stock-flow adjustments, political feasibility and adverse scenarios.
